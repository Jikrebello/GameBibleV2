# Atherian Astrology

Atherian astrology interpreted the ruling constellation at a person's birth as an influence upon temperament, vocation and recurrent danger. Households used natal signs when naming children, arranging apprenticeships, negotiating marriages and choosing dates for travel. Learned astrologers produced far more elaborate charts from planetary positions, seasonal winds and the relation between several Star Children.

The system records Atherian doctrine rather than a reliable anatomy of fate. Thanatos was the imperial name given to [[The Boatman]], who exercises no astrological dominion. The other signs gather cultural interpretations around real Menhir powers without proving that those powers assigned individual destinies.

## The Seven Natal Signs

### Arachnara, the Weaver

Arachnara's sign is the spider and its associated element is shadow. People born under it were said to possess patience, secrecy and an instinct for joining small facts into a larger design. Praise poetry made them careful planners; satire presented them as people who could turn breakfast into a conspiracy.

Families often directed Weaver-born children towards archives, research, estate management, intelligence work and crafts requiring exact preparation. Astrologers warned that guardedness could harden into manipulation or isolation.

### Astaroth, the Warrior

Astaroth's sign is the broken sword. Its children were described as courageous, passionate and quick to enter conflict. Military households valued the sign, particularly when a birth coincided with the red star's seasonal prominence.

Common vocations included soldiering, command, public advocacy and competitive civic office. Traditional warnings concerned needless quarrels, delight in fear and the habit of treating every disagreement as a test of strength.

### Hecatara, the Key-Bearer

The torch and key mark Hecatara's sign. Its children were associated with curiosity, difficult decisions and knowledge found beyond ordinary routes. They frequently appear in biographies of magicians, physicians, counsellors and travellers.

A favourable reading promised ingenuity at moments of crisis. An adverse reading warned of secrecy pursued for its own sake, fascination with forbidden practice and the willingness to expose others to dangers the astrologer believed they could master.

### Lyraxis, the Muse

Lyraxis is represented by the harp or laughing mask. Muse-born people were expected to show wit, charm, invention and resistance to routine. Performers invoked the sign openly, and artisan households sometimes sought it for children expected to continue a workshop through new designs.

Its dangers were instability, cruelty disguised as humour and a taste for disruption after the useful purpose of a trick had ended. A common comic character used a Lyraxis birth chart to excuse debts and infidelity.

### Orionis, the Hunter

The bow and stag belong to Orionis. Hunter-born people were described as self-reliant, observant and driven by difficult pursuits. Hunters, scouts, navigators, surveyors and frontier officers claimed a natural affinity with the sign.

Astrologers praised endurance and loyalty to a chosen purpose. They warned that pursuit could become domination and that independence could leave a person unable to receive help or release a defeated rival.

### Thanatos, the Boatman

Thanatos was marked by the hourglass, dark oar or river boat. People born under the sign were thought calm in the presence of death and unusually attentive to timing, boundaries and transition. Undertakers, guides, mediators, thieves and legal officers all appear among its traditional vocations.

The sign's compassionate interpretation emphasised the ability to accompany another person through grief. Its darker reading described coldness, secrecy and fascination with crossing boundaries that should remain closed. These traits belong to Atherian astrology; the actual Boatman neither distributes them nor governs thieves.

### Typhonius, the Beast

Typhonius is represented by the dragon or composite beast. The sign promised force, adaptability and the ability to continue after catastrophe. Breeders, engineers, soldiers and people responsible for disaster works sometimes adopted it as a professional emblem.

Traditional warnings concerned uncontrolled experiment, hunger for spectacle and the belief that survival proves wisdom. Families often treated a difficult birth beneath the sign as an omen of either exceptional achievement or bodily danger.

## Charts and Practice

The ruling sign supplied only the beginning of a formal chart. Astrologers also recorded the phase of the moon, visible planets, the position of important fixed stars and the hour at which the child first cried. Contradictory influences gave trained readers room for interpretation and allowed families to seek a second opinion.

Urban clients received written charts. Rural households often kept the same information through a birth song, a marked roof beam or an object placed near the infant. Soldiers consulted campaign astrologers for departure dates, merchants timed contracts to favourable conjunctions and couples sometimes delayed a wedding until a hostile sign had passed.

## Social Influence and Criticism

Astrology shaped expectation as much as decision. A child repeatedly praised for Astarothian courage might be pushed towards military service; one called an Arachnaran schemer could become secretive under suspicion. Philosophers and comic writers recognised this effect and argued that charts often described the education imposed after birth.

Priests disagreed about the strength of celestial necessity. Strict schools taught that every life followed a legible course. Moderate readers treated the stars as dispositions and warnings. Sceptics used failed military predictions, incompatible twin charts and the prosperity of people born under supposedly ruinous signs to attack the profession.

Astrological practice survived the conversion of the empire in calendars, weather lore, medical timing and popular character types. Both successor churches condemn claims that the stars command a human will, though consultation remains common under names such as seasonal reckoning or household wisdom.
