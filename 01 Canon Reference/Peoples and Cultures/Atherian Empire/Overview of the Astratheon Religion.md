# Astratheon Religion

Astratheon was the principal public religion of Atherian civilisation before the imperial conversion to the Redeemer's faith. It placed seven Star Children within a shared celestial family and interpreted constellations, seasons and unusual heavenly events as signs of their favour or hostility. The religion united older maritime cults, inland rites and Giant-era astronomy while leaving considerable room for household worship and local variation.

Astratheon described several genuine powers through an inaccurate divine genealogy. Most of its Star Children corresponded to Menhir or powers encountered through their courts. Thanatos was an Atherian interpretation of [[The Boatman]], whose actual commission never permitted bribes, bargains over souls or membership in a celestial family. Political theology and received myth therefore reveal Atherian belief without settling the identities of the beings concerned.

## Historical Formation

The religion developed over many centuries. Maritime settlers brought navigation rites, heroic songs and memories of deliverance into the Aurin basin. Enathi, Serathi and Vardeni communities contributed river ceremonies, ancestor cults, crossroads shrines, hunting customs and knowledge preserved after the fall of Arsuvan. Royal astronomers had inherited useful observations from the Giant kingdom, although human teachers rejected its claims of dynastic supremacy.

Around 1000–950 BR, King Ilaron and Queen Tarsenna established the [[The Common Sky and the Formation of Astratheon|Common Sky]] at Caleran. Their settlement gave the principal cults a shared calendar, public festival ground and system of royal patronage. The Celestial Compact later adopted this calendar, and republican families used priesthoods and festivals to display wealth, ancestry and civic service.

Imperial rule strengthened the common pantheon. Thalion's government raised Astaroth as protector of imperial destiny and presented conquest as the extension of celestial order. Provincial communities continued to adapt the cults to local histories. Astratheon consequently possessed common names and ceremonies without ever becoming wholly uniform.

## The Star Children

| Star Child | Principal concerns in Atherian worship | Common symbols |
|---|---|---|
| **Astaroth** | war, fear, military fortune and the authority of victory | broken sword, red star, raven |
| **Lyraxis** | music, wit, invention, performance and dangerous inspiration | harp, blue star, laughing mask |
| **Thanatos** | death, travel, theft and passage across boundaries | boat, hourglass, dark oar |
| **Orionis** | hunting, wilderness, pursuit and mastery | bow, stag, hunting hound |
| **Hecatara** | sorcery, crossroads, hidden approaches and decisive choices | torch, key, three-way road |
| **Typhonius** | monsters, catastrophe, breeding and uncontrolled change | dragon, storm-wound, mixed beast |
| **Arachnara** | vermin, shadow, infestation, concealment and patient design | spider, web, covered lamp |

The Star Children were feared as powerful and capricious. Worshippers sought usable relations with them through offerings, vows and attention to signs. Priests also told stories of rivalry and kinship that made political conflict intelligible: generals resembled Astaroth, artists invoked Lyraxis and a dangerous journey could be understood as a dispute between Hecatara and Thanatos.

## Fate and the Heavens

Atherian astrologers taught that the sky at birth inclined a person's character and revealed likely dangers. Natal signs affected naming, education, marriage negotiations and the selection of auspicious dates. Practice differed by class. A wealthy household might commission a complete chart, while a dock family remembered the ruling constellation and one warning attached to it.

Fatalism remained influential without being absolute. Philosophers argued over whether a sign imposed destiny, disclosed probability or merely revealed the temperament with which a person would meet events. Generals frequently ignored adverse readings after commissioning a second priest, and comic drama made much of people who blamed the stars for choices everyone else recognised as their own.

The separate [[Atherian Astrology System]] describes the seven principal natal signs.

## Death and the Thanatos Cult

Astratheon taught that Thanatos carried the dead towards a destination fitted to their lives and divine allegiances. Funerary rites supplied food, coins, miniature vessels and written petitions for the journey. Wealthy families purchased elaborate assurances from priests; poorer households relied upon songs, a simple crossing gift and the care taken over burial.

The cult described three broad destinations. The Celestial Realm received people favoured by a Star Child. The Abyss of Stars held the condemned and forgotten. The Shadowed Crossing received those whose fate remained contested. Myths claimed that Thanatos could delay passage, lend back a dead soul or improve its destination in exchange for payment.

These promises rested upon a false understanding of the Boatman. He carries human souls under a heavenly commission and cannot sell their judgement. Atherian funerals nevertheless possessed genuine social value: they gathered kin, honoured the dead and ensured burial. Fraud arose when priests sold authority they did not have.

## Worship and Sacrifice

Public worship followed the civic calendar. Processions, races, dramatic contests, military dedications and night watches joined the life of temples to markets and government. Birth, adulthood, marriage, military service and death could each be marked by a rite under the appropriate constellation. Pilgrims travelled among prominent shrines and observatories, often combining devotion with trade or education.

Offerings included animals, wine, grain, crafted objects, weapons, songs and treasure. Human sacrifice occurred at particular royal and military ceremonies, especially after conquest or during feared celestial alignments. Practice varied across periods and cities and was frequently contested. The Common Sky settlement itself included the killing of captives supplied by Ilaron after a river campaign.

## Temples and Priesthoods

Astratheon temples favoured open courts, elevated ground and clear sightlines to the night sky. Their architecture reflected the local cult: Hecataran shrines stood beside branching roads, Astaroth received weapons on old battlefields and Lyraxis temples supported theatres and schools of music. Urban sites also owned workshops, land and housing, making them important employers and creditors.

Each cult maintained its own priests, property and rites. The Astral Keepers studied calendars and large celestial events across cult boundaries. Their observations supported navigation, agriculture and state ceremony as well as divination. Republican and imperial families competed for religious office because priesthood conferred public visibility and access to temple wealth.

## Conversion and Survival

Redeemer communities condemned sacrifice, celestial fatalism and the worship of created powers. Astratheon remained publicly dominant through the Underground Centuries, then shared imperial patronage after legalization in 292 AR. Vestian's compulsory settlement of 380 closed or converted many temples, dissolved augur colleges and exposed priests and dependants to confiscation, violence and forced conformity.

The religion survived in household customs, rural shrines, philosophical schools and civic festivals stripped of their older dedications. Eclessian, Thalmyric and Sestaran societies inherited its calendars, architecture, dramatic traditions and celestial vocabulary. A constellation may now provide the date of a feast without receiving worship, while old temple ruins continue to attract pilgrims, antiquarians and cult revivalists.
