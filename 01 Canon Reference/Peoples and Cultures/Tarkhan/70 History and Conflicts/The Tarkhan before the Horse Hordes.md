# The Tarkhan before the Horse Hordes

The **ancient Tarkhan** developed among human communities which survived the fall of [[Vathra and the Great Unyoking|Vathra]] outside the Glass refuge. Their ancestors included camel keepers, tether-town craftsmen, eastern herders, hunters, captive labourers, soldiers, fugitives and families which had resisted Giant rule. Shared speech, fosterage, assembly customs and repeated movement between western wells and eastern grass gradually made them recognisable as one people.

The broad name **Tarkhan** became common during the Hearth-Crown centuries. Earlier communities usually identified themselves by a well, seasonal route, craft household or descent group. Southern records first applied the name to related northern populations. Northern singers later adopted it while preserving several incompatible explanations for its origin.

This ancient civilisation relied on camels, carts, sledges and walking herds. Horses remained scarce and concentrated in a few chariot households. The mounted Grass-Tarkhan world began after the [[First Catch and Grey Sister|First Catch]] of 905–920 AR.

## The post-Vathran peoples

Four regional populations contributed strongly to Tarkhan formation.

The **Tergai well houses** maintained deep water in the central dry belt. Their claims rested upon digging, draw gear, burial and seasonal use. Different houses controlled approaches, gardens and storage rooms around the same water.

The **Elkari grass families** followed cattle, sheep and wild herds through the eastern seasonal lake country. They moved between cold-weather shelter and summer grass, using carts on firm ground and sledges after snow. Hunting sustained them during herd disease and drought.

The **Taleri tether towns** grew around grounded Vathran works. Their inhabitants preserved masonry, metal repair, wind-cloth weaving and knowledge of old machines. Some descended from privileged sky households. Others came from captives freed during the Great Unyoking.

The **Narashi caravan houses** held western wells and camel routes. Their maternal crowns predated Vathra's fall. Narashi families entered both Tarkhan and Ahdari communities over later generations, and the old network crossed the boundary between the two peoples.

These populations intermarried, exchanged foster children and admitted outsiders. They also fought over water, enslaved one another and told hostile stories about conduct during the revolt. Their common life developed through these relationships.

## Rebuilding on the ground

Post-Vathran communities faced ruined stores and displaced populations. Released animals scattered. Wells built for royal landing fields could support only part of the people seeking refuge. Families had to choose between useful ruins and routes with less food but fewer armed claimants.

At Kel-Orun, survivors turned lower reservoirs into public cisterns. Former royal servants claimed the apartments around them and attempted to preserve their privileges. Freed labourers expelled one household after discovering hidden grain. They retained the family's water measurements because the records remained useful.

Cattle and sheep recovered across many eastern camps. Horses had been concentrated at royal estates. People ate some captured horses during famine and traded others to southern rulers for seed. The exchange kept children alive while ending several prestigious bloodlines.

Violence continued after the sky cities fell. Armed bands took animals from weakened settlements. Former tether officers restored tribute under human rulers. One Giant cadet held an upland pasture for forty years by taking a child from each dependent household. A herding coalition killed him bodily, then divided over the property of hostage families which had profited from his rule.

## Household and belonging

An ancient Tarkhan household was a working and protective association. It might include relatives by birth, spouses, foster children, adopted strangers, apprentices, freed dependants, clients and slaves. Members shared animals, shelter and obligations according to rank. Experienced animal keepers could remove part of a herd if a leader endangered the camp.

Fosterage joined distant communities. A child lived for several years with another household, learned its route and acquired relatives obliged to protect them. Giant courts had used fosterage as hostage-taking. Human communities gave it reciprocal forms, although powerful houses continued to abuse it by refusing to return a child or demanding excessive service.

Adoption admitted an outsider to burial and revenge obligations. Manumission ended ownership, but a freed person still needed animals, craft or a community willing to provide protection. Some freed families formed new camps. Others remained clients of their former owners.

Marriage connected routes and property. Women of important camel and water houses often controlled breeding lines and well shares. Eastern households valued spouses who connected winter shelter to distant pasture. Captive marriage and concubinage also occurred; later kinship never made the originating coercion voluntary.

## Camels, carts and craft

Camel husbandry became the principal western inheritance of Vathra's ground economy. A good camel carried water and stores between distant stages, produced milk and survived country which exhausted horses. Breeders remembered maternal lines, temperament and endurance through marked cords and songs.

Eastern carts moved households, felt, food and tools. Wheelwrights travelled between assemblies. Sledges crossed winter ground and carried the products of communal hunts. Foot runners delivered messages more reliably than the few remaining royal horses.

Animal healers treated split pads, harness wounds, difficult births and herd fever. Some remedies came from Vathran animal houses; others belonged to older hunters and well communities. Healers could travel through feuds when both sides needed their skill.

Taleri families preserved fragments of sky craft. Metal thread strengthened ceremonial cloth. Pieces of pressure mantle covered graves or shaded public fires. A few pieces twitched or lifted near a surviving anchor. No family could rebuild the aerial system.

## Assemblies and leadership

The principal seasonal assembly met around an **open fire** supplied by several households. Each participating camp contributed fuel or dried dung, making the gathering's duration visible in the common pile. Elders arranged pasture, ransom and compensation. War leaders sought followers for particular expeditions. Singers recited genealogies and exposed people who had broken guest protection.

An assembly's decision bound the houses which accepted it and allies willing to enforce it. A leader gathered wider support by feeding followers and distributing spoil. The coalition shrank when gifts stopped, pasture failed or demands exceeded the original purpose.

Women exercised public authority as well keepers, herd matrons, healers, senior foster relatives and patrons of singers. A Narashi crown-mother could close sanctuary to her armed kin. An Elkari matron could remove breeding animals from a campaign and end it. Property, expertise and alliances determined the strength of this authority.

Gatherings also supported camel and cart races, wrestling, coloured-cloth markets and comic songs about Vathran kings. Children played the unyoking game inherited from the revolt. Rock-cut laments placed the names of dead relatives beside roads used by later generations.

## The chariot houses

The Bronze Roads sustained a few chariot dynasties between approximately 1750 and 1325 BR. Their horses grazed in sheltered valleys and at stations supplied by Qahuri or river-city grain. Wheelwrights, metalworkers and enslaved grooms maintained the vehicles. Chariots were effective on firm ground and failed among stones, dunes and winter mud.

**Boran Tall-Hand** built the best remembered chariot house around Daram Ford. His father had married into a Taleri craft family, giving him reinforced wheels. His mother held pasture through Elkari foster kin. Boran protected convoys and defeated rival lords. He also seized children from households which refused animals for his teams.

During the Breaking of the Bronze Roads, Boran contracted to carry copper west. Mud trapped the convoy after poor floods raised the price of food. Porters asked him to abandon the metal. Boran ordered grain dumped so that the wagons could preserve the copper and horses. The porters killed him and distributed the grain among nearby settlements.

Rival captains divided the horses, and most of the lines disappeared within a generation. Later songs named the event the **Feast of the Copper Wheels**. Some praised the porters who saved the seed. Others lamented a king murdered by servants.

## A common people

The collapse of long-distance exchange drew communities towards shorter circuits. Tergai families met Elkari herders at western winter markets. Taleri smiths repaired camel gear for Narashi caravans. Marriage and fosterage carried words, melodies and burial customs across the region.

Southern treaties and tribute accounts applied *Tarkhan* to these related populations. The word spread because it allowed people from different routes to negotiate collectively with Qahuri and Arqeshite rulers. A person could call herself Tarkhan among foreign merchants and name only her well and foster house at home.

The Great Unyoking became their most widely shared history. It told how people with different work cut the apparatus which carried rulers above them. The Copper Wheels warned that a king's horses depended on porters and grain. Comic verses accused patrons of claiming descent from animals they could not feed.

## Raid, feud and captivity

Raids took animals, food and people. Leaders divided shares among sworn participants. Success could preserve a hungry camp; it could also starve the children of the household whose herd was removed.

Captives were ransomed, exchanged, adopted, forced into marriage or concubinage, kept as slaves and sold to settled markets. Sexual violence occurred during attacks and captivity. Feud exposed the raider's relatives to retaliation, including people who had opposed the expedition. Compensation ended some conflicts when affected families accepted it.

One remembered covenant joined twenty-one reins at a route stone. Seven riders withdrew when their leader changed the target from an enemy to an undefended ally. Praise poetry removed their names and celebrated the animals taken. A captive woman's lament preserved the betrayal and the sale of children afterward.

## Religion and burial

Early Tarkhan communities sacrificed sheep, goats, cattle and camels for rain, safe travel, healthy births and protection. High places retained Vathran stones and broken anchors. Some rites addressed local court persons or Menhir correspondences. Others gathered around memorials or empty cult objects.

Idol Masks exploited certain traditions. Wrath fed through feud standards demanding blood beyond the original offence. Greed fed where ransom and water became occasions for owning people. Pride attached itself to dynasties claiming that their ancestors had ruled the upper sky.

The dead were buried near roads and dependable water. Grave goods included tools, animal gear, food and coloured cloth. Vathran royal funerals had killed servants and animals beside Giant rulers. Some human prestige houses continued the practice. Other communities cut the tether of a living animal at the grave, declaring that no dead chief would take the household's survival with him.

## Foreign clients

Qahuri crowns, Arqeshite governors and lesser river powers recruited northern guides, camel carriers, scouts, archers and guards. They paid in grain, metal, cloth and captives. Foreign service supported rich local courts and drew dependants into distant wars.

The cousins **Savan Uruz** and **Demer Khol** had been raised in the same foster camp. Savan accepted an Arqeshite crown and garrison at an eastern station. Demer controlled western camel roads with Qahuri support. Each claimed to protect the country from the other. Each raided people beyond the districts whose tribute he received.

Savan employed Taleri smiths and Arqeshite deportees. Demer supported poets, camel races and a sanctuary for fugitives from Savan's taxes. He also sold captured eastern families into Qahuri estates.

When Arqeshite payments stopped after 706 BR, Savan seized grain from his soldiers' families. The soldiers killed him. Demer tried to recruit the unpaid companies and was defeated by former captives. The client courts disappeared while their mixed households and craftsmen remained.

## Arqesh's fall

Arqeshite government divided Tarkhan interests. Client leaders gained weapons and food. Hostages acquired education and office in river cities. Other communities lost pasture to imperial stations, suffered deportation or supplied scouts under threat.

**Yalun of the White Camel** entered the coalition against Arqesh to recover hostages held at Durqesh. **Marat Two-Bows** sought the station herds and accepted gifts from Arezan Tiravan. Yalun released captives from several peoples; Marat sold captured grooms after the victory.

Arezan recognised both as local protectors. Their temporary cooperation ended as soon as their objectives diverged. The Tarkhan inherited no part of Arqesh as a united nation, and Vashar acquired influence along selected roads rather than sovereignty over the open country.

## Rethmar, 381 BR

Rethmar developed around winter water, storage and a market where western circuits met. Permanent gardeners, smiths and water keepers lived beside seasonal compounds used by visiting households.

When Lethren Ostar advanced in 381 BR, **Kharuz of the Stone Wells** organised camel scouts and foot archers. He drew Atherian columns apart by offering separate negotiations and attacked detachments moving between wells. **Tavisha**, an Ahdari keeper, removed the water Ostar expected to seize.

Ostar and his son died during the retreat. Northern forces captured officers, soldiers, servants and carriers. Fever entered the camp before ransom could be arranged. Tavisha ordered sick captives and water workers released. Kharuz exchanged several healthy officers for weapons and animals. Captives whose families sent no payment were exchanged, admitted to local households or enslaved.

The coalition dispersed after securing Rethmar. Kharuz commanded a wartime following; Tavisha controlled the water upon which it depended. Neither founded a kingdom.

## The Tarkhan in 350 BR

By 350 BR, the Tarkhan occupied a broad world of western wells, central craft towns and eastern grasslands. Their dialects were mutually intelligible along the principal roads. Fosterage, marriage, assemblies and song connected people more effectively than government.

The west relied upon camels, defended water and caravan brokerage. The east relied upon cattle, sheep, carts, hunting and seasonal grass. Taleri towns preserved craft concentrations around Vathran ruins. Narashi houses crossed into Ahdari society.

Foreign observers knew several strong houses and temporary coalitions. None possessed the remount depth, mounted archery or continent-wide messenger system of the later Horse Centuries. The ancient civilisation was fully developed before horses transformed it.
