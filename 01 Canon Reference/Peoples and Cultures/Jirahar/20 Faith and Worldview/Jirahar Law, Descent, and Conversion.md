# Jirahar Law, Descent, and Conversion

Jirahar communal law defines how a person enters the covenant by birth or conversion and how that membership affects marriage, inheritance, worship and public responsibility. Maternal descent provides the ordinary rule, while adoption, disputed parentage and sincere conversion are governed by developed legal traditions. These laws have helped dispersed communities preserve a common life, although old families have repeatedly used lineage and records to resist the equality formally granted to converts.

## Birth

A child born to a Jirahar mother belongs to the covenant and must be entered in a maternal register. A child of a Jirahar father and an outsider mother requires formal reception, normally with the mother's consent and instruction. Abandonment, rape, missing records, adoption, and disputed maternity have extensive case law rather than one automatic answer.

## Conversion

An outsider may become fully Jirahar through supervised study, public renunciation of incompatible worship, acceptance of the Orakha, immersion, entry into a household and court register, and a probation long enough to test whether the choice survives inconvenience. Courts are instructed to discourage shallow conversion before accepting sincere persistence.

A convert and the convert's children possess full covenant standing. Old houses that treat them as lesser violate the formal law while often escaping meaningful sanction.

## Marriage

Marriage joins households, property, inheritance, and communal responsibility. Marriage to an outsider requires the outsider's conversion before the union is recognized. Host-state civil marriage may create foreign obligations without creating a Jirahar marriage.

## Communal Separation

Jirahar may hold foreign citizenship, serve lawful employers, speak local languages, and maintain friendships outside the covenant. They do not thereby surrender separate worship, marriage, burial, food, education, charity, or internal adjudication. Integration that demands abandonment of those institutions is understood as dissolution.

## Discipline and Departure

Courts may impose restitution, loss of communal office, exclusion from rites, or separation from shared funds. A person may leave in fact, but courts preserve birth records and obligations involving spouses, children, debts, and entrusted property. Forced return is illegal except under a specific lawful judgement.
