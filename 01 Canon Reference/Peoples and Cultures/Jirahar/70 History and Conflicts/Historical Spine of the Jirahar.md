# Historical Spine of the Jirahar

The Jirahar are a dispersed Ark-derived people whose history connects the One Glass People, the Near-Glass fission, the tower cities of Qiryath and repeated eras of exile. Portable courts, sacred learning, trade and communal descent allowed Jirahar communities to endure the destructions of 742 BR and 72 AR and the Vestian persecutions of 377–398. Five great towers and numerous smaller communities remain active across the world in 1360.

## Exodus and Scattering

The Great Prophet's movement became the centre of the Great Unbinding and led the principal liberated remnant into the Glass. Approximately a century of refuge formed one people. The Prophet died at the outward threshold around c. 2165 BR; no human office inherited his universal commission.

After the southern war-settlement leagues departed, the ancestors of the Jirahar remained within the Near-Glass camp continuum. Between approximately c. 1950 and 1750 BR, water, distance, marriage, trade, raiding and reciprocal Tarkhan contact produced the proto-Jirahar emphasis on portable courts, route memory, burial obligation and the Scrolls.

## First Qiryath

Between c. 1400 and 800 BR, seasonal courts became tower towns. Qiryath developed into the principal sanctuary and legal centre after centuries of road-house life, local judges and protector households.

The judges' centuries left stories the towers still tell against their own heroes. Around 1335 BR Menakhel of the Reed Cloak broke a raiding household at three wells and executed the men who had surrendered to him; within two generations his sons' toll house was the armed House Sereq. Road tradition says that around 1290 a herd boy named Tovan bar Rameq, hidden in an old cistern from Sereq's collectors, was answered by name by something in the deep water and offered the shelf's streams. The stream he called down on the Sereq camp came out of season, and it drowned the raiders together with the pledged children penned there. The road judges bound his line to call the streams for the bereaved villages as a debt and gave it no office, because the Prophet's people founded nothing on a bargain. The water went on answering his sister's children, as the shelf villages reckoned a line through mothers, and four of his sons drowned trying to take it from them. At the toll fortress of Gedran, around 1171, the lords killed their weigher under his own false weights. His daughter Nareh carried his honest stones from village to village, and Hador bar Mavin, who had made a private war in her name, was declared unhoused by his own elders and ended as the fortress's gate captain. Around 1128 Othan bar Raqel broke a harvest truce to take Gedran. The old captain killed two of the sureties and carried the third, Othan's daughter Naveth, a child of the cistern line, down the river road, and the towers still argue over what her father knew.

At Qiryath an accepted offering burned without smoke and left the altar stones cool to the hand. Its protectors became kings by the altar: a guardian who carried the appointed offering was acclaimed beside the cool stones at every festival, and the house of Oram held the basket, the grain rooms and one of the three witnessing hands. Around 953 BR the wood-carrier Yedran bar Aqev was named guardian because he caught the basket when the fevered guardian Zeran Oram fell on the sanctuary stair. Zeran lived thirteen years more. After his death his sons and the lower town fought for the basket on the stair, and it reached the altar in the hands of a lamp-filler with broken ribs. His widow, Rava bat Horel, asked the custodians publicly whom the stones had answered. They said the stones had only ever answered the offering, and the bearer was chosen by lot from then on. Around 829 the guardian Seram ben Teth married into an Arqeshite river house, and his levy starved the cistern house off the shelf; the streams ran astray for four autumns. Around 826 his soldiers killed two people at a custodian's door so that an Arqeshite royal image could stand on the stair, and the offering burned clean on a day the custodians wrote with a blank. The water came back when Seram's wife Tilqa, whose mothers descended from Naveth, went down Tovan's cistern. The prophet Haviel of the Lower Stair then had her diviners drowned in the cistern they had drained to wash the image, and the road judges unhoused him for killing without trial. ## Arqeshite Destruction and Exile

In 746 BR King Ilbarun II of Arqesh demanded priestly hostages and a place for his image in the upper sanctuary, and his demand relied on the morning when a river king's image had stood on the stair and the offering had burned clean. Qiryath's custodians kept a roll with a blank in it for that morning, and the council refused. A woman of Tilqa's line came up the road with the besieging army, went down the old cistern at the first rain and sent the shelf's streams past the city's cisterns. In the first winter of the Arqeshite siege, around 743 BR, the council declared the city's foreign residents unhoused to save grain, and they starved in the caravan yard below the sanctuary. At the next appointed offering the smoke rose black and the stones were hot. In 742 the [[First Qiryath and the Arqeshite Exile|Arqeshite River Empire destroyed Qiryath]], deported leading households and scattered sacred vessels. The exiles prayed towards the ruin, their judges ruled on witnesses alone, and at every appointed festival a scribe wrote one more line in the Book of the Unanswered Years: the year, and "no answer". In a Nemeru canal gang the musician Sameth bar Yorin lost two fingers to a sluice winch, worked out a new fingering for the long reed pipe with the hand he had left, and set the Book's empty lines to the tune the deportees knew best.

Arqesh fell in 690 to its own succession war and the separate armies of Arezan Tiravan and Eryndor III. The capital's dredging gangs and deportee quarters, Jirahar households among them, opened its gates when the king cut their rations, and both conquerors set them back to work under guard. Arezan's Decree of Roads and Houses permitted return after Eryndor's death in 684, and the Vasharan crown paid for the first courses of the rebuilding, wagering that the sign would come back under its protection, and left the city to finish it. The Second Qiryath was consecrated around 660 under Vasharan supremacy, and the offering made in the king's name smoked. A severe faction blamed the spouses and children of Arqeshite settlers and demanded their expulsion. The cistern keeper Yaelith bat Coren refused. Around 654, at the next appointed festival, her mixed households carried the offering, and the stones went cool. The Jirahar call those eighty-nine years the Withholding. Which act the silence answered is still argued, but the Jirahar have outlived one withholding, and they remember that it ended.

## Atherian Accommodation

Atheria absorbed the eastern riverlands in stages. Raveth's river towns left Vasharan canal duty for the empire around 160 BR, and Jirahar scribes, interpreters and store examiners followed the roads into imperial service. From 151 BR they could become citizens by swearing with the emperor's image veiled.

Qiryath passed to Atheria under the [[The Last Dynasties of the Age of Glory|late Halreths]]. During Vashar's War of the Two Dawns the sanctuary's Keeper fell on the cistern stair and died. An interim Keeper chosen by the city made the pilgrimage offering and the stones went cool; a month later a river-quarter house that had bought confirmation of the office from Daryam came up the Dawn Road with an escort and took the altar. The tower houses wrote to the Atherian governor at Arcelon, and at the next pilgrimage the governor's augur of the Red Star asked the sign to decide between them. The purchaser refused to make the Lord answer a foreigner's question. The interim Keeper offered before the crowd, and the fire smoked like any fire. Atherian writers called the day the Trial before the Stars; in the towers it was the Governor's Question.

The governor ruled on the sanctuary's written record of the earlier sign. A scribe of the house of Qoreth drafted the charter: sacrifice, courts and burial jurisdiction protected in Qiryath and the province's tower towns; no image in the sanctuary; a Keeper chosen by the council and confirmed by the governor; a daily offering for the emperor; the governor's seat at the pilgrimage and his sole power of death over offences against imperial rule; the sanctuary's dues as surety for the tribute; one copy in the sanctuary and one in the chancery. The defeated houses loaded their scrolls onto mules and went east into Vasharan country, telling the story their own way: the sign had refused to answer a governor. At Qiryath it went on answering the appointed offerings. Vashar took the Dawn Portion of pilgrim dues and called it a lease.

The Age of Glory created varied diaspora relationships with imperial towns and offices. [[The Keeper of Borrowed Grain|Neriah bar Sorek]] directed relief during the Raveth hunger from 119 BR. His relief saved towns. He wrote it as village debts to be worked off in the flood channels; the court made a crown estate of them against his advice, and he resigned rather than administer the oath before the emperor's image that estate officers put in place of the villages' countersigned tallies, leaving the villages their copies. Later praise and anti-Jirahar accusation both laid on him a policy that belonged to Emperor Elian II, his sister's stewards and Atherian officials.

## Isfir and the Silence

Isfir was born Jirahar, in 33 BR, in a tower town whose court sat under the charter's protection. In 0 AR a controlling council faction led by Ezrahel bar Qoreth pressed Atheria for his execution. His house kept the sanctuary copy of the charter, and every step of the referral ran through it: the governor sitting at the pilgrimage with an augur beside him, the clause for cases touching imperial rule, the governor's sole power of death. Atheria killed him; he rose. From that point, lawful sacrifices burned but no authenticated sign of acceptance returned. The Jirahar call it the Silence, and keep the name apart from the Withholding, which ended.

## Great Tower Revolt

In the spring of 61 AR the emperor's daily offering lay unburnt on Qiryath's altar for nine mornings. The governor's augur called it a fraud, and soldiers sent up the sanctuary stair to burn a bull in pitch killed the custodians of the three old houses and two hundred others. The city drove out its garrison, and for eight years no army came against it. Its Keeper, Elqan bar Sered, went on offering for the emperor every morning, and Jirahar jurists still hold that Qiryath never broke its charter. The legions went first to Migdal Shahar, the Dawn Tower, two days east on the Dawn Road: a gatehouse above a city cut into the hill, with a Way-Gate in its deepest chamber. They dug down for two years and dropped the roofs of the outer galleries on the people sheltering in them. In 64 the bench of the partner gate, in a tower across the Vasharan frontier, agreed to take the households and no one who had borne arms. The war captain Eshkar bar Hanan refused terms that left his fighters to die. The gate keeper Yaltah bat Ozer opened the gate on the bench's terms anyway and sent the households through for nine days while the fighters held the stairs, then broke the gate's scroll-keys before the soldiers reached the chamber. The legions killed or sold whoever was left. Gate benches still cite Shahar when they weigh another tower's danger against its people, and after it none would open for Qiryath's. The legions then dug out the province's smaller tower towns one at a time. The legate closed the city in the autumn of 69 and broke its cisterns. Before the breach the Keeper sent the sanctuary's vessels through Qiryath's own gate to a bench that has never been named, and the gate has not opened since. Atheria burned the sanctuary in 72 AR, from a fire the legate's augur lit on the altar, and carried empty litters in its triumph four years later. Qiryath lost its charter and passed under direct provincial rule, Vashar's Dawn Portion ended with the sanctuary, and the treasury laid the price of the vessels, the Litter Debt, on every Jirahar household in the empire. Many displaced Jirahar sought refuge across the Vasharan frontier, where the houses that had refused the charter had settled long before, or among independent dry-margin communities. Older diaspora households helped them establish courts, secure burial grounds and resume their trades. [[Great Tower Revolt and the Second Destruction]]

## Long Diaspora

Surviving towers, quarters, fleets, and companies absorbed captives and refugees. Imperial policy varied until [[Vestian Persecutions and the Long Diaspora|Vestian's persecutions]] destroyed or confiscated most eastern towers in 377–398 AR. Baptised congregations of Jirahar descent in the gateway ports named the Second Silence when the lamps of Vestian's compulsory Profession failed to kindle in 385, and lodged their sworn accounts at the Raveth hearing house, which a crowd burned about 389; their dialect passed into the Velian liturgy. About 396 Vestian cleared the last inhabitants from Qiryath. The chancery's draft forbade rebuilding "in perpetuity"; Vestian struck the words out and wrote *before the Mesakha* in his own hand. Caleran's canonists read the clause as "never". The custodians at the gate read it as a condition, written in their persecutor's hand.

## Covenant and Successor Ages

Jirahar jurists, converts, and resident communities influenced early desert law. New towers arose under Covenant, Eclessian, Thalmyric, Sestaran, and Tarkhan charters.

Under the Banu Mizan emirs the law required a witness at every bound deathbed before a name could be released, and in the bond-hospitals and salt pans of Zanthir's dry margin the witnesses were often Jirahar physicians, whom Covenant courts and Velian families both accepted. Rab Yequtiel of Zanthir (c. 748–831 AR) sat at those beds for fifty years and wrote down what the dying asked for and whom they cursed. His book, *What the Register Keeps*, argues that a register holds a name and lets the person go. Custodians quoted it to quiet the dying, the Keepers of the Path called it a licence for bondage, and Velian copyists carried it into their churches.

In 1288 Meshalem, a road-clerk of Migdal Melach, proclaimed himself the Mesakha, because a Black Wind ride had burned a caravan camp on the Iqashar road and passed his wagons by. Families of half the tower towns sold or mortgaged what they owned and took the road to the Empty City, on roads the Black Wind had emptied of Covenant garrisons and at a price per wagon that Qarayel himself had named. Behind them the Covenant's courts declared their charters forfeit. By 1291 tens of thousands were camped outside Qiryath Reqa, and the riverland lords were threatening to clear the camp. The Keepers asked Meshalem to go down the stair before all of them and lay his hand on the silent gate. Twice he went down, and twice he came back up without touching it. The return broke up within the month. Its families had no tower towns to go home to, Sestaran creditors took what had been mortgaged, and many died on the roads or settled landless in the river towns. Meshalem the Twice-Turned stayed at the Empty City as a mourner, sweeping its outer court for a generation, until he died about 1316. [[Mesakha Traditions]]

## Present Day

Five living great towers remain. Qiryath is empty by law, held by Jirahar custodians under the Empty Charter. High Keeper Zerahiah holds no crown but commands extraordinary influence through rulings, access, and the Way-Gates. The Meshalem disaster still governs prophecy: every claimant is presumed false, and none may go down the gate stair until the Keepers have examined him.

## Historical Atlas — wider inheritance

The Jirahar inherit the One Glass People's formation and the later Near-Glass fission. Portable courts, route memory and translocal households acquired strength through water scarcity, distance and relations with neighbouring Tarkhan and Ahdari communities.

Tower towns and Qiryath added urban and sanctuary histories to that mobile inheritance. The First Destruction in 742 BR, exile under Arqesh and later return preceded Atherian unification. The Second Qiryath's destruction in 72 AR and Vestian's persecutions in 377–398 caused different displacements.

Communal descent and conversion law preserved identity across host societies. During the Long Frontier, translation, medicine, trade and household learning created several forms of public life. Theological disagreement after the Redeemer never authorises hereditary guilt. Their tales of strangers, family promises and unrecoverable homes belong beside the imperial histories which usually see only revolt and exile.
