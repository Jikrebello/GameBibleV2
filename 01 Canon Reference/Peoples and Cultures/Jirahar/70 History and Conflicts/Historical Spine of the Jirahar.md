# Historical Spine of the Jirahar

The Jirahar are a dispersed Ark-derived people whose history connects the One Glass People, the Near-Glass fission, the tower cities of Qiryath and repeated eras of exile. Portable courts, sacred learning, trade and communal descent allowed Jirahar communities to endure the destructions of 742 BR and 65 AR and the Vestian persecutions of 377–398. Five great towers and numerous smaller communities remain active across the world in 1360.

## Exodus and Scattering

The Great Prophet's movement became the centre of the Great Unbinding and led the principal liberated remnant into the Glass. Approximately a century of refuge formed one people. The Prophet died at the outward threshold around c. 2165 BR; no human office inherited his universal commission.

After the southern war-settlement leagues departed, the ancestors of the Jirahar remained within the Near-Glass camp continuum. Between approximately c. 1950 and 1750 BR, water, distance, marriage, trade, raiding and reciprocal Tarkhan contact produced the proto-Jirahar emphasis on portable courts, route memory, burial obligation and the Scrolls.

## First Qiryath

Between c. 1400 and 800 BR, seasonal courts became tower towns. Qiryath developed into the principal sanctuary and legal centre after centuries of road-house life, local judges and protector households.

The judges' centuries left stories the towers still tell against their own heroes. Road tradition says that around 1290 BR something in an old cistern on the eastern shelf answered the thresher Tovan bar Rameq by name and offered him the shelf's streams. The stream he called down on the raiders of House Sereq came out of season, and it drowned them together with the pledged children penned in their camp. The road judges gave his household no office, because the Prophet's people founded none on a bargain. The water went on answering his sister's children, as the shelf villages reckoned a line through mothers, and four of his sons drowned trying to take it from them. Hador bar Mavin made war on the toll fortress of Gedran around 1166. His own fire killed Nareh, the weigher's daughter he had come to free, and her father with her; his elders declared him unhoused, and he ended his life weighing orchard carts as the fortress's gate captain. As an old man he kept Gedran's custom and killed the child Seleth, surety for a harvest truce her father Othan chose to break, and Reqa women still bake a loaf in her name and leave it uneaten.

At Qiryath an accepted offering burned without smoke and left the altar stones cool to the hand. Its protectors became kings by the altar: a guardian who carried the appointed offering was acclaimed beside the cool stones at every festival, and around 959 BR the wood-carrier Yedran bar Aqev was named guardian because he caught the basket when a fevered guardian fell on the sanctuary stair. Around 945, after Yedran had left a ford garrison to die so that he could keep his place at the festival, the widow Rava bat Horel asked the custodians publicly whom the stones answered. They said the stones had only ever answered the offering, and the bearer was chosen by lot from then on. Around 826 the guardian Qoram ben Teth had a custodian killed so that an Arqeshite royal image could stand on the stair, and the stones were cool, as they always were. In the drought that followed, the prophet Haviel of the Lower Stair had the image's diviners thrown down the cistern they had drained to wash it, and the road judges unhoused him for killing without trial. ## Arqeshite Destruction and Exile

In 746 BR King Neshkar II of Arqesh demanded priestly hostages and a place for his image in the upper sanctuary, and his demand relied on the morning when a river king's image had stood on the stair beneath cool stones. Qiryath's council remembered what the diviners had made of that morning, and refused. In the first winter of the Arqeshite siege, around 743 BR, the council drove foreign residents out of the gates to save grain, and several died in the ditch between the lines. At the next appointed offering the smoke rose black and the stones were hot. In 742 the [[First Qiryath and the Arqeshite Exile|Arqeshite River Empire destroyed Qiryath]], deported leading households and scattered sacred vessels. The exiles prayed towards the ruin, their judges ruled on witnesses alone, and at every appointed festival a scribe wrote one more line in the Book of the Unanswered Years: the year, and "no answer". In a Nemeru canal gang the musician Sameth bar Yorin lost two fingers to a sluice winch, worked out a new fingering for the long reed pipe with the hand he had left, and set the Book's empty lines to the tune the deportees knew best.

Arqesh fell in 690 to provincial revolt and the separate armies of Arezan Tiravan and Eryndor III. Arezan's Decree of Roads and Houses permitted return after Eryndor's death in 684, and the Vasharan crown paid for the rebuilding, wagering that the sign would come back under its protection. The Second Qiryath was consecrated around 660 under Vasharan supremacy, and the offering made in the king's name smoked. A severe faction blamed the spouses and children of Arqeshite settlers and demanded their expulsion. The cistern keeper Yaelith bat Coren refused. Around 654, at the next appointed festival, her mixed households carried the offering, and the stones went cool. The Jirahar call those eighty-nine years the Withholding. Which act the silence answered is still argued, but the Jirahar have outlived one withholding, and they remember that it ended.

## Atherian Accommodation

Atheria absorbed the eastern riverlands in stages. Raveth's river towns left Vasharan canal duty for the empire around 160 BR, and Jirahar scribes, interpreters and store examiners followed the roads into imperial service. From 151 BR they could become citizens by swearing with the emperor's image veiled.

Qiryath passed to Atheria under the [[The Last Dynasties of the Age of Glory|late Serrats]]. During Vashar's War of the Two Dawns the sanctuary's Keeper fell on the cistern stair and died. An interim Keeper chosen by the city made the pilgrimage offering and the stones went cool; a month later a river-quarter house that had bought confirmation of the office from Daryam came up the Dawn Road with an escort and took the altar. The tower houses wrote to the Atherian governor at Arcelon, and at the next pilgrimage the governor's augur of the Red Star asked the sign to decide between them. The purchaser refused to make the Lord answer a foreigner's question. The interim Keeper offered before the crowd, and the fire smoked like any fire. Atherian writers called the day the Trial before the Stars; in the towers it was the Governor's Question.

The governor ruled on the sanctuary's written record of the earlier sign. A scribe of the house of Qoreth drafted the charter: sacrifice, courts and burial jurisdiction protected in Qiryath and the province's tower towns; no image in the sanctuary; a Keeper chosen by the council and confirmed by the governor; a daily offering for the emperor; the governor's seat at the pilgrimage and his sole power of death over offences against imperial rule; the sanctuary's dues as surety for the tribute; one copy in the sanctuary and one in the chancery. The defeated houses loaded their scrolls onto mules and went east into Vasharan country, telling the story their own way: the sign had refused to answer a governor. At Qiryath it went on answering the appointed offerings. Vashar took the Dawn Portion of pilgrim dues and called it a lease.

The Age of Glory created varied diaspora relationships with imperial towns and offices. [[The Keeper of Borrowed Grain|Neriah bar Sorek]] directed relief during the Raveth hunger from 119 BR. His policy saved towns while transferring land and labour to the imperial estate, and he resigned rather than administer the oath before the emperor's image that estate officers put in place of neighbours' countersignatures. Later praise and anti-Jirahar accusation both distorted responsibility shared with Emperor Elian II and Atherian officials.

## Isfir and the Silence

Isfir was born Jirahar, in 33 BR, in a tower town whose court sat under the charter's protection. In 0 AR a controlling council faction led by Ezrahel bar Qoreth pressed Atheria for his execution. His house kept the sanctuary copy of the charter, and every step of the referral ran through it: the governor sitting at the pilgrimage with an augur beside him, the clause for cases touching imperial rule, the governor's sole power of death. Atheria killed him; he rose. From that point, lawful sacrifices burned but no authenticated sign of acceptance returned. The Jirahar call it the Silence, and keep the name apart from the Withholding, which ended.

## Great Tower Revolt

In 61 AR the governor's officers took money from the sanctuary treasury for arrears, under the charter clause that made the sanctuary's dues surety for the tribute, and soldiers killed the people who protested. Initial victories gave way to civil division and siege. Atheria destroyed the sanctuary in 65 AR. The war party and several thousand refugees went down into Migdal Shahar, the Dawn Tower, two days east on the Dawn Road: a gatehouse above a city cut into the hill, with a Way-Gate in its deepest chamber. The legions dug down for two years and dropped the roofs of the outer galleries on the people sheltering in them. In 68 the bench of the partner gate, in a tower across the Vasharan frontier, agreed to take the households and no one who had borne arms. The war captain Eshkar bar Hanan refused terms that left his fighters to die. The gate keeper Yaltah bat Ozer opened the gate on the bench's terms anyway and sent the households through for nine days while the fighters held the stairs, then broke the gate's scroll-keys before the soldiers reached the chamber. The legions killed or sold whoever was left. Gate benches still cite Shahar when they weigh another tower's danger against its people. Qiryath lost its charter and passed under direct provincial rule, and Vashar's Dawn Portion ended with the sanctuary. Many displaced Jirahar sought refuge across the Vasharan frontier, where the houses that had refused the charter had settled long before, or among independent dry-margin communities. Older diaspora households helped them establish courts, secure burial grounds and resume their trades. [[Great Tower Revolt and the Second Destruction]]

## Long Diaspora

Surviving towers, quarters, fleets, and companies absorbed captives and refugees. Imperial policy varied until [[Vestian Persecutions and the Long Diaspora|Vestian's persecutions]] destroyed or confiscated most eastern towers in 377–398 AR. Baptised congregations of Jirahar descent in the gateway ports named the Second Silence when the lamps of Vestian's compulsory Profession failed to kindle in 385, and lodged their sworn accounts at the Raveth hearing house, which a crowd burned about 389; their dialect passed into the Velian liturgy. About 396 Vestian cleared the last inhabitants from Qiryath. The chancery's draft forbade rebuilding "in perpetuity"; Vestian struck the words out and wrote *before the Mesakha* in his own hand. Caleran's canonists read the clause as "never". The custodians at the gate read it as a condition, written in their persecutor's hand.

## Covenant and Successor Ages

Jirahar jurists, converts, and resident communities influenced early desert law. New towers arose under Covenant, Eclessian, Thalmyric, Sestaran, and Tarkhan charters.

Under the Banu Mizan emirs the law required a witness at every bound deathbed before a name could be released, and in the bond-hospitals and salt pans of Zanthir's dry margin the witnesses were often Jirahar physicians, whom Covenant courts and Velian families both accepted. Rab Yequtiel of Zanthir (c. 748–831 AR) sat at those beds for fifty years and wrote down what the dying asked for and whom they cursed. His book, *What the Register Keeps*, argues that a register holds a name and lets the person go. Custodians quoted it to quiet the dying, the Keepers of the Path called it a licence for bondage, and Velian copyists carried it into their churches.

In 1288 Meshalem, a road-clerk of Migdal Melach, proclaimed himself the Mesakha, because a Black Wind ride had burned a caravan camp on the Iqashar road and passed his wagons by. Families of half the tower towns sold or mortgaged what they owned and took the road to the Empty City, on roads the Black Wind had emptied of Covenant garrisons and at a price per wagon that Qarayel himself had named. Behind them the Covenant's courts declared their charters forfeit. By 1291 tens of thousands were camped outside Qiryath Reqa, and the riverland lords were threatening to clear the camp. The Keepers asked Meshalem to go down the stair before all of them and lay his hand on the silent gate. Twice he went down, and twice he came back up without touching it. The return broke up within the month. Its families had no tower towns to go home to, Sestaran creditors took what had been mortgaged, and many died on the roads or settled landless in the river towns. Meshalem the Twice-Turned stayed at the Empty City as a mourner, sweeping its outer court, until he died in 1303. [[Mesakha Traditions]]

## Present Day

Five living great towers remain. Qiryath is empty by law, held by Jirahar custodians under the Empty Charter. High Keeper Zerahiah holds no crown but commands extraordinary influence through rulings, access, and the Way-Gates. The Meshalem disaster still governs prophecy: every claimant is presumed false, and none may go down the gate stair until the Keepers have examined him.

## Historical Atlas — wider inheritance

The Jirahar inherit the One Glass People's formation and the later Near-Glass fission. Portable courts, route memory and translocal households acquired strength through water scarcity, distance and relations with neighbouring Tarkhan and Ahdari communities.

Tower towns and Qiryath added urban and sanctuary histories to that mobile inheritance. The First Destruction in 742 BR, exile under Arqesh and later return preceded Atherian unification. The Second Qiryath's destruction in 65 AR and Vestian's persecutions in 377–398 caused different displacements.

Communal descent and conversion law preserved identity across host societies. During the Long Frontier, translation, medicine, trade and household learning created several forms of public life. Theological disagreement after the Redeemer never authorises hereditary guilt. Their tales of strangers, family promises and unrecoverable homes belong beside the imperial histories which usually see only revolt and exile.
