# Great Tower Revolt and the Second Destruction

The Great Tower Revolt began in the spring of 61 AR, when the emperor's daily offering lay unburnt on Qiryath's altar for nine mornings and the governor's soldiers killed the altar's custodians on the sanctuary stair. No Atherian army came against the city for three years. The legions went round it to the Dawn Road and dug down into the deep tower of Migdal Shahar, whose households went out through its Way-Gate in the spring of 64 AR while its fighters held the stairs. Second Qiryath fell in 67 AR. Its Keeper had sent the sanctuary's vessels through the city's own gate before the breach, the legate's augur burned the emperor's offering on the altar and the sanctuary with it, and the triumph at Caleran carried empty litters. Atheria abolished the charter under which Qiryath had governed itself, laid the price of the missing vessels on every Jirahar household it ruled and put the city under a garrison. Captivity and flight scattered the city's people, and many sought refuge beyond Atherian rule, where older communities already had households, courts and agreements with local rulers.

## The altar before the war

After Isfir's death the silence of sacrificial acceptance divided Qiryath. Every morning the offerings went up the stair, the fire smoked, and the custodians of the three old houses laid their hands on the altar stones and said aloud that they were hot. Militants claimed the sign would return when foreign rule ended. They cited the Trial before the Stars, when the sign had stayed silent before an Atherian governor's augur. Older houses answered that the Withholding had ended under a Vasharan king, at an offering carried by households the severe faction of that day wanted expelled. Cautious houses feared another Arqeshite ruin and held to the charter.

The charter required a daily offering on the emperor's behalf. The governor supplied the beast and its wood from imperial stores, so that no Jirahar household paid for it, and it went up the stair first. For sixty-one years it had burned as every offering had burned since the Silence, with smoke.

## The Nine Mornings, 61 AR

On the first morning of the spring pilgrimage in 61, after three days of rain, the emperor's bull would not burn. The fire sank under it to a bed of hissing embers and the carcass lay raw on stones too hot to touch. The offerings laid after it on the sanctuary's own seasoned wood burned as they always did. The paved yard was full of pilgrims from every road in the east, and by noon the whole city had been up the stair to look.

It happened again the next morning, and the next. The Keeper of the sanctuary, **Elqan bar Sered**, had the offering laid afresh each day before witnesses and said that a bull which would not burn was a question about wood until the Book of the Unanswered Years said otherwise. The war houses said the Lord had begun to speak again, and that his first word was against the emperor: strike the emperor's portion from the day, and the stones would cool. For nine mornings the bull lay raw, and each evening more pilgrims left for home with the story.

In imperial law a wonder existed only when an augur college had entered it, and since the Great Purge it had been a crime to publish one the colleges had not. The governor's augur of the Red Star, **Dessan Vole**, entered the nine mornings in the provincial college's book as a fraud against the emperor's name. On the tenth, Governor **Halen Corvath** sat at the pilgrimage tribunal outside the sanctuary gate, where the charter gave him the power of death, demanded the carriers of the emperor's wood for trial and sent a file of soldiers up the stair with a fresh bull and a cask of army pitch. No foreigner had stood on that stair since the charter was sealed. The custodians of the three old houses stood across it with their witness-staffs. The soldiers killed them where they stood, and then the people who came up behind them; the towers' count is two hundred and six dead on the stair and in the paved yard. The bull burned in pitch smoke, and Vole entered it as answered.

By evening the garrison was shut inside the tribunal. Within the month it had been driven from its posts, and Corvath had fled down the river road to Arcelon.

## The city that kept its charter, 61–64 AR

Corvath had too few men to come back. The legate **Karsen Maruth** brought legions east in 62, and he did not march on Qiryath. The province's tower towns had risen behind it, and its door to Vashar stood open on the Dawn Road. Maruth's commanders spent that year and the next recovering crossings, fortified towns and storehouses. The campaign left burned villages and long lines of captives and drove people towards the two great towers of the province. Qiryath appointed commanders and collected food and money in its own name. Towns and travelling companies divided over whether to answer its summons.

Inside the city two parties held different stairs. The Keeper and the charter houses held the sanctuary and the upper tower. Elqan kept every clause of the charter that the governor had broken. The emperor's offering went up first each morning, on the sanctuary's own wood now, and burned like any other. "We have broken nothing," he wrote to the court at Caleran, "and when you come to judge us you will find that we have broken nothing." The war houses under the captain **Zevad bar Tirzan** held the walls and the cistern stairs, and wanted the emperor's portion gone.

At the autumn assembly of 62 a carrier of the emperor's wood, **Gedor bar Ammi**, stood up before the council. He said that from the second morning to the ninth he had laid the emperor's bull on billets soaked overnight in a cistern, because the governor's men had flogged his brother to death at the tribunal in the year before the war. He said he had done nothing on the first morning. The war houses called it a confession bought by the Keeper. Zevad's men killed Gedor in the wood-store that night, before the council could question him, and in the months that followed they killed others, among them people trying to arrange a surrender.

Corvath had shut the pilgrim roads in 61 and they stayed shut. No dues came in, and Vashar's Dawn Portion stopped with them. Cautious households left while the roads east and north were open, taking what they could carry. Others chose the tower's cisterns and walls over flight.

## The Dawn Tower, 62–64 AR

Migdal Shahar, the Dawn Tower, stood two days east of Qiryath on the Dawn Road, the old pilgrim and tribute road to Vashar. Above ground it was a gatehouse, a watch-tower and a walled court. Below, generations had cut dwelling halls, granaries, cisterns and burial galleries into the hill, and in the deepest chamber stood one of the old Way-Gates. Its partner gate lay in an older tower across the Vasharan frontier, where the houses that had refused the Qiryath charter had settled a century and a half before.

Maruth went there first, to shut the road by which Qiryath could be fed or emptied. By the summer of 62 Shahar held its own households, the war houses of the Dawn Road villages and several thousand refugees from the burned country. The legions took the upper tower within a month and found that they held a gatehouse. The city was underneath, its stairs blocked and defended and its cisterns full. For two years the besiegers dug down instead of building up. Their engineers did what Atherian siege engineers had done since Darneth: they loaded a siege frame before a priest of the Red Star until it broke, and read from the break where the ground over the outer galleries would fail. Then they piled quarry stone where the break pointed. The roofs of the outer refuge halls came down on the people sheltering in them. Families dug some of the dead out from below; many were never reached. Shahar's survivors said the Star had shown the enemy where to dig. The legate's engineers called it survey.

In the winter of 63 the tower's council sent word to the bench of the partner gate and asked it to open. An opening would tell Atheria where the gate led, and a tower that took in the rebels might bring the legions to Vashar's border or tempt Vashar to sell them back. The bench argued through the winter and answered in the spring of 64: it would receive the households, and no one who had borne arms in the revolt. The war captain, **Eshkar bar Hanan**, would not accept terms that left his fighters to die. The gate keeper, **Yaltah bat Ozer**, opened on the bench's terms anyway. For nine days she sent the households through in parties of a few hundred, with their children, their scrolls and their dead's names, while the fighters held the stairs above. Some fighters went through as fathers with empty hands. The receiving tower's records and Shahar's later histories still argue over how many, and over whether the bench was right to refuse the rest. Eshkar stayed on the stair.

On the tenth day the soldiers broke into the lower halls. Yaltah broke the gate's scroll-keys before they reached the chamber, and the gate at Shahar has not opened since. The legions killed the fighters, the keepers and many of the old and sick who had refused to leave their dead, and sold the people they took alive. The legate's report says the rebels killed their own wounded and old before they "went into the ground like ants"; the receiving tower's mourning court records the same dead as killed by the legions. Atherian envoys asked Vashar for the Shahar households for years afterwards, and the Vasharan court answered that it knew of no gate.

## The siege of Qiryath, 64–67 AR

Maruth now knew what a deep tower was and what stood at the bottom of one. He marched west and closed Qiryath in the autumn of 64. The city he closed held no pilgrims, since none had come for three years, and fewer of its own people than at any time in a century. Its refuge halls were full of the province: villagers driven in ahead of the burning with whatever food they could carry.

He did not storm the walls. His engineers broke their frames before the Red Star and dropped the cistern galleries one after another, beginning with the outer reservoirs under the river quarter. Qiryath went dry before it went hungry. Thirst, hunger and the flux went through the refuge halls together. Soldiers killed or displayed some of the people who tried to get out.

The offering did not stop. When the beasts were gone the priests burned a handful of barley each morning, the emperor's first, and the custodians who had succeeded the dead of 61 laid their hands on the stones and said that they were hot.

Maruth's envoys had been at the Vasharan court since Shahar, and every tower that held keys to Qiryath's gate had learned what receiving cost. The Keeper asked the benches to take the city's children. None would open for anyone who could be counted coming out or asked where they had come out. In the last winter one bench, which the Keepers have never named, agreed to receive what could not be questioned. Over two nights before the spring of 67 Elqan and the custodial houses carried the vessels of the altar and the custodians' books down the gate stair and sent them through. Qiryath's gate has not opened since.

The legions came in through the fallen galleries of the river quarter a few weeks later. Fighting reached the sanctuary precinct, and the soldiers killed defenders and civilians inside it. That morning's barley had already been burned. Dessan Vole, six years older, had a bull carried up the stair he had first sent one up in 61, laid it on Qiryath's altar on cedar from the sanctuary's own store and lit it himself. It burned. The fire went from the altar into the cedar of the roofs and from the roofs into the upper tower. Maruth's dispatch said the Red Star had answered for the emperor on the altar that had refused him, and that the house had been the Star's share. Survivors remembered soldiers carrying brands from the altar to the cedar stores, and a systematic destruction of the precinct once it was taken. Nobody disputes who lit the fire. Whether Maruth meant the whole precinct to burn with his bull is still argued.

His officers went through the treasury rooms with the sanctuary's own inventory, taken from the Qoreth archive, and found shelves. They went down the gate stair and found a swept chamber and a gate that did nothing. The engineers loaded a frame before the Red Star in the chamber itself; it showed them where the stair would fail and nothing else. The defences were demolished. Captives were sorted for sale, forced service and ransom. Elqan was taken alive and questioned at Arcelon about where the gate led, and died under the question the following winter without naming a bench.

The siege works and destruction cut off access to the burial slope where Isfir had been laid. The households that knew the approach dispersed, and later pilgrims could no longer establish which chamber had held him.

## The empty litters

Maruth held his triumph at Caleran the year after the city fell. The sanctuary's carrying-litters went through the streets empty, each behind a painted board showing what the inventory said it had held. The crowd counted the boards and laughed, and a street song about the general who conquered a cupboard outlived him. When the Othran succession failed, the eastern army put Maruth forward, and the ports preferred a supply officer. He never ruled.

Among the Road Companies there is a hard saying about the two towers: *Shahar sent its children; Qiryath sent its cups.* The Keepers answer that no bench offered Qiryath's children a door. Where the vessels went, no Keeper has ever said. In the towers a thing long expected and not arrived is said to be on the road with the vessels.

## Direct rule, the Litter Debt and exile

The Atherian governor annulled the sanctuary charter after the city's capture. Imperial officers took the courts, cisterns, roads and records of land. A permanent garrison held the crossings and the surviving approaches, and the governor assessed confiscated sanctuary and rebel estates as imperial property.

The treasury would not write off the triumph. Its clerks reckoned the weight of the missing vessels from the inventory and laid it as a debt on every Jirahar household under Atherian rule, to be paid by the head each year until the vessels were surrendered. Nothing paid was ever counted against the sum. The clerks called it the vessel reckoning and the towers called it the **Litter Debt**. The rolls drawn up to collect it went on telling officers where Jirahar households lived for three centuries, and Vestian's searchers used them. A diminished settlement of labourers, returning families and households granted leave to remain lived at Qiryath under direct provincial rule. Vestian's clearance, about 396 AR, ended that settlement and left the city empty under a charter forbidding rebuilding "before the Mesakha". [[Vestian Persecutions and the Long Diaspora]]

The free exile from Qiryath went chiefly beyond direct Atherian control. Across the eastern frontier, households sought kin in communities under Vasharan authority, where the Shahar households had come out of the partner gate three years earlier into a tower that had agreed to take them and not their defenders. Others took dry-margin routes through independent well towns and territories where caravan leaders could negotiate protection. Hosts bargained over water, pasture, taxes, burial ground and responsibility for newcomers. Some accepted a family while refusing its armed escort. Older settlements absorbed relatives, teachers and craftspeople as well as people with few resources to offer. Captives sent into the empire and Jirahar communities already living there had a different experience of the same defeat.

The sanctuary priesthood lost its place of service. Lawful sacrifice at Qiryath never resumed. Study halls, households, travelling courts and teachers of the Peshara took on work previously coordinated through the sanctuary. They preserved names, judged disputed vows, found money for ransoms and kept feast days across different jurisdictions. Older diaspora institutions supplied places and people capable of receiving the exiles.

## What is still disputed

The killing on the stair, the nine days at Shahar, the collapses, the killing in the lower halls, the fire lit on the altar and the empty litters are established. The towers still argue over what happened on the first of the Nine Mornings, and over whether Gedor bar Ammi's confession was his own. They argue over how many went through Shahar's gate, how many fighters went as fathers, who killed its wounded and whether the bench could have taken more. They argue over whether Qiryath's Keeper asked first for the people or for the vessels. Jirahar jurists hold that Qiryath never broke its charter, and that a governor broke it on the stair. Jirahar gate benches still cite Shahar when they weigh another tower's danger against its people.
