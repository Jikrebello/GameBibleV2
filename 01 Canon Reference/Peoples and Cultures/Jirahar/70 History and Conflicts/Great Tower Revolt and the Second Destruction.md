# Great Tower Revolt and the Second Destruction

The Great Tower Revolt began in 61 AR against Atherian rule. In 65 AR the legions took Second Qiryath and burned its sanctuary. The last rebel stronghold, the deep tower of Migdal Shahar on the Dawn Road, fell in 68 AR after most of its households had gone out through its Way-Gate and its fighters had stayed behind to hold the stairs. Atheria abolished the charter under which Qiryath had governed its communal affairs and placed the city and its approaches under direct military and provincial authority. Captivity and flight scattered the city's people. Many displaced Jirahar sought refuge beyond direct Atherian rule, where older communities already had households, courts and agreements with local rulers.

## Causes

After Isfir's death, the silence of sacrificial acceptance divided Qiryath. Militants claimed the sign would return when foreign rule ended. They cited the Trial before the Stars, when the sign had stayed silent before an Atherian governor's augur. Older houses answered that the Withholding had ended under a Vasharan king, at an offering carried by households the severe faction of that day wanted expelled. Cautious houses feared another Arqeshite ruin and tried to preserve the charter. In 61 AR the governor's officers took money from the sanctuary treasury on the pretext of arrears, under the charter's clause that made the sanctuary's dues surety for the tribute. When people protested, soldiers killed demonstrators near the sanctuary. Intermediaries tried to arrange payment and secure the withdrawal of troops. Militant priests stopped the offering made on behalf of the emperor, and the garrison lost its local supply and protection.

## Revolt, 61 AR

Rebels drove the garrison from its posts and broke the first relief force as it withdrew through the river approaches. They took its siege equipment and stores. Qiryath appointed commanders and collected food and money in its own name. Towns and travelling companies divided over whether to answer its summons. Priestly houses, popular captains and aristocratic moderates competed over the city's defences. Armed factions killed rivals, including people trying to negotiate a surrender.

From 62 to 64 AR Atherian commanders recovered crossings, fortified towns and storehouses before marching on Qiryath. The campaign left burned villages and captives and drove people towards the city. Some households crossed the frontier before the siege closed. Others chose the tower's cisterns and walls over flight, and brought what food they could carry.

## Siege and Destruction, 65 AR

The Atherian army arrived while the city still held pilgrims and refugees. The legions breached the outer defences, built a siege line around the remaining city and cut off its river landings. Rival factions had burned or seized stores to deny them to one another. Hunger and disease spread through the refuge halls. Soldiers killed or displayed some people who tried to escape. The ordinary sacrifice ceased during the siege as the priests ran out of people and provisions to continue it.

In the final assault, fighting reached the sanctuary precinct. Fire spread into the sanctuary and upper tower, and the legions killed defenders and civilians inside. The commanders' accounts later blamed the first flame on soldiers acting without orders. Survivors remembered a systematic destruction of the precinct once it was taken. The first ignition remains disputed. Imperial officers stripped the sanctuary, took its vessels to Caleran for a triumph, and demolished defences after the fire. Captives were sorted for sale, forced service and ransom.

The siege works and destruction cut off access to the burial slope where Isfir had been laid. The households that knew the approach dispersed, and later pilgrims could no longer establish which chamber had held him.

## The Dawn Tower, 65–68 AR

Migdal Shahar, the Dawn Tower, stood two days east of Qiryath on the Dawn Road, the old pilgrim and tribute road to Vashar. Above ground it was a gatehouse, a watch-tower and a walled court. Below, generations had cut dwelling halls, granaries, cisterns and burial galleries into the hill, and in the deepest chamber stood one of the old Way-Gates. Its partner gate lay in an older tower across the Vasharan frontier, where the houses that had refused the Qiryath charter had settled a century and a half before.

After Qiryath fell, the surviving war party and several thousand refugees went down into Shahar. The legions took the upper tower within a month and found that they held a gatehouse. The city was underneath, its stairs blocked and defended and its cisterns full. For two years the besiegers dug down instead of building up. Their engineers did what Atherian siege engineers had done since Darneth: they loaded a siege frame before a priest of the Red Star until it broke, and read from the break where the ground over the outer galleries would fail. Then they piled quarry stone where the break pointed. The roofs of the outer refuge halls came down on the people sheltering in them. Families dug some of the dead out from below; many were never reached. Shahar's survivors said the Star had shown the enemy where to dig. The legate's engineers called it survey.

In the winter of 67 the city's council sent word to the bench of the partner gate and asked it to open. An opening would tell Atheria where the gate led, and a tower that took in the rebels might bring the legions to Vashar's border or tempt Vashar to sell them back. The bench argued through the winter and answered in the spring of 68: it would receive the households, and no one who had borne arms in the revolt. The war captain, **Eshkar bar Hanan**, would not accept terms that left his fighters to die. The gate keeper, **Yaltah bat Ozer**, opened on the bench's terms anyway. For nine days she sent the households through in parties of a few hundred, with their children, their scrolls and their dead's names, while the fighters held the stairs above. Some fighters went through as fathers with empty hands. The receiving tower's records and Shahar's later histories still argue over how many, and over whether the bench was right to refuse the rest. Eshkar stayed on the stair.

On the tenth day the soldiers broke into the lower halls. Yaltah broke the gate's scroll-keys before they reached the chamber, and the gate at Shahar has not opened since. The legions killed the fighters, the keepers and many of the old and sick who had refused to leave their dead, and sold the people they took alive. The legate's report says the rebels killed their own wounded and old before they "went into the ground like ants"; the receiving tower's mourning court records the same dead as killed by the legions. Atherian envoys asked Vashar for the Shahar households for years afterwards, and the Vasharan court answered that it knew of no gate. The collapses, the nine days and the killing in the lower halls are established. The number who went through, the number of fighters who went as fathers, who killed the wounded, and whether the bench could have taken more remain disputed. Jirahar gate benches still cite Shahar when they weigh another tower's danger against its people.

## Direct rule and exile

The Atherian governor annulled the sanctuary charter after the city's capture. Imperial officers took the courts, cisterns, roads and records of land and tribute. A permanent garrison held the crossings and the surviving approaches. The governor assessed confiscated sanctuary and rebel estates as imperial property and levied a tribute in place of the old sanctuary contribution within the empire. The tribute lists drawn up in those years went on telling officers where Jirahar households lived for three centuries, and Vestian's searchers used them. A diminished settlement of labourers, returning families and households granted leave to remain lived under direct provincial rule. Vestian's clearance, about 396 AR, ended that settlement and left the city empty under a charter forbidding rebuilding "before the Mesakha". [[Vestian Persecutions and the Long Diaspora]]

The free exile from Qiryath went chiefly beyond direct Atherian control. Across the eastern frontier, households sought kin in communities under Vasharan authority, and the Shahar households came out of the partner gate into a tower that had agreed to take them and not their defenders. Others took dry-margin routes through independent well towns and territories where caravan leaders could negotiate protection. Hosts bargained over water, pasture, taxes, burial ground and responsibility for newcomers. Some accepted a family while refusing its armed escort. Older settlements absorbed relatives, teachers and craftspeople as well as people with few resources to offer. Captives sent into the empire and Jirahar communities already living there had a different experience of the same defeat.

The sanctuary priesthood lost its place of service. Lawful sacrifice at Qiryath never resumed. Study halls, households, travelling courts and teachers of the Peshara took on work previously coordinated through the sanctuary. They preserved names, judged disputed vows, found money for ransoms and kept feast days across different jurisdictions. Older diaspora institutions supplied places and people capable of receiving the exiles.
