# Historical Atlas of Qiryath before Atheria

The early history of **Qiryath** follows Jirahar communities from the Near-Glass road camps to a populous sanctuary city under Vasharan rule. Between about 2165 and 350 BR, travelling households developed public stores, burial duties and portable judgement; local protectors defended or exploited settlements; and towers gathered workshops, markets and schools around dependable water. Arqesh destroyed the first city in 742 BR. Deportees, surviving villages and road families later rebuilt it without recovering its former sovereignty.

## Historical setting

Qiryath arose on the **Reqa shelf**, a broken limestone country between the Near-Glass roads and the western approaches to the eastern riverlands. Seasonal streams descended from the shelf into the cultivated valleys below. Three dependable springs, several older cistern fields and a ford across the lower **Ubar River** made the district useful long before it possessed a sacred city.

The country supported villages, orchards, grazing grounds and caravan yards. Its people cultivated barley, lentils, onions and hardy fruit, kept goats and pack animals, made coarse cloth and repaired goods carried between richer countries. Timber remained scarce. Large roofs, siege works and the upper storeys of the first tower therefore depended upon imported beams. Qiryath's later prominence rested upon the labour and food of this surrounding country as much as upon pilgrimage.

The ancestors of the Jirahar entered this inhabited region gradually. They descended principally from the Near-Glass camp continuum, but their households lived beside Orphaned well keepers, river farmers and survivors of former Giant estates. Marriage, adoption and conversion drew some of these neighbours into the developing people. Other communities remained distinct and negotiated with the road houses as trading partners, rivals or landlords.

## The road-house centuries, c. 2165–1750 BR

The Prophet's death left the One Glass People with several necessary offices and no universal human successor. Judges heard disputes between families and camps. Provisioners counted food and decided when a common store could be opened. Captains organised guards and chose how a travelling column would meet an attack. Archivists preserved teachings and the names of the living and the dead. Each office answered a particular need; the Prophet's commission passed to none of them.

The southern leagues departed first. Households remaining near the Glass continued to share camps, marriages and worship for generations. Drought and distance gradually changed this common life. Some communities fortified reliable wells and treated the surrounding ground as a territory. Others occupied several routes and needed a judgement made in one camp to be recognised in another. Their judges began travelling between agreed stopping places, hearing disputes in the presence of household elders and carrying their decisions to the next camp. This practice became an ancestor of the Jirahar travelling bench.

The camps were made up of extended households rather than isolated families. A household might contain married kin, widows, servants, clients, converts and enslaved people. Most grain, goats and pack animals belonged to particular households. Each camp also maintained a common store for guards, sick people and families whose food had failed. Grain borrowed from that store was normally repaid after the next harvest, while the owners of pack animals expected labour or a share of the cargo in return for carrying another family's goods.

The oldest road traditions name **Mareh bat Sivan**, a widow who managed three related camps after a failed harvest and a fever killed their senior provisioners around the eighteenth century BR. She had worked beside the dead provisioners and knew what the store contained. Mareh weighed the household sacks in public, postponed repayment of grain loans until the next harvest and divided the common reserve among the fever tents. She kept the sound pack animals from being slaughtered because they would have to carry water, seed grain and people too weak to walk across the next waterless stretch of road.

An armed captain attempted to take the remaining grain for his followers. Mareh required every person seeking a portion to carry food to the sick before receiving it. When the captain struck her brother, the household elders and camp guards drove him out. Two families followed him and later founded a fortified well community. Mareh's rationing carried the three camps through the fever and preserved enough seed and transport for their departure. The public weighing of every sack gave her the later name **Mother of Measures**.

Her road tradition also remembers the people whom the camps failed. During the departure, the cart of a servant household split its axle. The household comprised a hired driver, his wife, their two children and his widowed mother. The camps had already lost animals and were trying to cross the waterless ground before their remaining supply ran out. After an unsuccessful attempt to repair the cart, the elders ordered the column onward. Some recitations say that Mareh opposed the decision; others say that she gave the deciding order. Raiders found the stranded family and sold them farther east. A later road house paid for two descendants' release, while the others disappeared into the river cities.

Work supplied many households with their public names. The **Bronze-Pan House** repaired cooking vessels and scales. The **Night-Rein House** guided animals between distant water. The **Washers of Nahal Or** prepared bodies and negotiated burial ground. These names passed through kinship, apprenticeship and adoption and could endure after the original occupation changed.

Burial carried unusual importance. The people who named and buried a dead traveller accepted responsibility for mourning, for notifying distant kin and for obligations that the death had left unfinished. Jirahar households carried these duties with them when they moved. Proto-Ahdari well coalitions increasingly based inheritance and authority upon control of the surrounding land. Disputes over whether a grave created a travelling family duty or a claim to the ground helped widen the division between the two peoples. Families continued to cross it by marriage for generations.

## The Bronze Roads, c. 1750–1325 BR

Between the eighteenth and fifteenth centuries BR, the northern routes became part of a broad exchange system. Qahuri grain and linen travelled east. Copper and worked bronze came from several upland and coastal sources. Gateway ships brought salt, timber and foreign cloth. Arqeshite canal cities exported grain, glazed wares and writing materials. Tarkhan carriers supplied hides, animals and knowledge of the open country. Captives moved with the same traffic.

Proto-Jirahar households found work between jurisdictions. They repaired carts, treated animals, translated bargains, sounded river landings and washed the dead. The fair at **Mattan Yard**, held after the first autumn rain, became known for mule races, marriage negotiations and a stew so heavily seasoned that later cooks claimed it could reveal a dishonest guest. Children learned route sequences as cumulative songs. Musicians returning from Arqesh added reed instruments to older frame drums.

Trade passed through a patchwork of royal districts, temple lands, fortified wells and independent towns. The ruling households of palace towns requisitioned animals and drivers. A successful merchant could claim the labour of a debtor's family. Escort companies sold information to raiders. Town guilds hired travelling smiths during shortages and expelled them when permanent workshops recovered. Road elders used coded phrases to protect children and routes, and powerful elders sometimes used the same secrecy to conceal violence within their households.

The **Breaking of the Bronze Roads**, conventionally dated c. 1450–1325 BR, unfolded district by district. Harvests declined in different years. Palace wars closed crossings. Armed sailors seized coastal stores. Refugees travelled beside mercenaries and slave dealers. Royal officials took pack animals faster than breeders could replace them. By the time the principal palace routes failed, many households had already shortened their journeys or abandoned them.

Tower settlements expanded as long journeys became dangerous. Stores, cisterns and workshops gathered around visible refuges. Travelling families shortened their circuits and relied upon settled branches. A tower could keep a district alive through a poor season. It could also refuse grain to families unwilling to accept its preferred judge.

## Judges of reed and bronze, c. 1400–1050 BR

Later Jirahar teaching arranges this period around judges and deliverers who lived in different districts and generations. Their stories follow communities that entrusted military, judicial or religious authority to a successful protector and then had to restrain the power they had granted.

The earliest of the developed cycles concerns **Tovan bar Rameq**, the youngest son of a threshing household on the eastern shelf around 1290 BR. The armed **House Sereq** descended after harvest to take grain, animals and children as pledges from three villages. Tovan first appears hiding barley in an abandoned cistern. An unnamed messenger finds him there and tells him to tear down the tribute image kept by his own father. Tovan asks for two signs involving a clay measure left overnight beneath the open sky. The surviving recitations describe the signs as genuine, although their exact nature remains uncertain.

Tovan broke the image and called the villages to fight. Most of the men who answered could not remain away from their fields and families, so he retained a small night force of hunters, drovers and threshers. They entered the Sereq camp carrying horns and lamps concealed inside grain jars. When the jars broke and the horns sounded from several slopes, frightened animals ran through the camp and the raiders began killing one another in the dark. Tovan's followers pursued the survivors, killed men attempting to surrender and recovered captives and winter stores.

The villages offered Tovan a hereditary protectorate. He refused the title but accepted a bronze ring from each household and used the metal to make a ceremonial standard. Families began carrying disputes to the object and treating its movement in lamplight as an answer. Tovan accepted several political marriages and allowed his sons to command the villages in his name. After his death, a son born to a lower-ranking partner hired armed men, murdered most of his half-brothers on the old threshing floor and briefly took the authority their father had refused in words. The cycle remembers a real deliverance, the slaughter of surrendered enemies and a sacred object that helped turn gratitude into dynastic violence.

**Tirzah bat Oren**, an animal physician from the Night-Rein House, became a judge after diagnosing deliberate poisoning at a chain of cisterns around 1210 BR. The armed household paid to defend the wells blamed foreign drovers and prepared to seize their children as hostages. Tirzah demonstrated that the poison came from an allied orchard owner who expected to claim the grazing land after the drovers fled. She then helped organise the pursuit when the owner withdrew behind armed retainers. The fighting killed two of her brothers and several field workers who had taken no part in the poisoning. The drovers recovered their surviving animals, buried the dead and left the district.

The cycle of **Hador bar Mavin**, placed around 1170 BR, belongs to orchard villages under the toll fortress of Gedran. His parents had been childless for many years and dedicated him before birth to a sanctuary vow. He was to leave his hair uncut, refuse strong drink and avoid contact with the dead. The songs describe him as an entirely human man of extraordinary strength whose gift served that vow. They also remember how often appetite, pride and anger governed him.

Hador courted **Nareh bat Dovan**, a woman of Gedran, and set an impossible riddle during their marriage feast. Her relatives threatened her household until she obtained the answer from him. Hador killed men on the road to pay the lost wager with their clothing. When Nareh's father gave her to another man, Hador burned the fortress grain fields. Gedran's rulers answered by burning Nareh and her father inside their house. Hador then killed guards, labourers and villagers in a private war which weakened the fortress without freeing the district.

Years later, Gedran's lords paid **Delira of the South Gate**, Hador's lover, and threatened to enslave her brothers unless she discovered the condition of his vow. Hador deceived her several times and assaulted men sent to bind him. He eventually told her about his hair. The fortress guard shaved him while he slept, blinded him and put him to work turning a grain mill. During a public feast, Hador was brought into the pillared hall as an object of ridicule. He prayed, tore down two supports and died beneath the building with Gedran's ruling household, its guards, servants, entertainers and captives. Later heroic songs count only the enemy dead. The orchard lament names Nareh, the mill workers and the prisoners killed with him. Gedran survived the disaster and remained strong enough for Othan's coalition to fight a generation later.

**Othan bar Raqel** commanded a coalition against the toll fortress of Gedran around 1140 BR. Before the campaign he vowed that the first person to greet him at Mattan would belong wholly to the sanctuary. His young daughter **Seleth** came out carrying a hand drum. The oldest account says that Othan killed her despite judges who offered payment and substitution as a lawful release from the vow. Later versions turn her into a lifelong temple singer or make the victim an animal. A women's fast held in several Reqa villages preserves the harsher ending and names Seleth rather than her father.

Another cycle concerns **Barakhel of the Reed Cloak**, who destroyed a raiding household by pretending to dismiss most of his followers before harvest. His small night force opened the enemy pens and drove frightened animals through the camp. The victory freed captives and ended several years of raids. Barakhel subsequently executed surrendered men after promising them safe conduct. His sons inherited a toll station and used his reputation to collect payment from the families he had rescued.

Repeated raids and the slow assembly of local councils encouraged settlements to retain successful protectors for longer terms. Tovan's night victory, Hador's private war, Othan's vow and Barakhel's executions gave later teachers very different examples of heroic power. A captain with loyal followers could recover captives and guard harvests after a travelling judge had departed. The same followers could massacre surrendered enemies, enforce a family cult or turn rescued villages into hereditary clients.

## The rise of First Qiryath, c. 1050–800 BR

Qiryath grew around the largest of the Reqa refuges. Its earliest monumental works were practical: a deep communal cistern, grain rooms, animal courts and a paved yard where several travelling benches could sit at once. The sanctuary developed beside them. It housed readings associated with the Prophet's road, objects carried from older camps and an altar served by several custodial families.

**Amariah bat Qoreb**, remembered as the first Keeper of the Upper Room, brought rival calendars into a common sequence around 1020 BR. Her calendar fixed the weeks of common pilgrimage while allowing remote towers to begin agricultural observances according to local conditions. Large gatherings became easier to provision and Qiryath's teachers gained a wider audience. Its custodians also acquired influence over market dates and the public standing of communities accused of observing the wrong week.

Permanent neighbourhoods spread below the tower and along the slope. Gardeners used carefully rationed spring water. Potters and metalworkers occupied the smoke quarter. Porters lived near the caravan yard. Foreign merchants maintained guest houses. The surrounding villages supplied grain, oil, fruit, animals and labour. These permanent workshops, markets, councils and waterworks made Qiryath the first large Jirahar city.

Qiryath's authority remained uneven. Some towers accepted its teachers but rejected its tax collectors. Road houses feared that the custodians of sacred objects would also claim their property. The councils answered military danger by granting longer commands to protector families. Within several generations the chief protector commanded soldiers, collected grain and arranged alliances in the manner of a king, although later sources disagree over the title.

## Yedran and the disputed crown

The best remembered protector was **Yedran bar Aqev**, born around 985 BR to a household that kept goats for the sanctuary and supplied singers at seasonal feasts. After killing a river prince's champion in single combat, Yedran entered the service of the protector **Shaqel Oram**. He married Shaqel's daughter **Mikalet bat Oram** and gathered a following among debtors, expelled craftsmen and refugees from the eastern canals.

Shaqel came to fear the younger man's popularity. Yedran spent several years as an outlaw along the shelf, protecting some villages while extorting food from others. When Arqeshite-backed raiders killed Shaqel, Yedran returned, defeated the responsible client house and accepted the title **Guardian of Qiryath**. Songs written two centuries later call him king. Road-house accounts insist that he was only the strongest protector recognised by a frightened city.

Yedran restored cisterns, admitted foreign artisans and established a regular grain contribution for defence. He also used office to take **Rava bat Horel**, the wife of his loyal officer **Uriad bar Sem**, into his household. Uriad was sent to hold an exposed river post and died when promised relief failed to arrive. Rava later secured property for her surviving children and publicly accused Yedran during a pilgrimage feast. His confession ended the celebration, and the struggle among his sons soon reached Rava's household and the families that had supported Uriad.

The household war following Yedran's death broke the protector dynasty. One son killed a half-brother after the rape of their sister; another raised rural followers against Qiryath; Mikalet's kin seized part of the grain stores. The city survived because village councils and road houses continued supplying it after the palace household failed. Later royalists remember Yedran as the founder of civic order. Rava's lament contrasts the poor goat keeper who once begged food from villages with the ruler who controlled Qiryath's granaries and army. Its image of a hungry man eating from a golden bowl accuses Yedran of having ample wealth and power when he still took Uriad's wife and arranged the posting in which Uriad died.

## Councils, prophets and the last protector houses

Qiryath's government after Yedran combined a council of neighbourhoods, sanctuary custodians, judges representing recognised roads and a military guardian elected for a stated emergency. Wealthy houses repeatedly extended emergency commands and used marriage to bring foreign money and soldiers into their factions.

### Qoram, Zimra and Haviel

The guardian **Qoram ben Teth** married **Zimra bat Keresh** of an Arqeshite river house during a succession crisis around 842 BR. House Keresh supplied grain credit, metal and soldiers at a time when raids had emptied Qiryath's military stores. Zimra brought her own estate staff and religious household. She secured a court shrine for the portable royal image through which the **Crowned Countenance** had begun to answer Arqeshite royal ceremonies, and appointed its diviners to advise the guardian. Qoram authorised these arrangements and used the alliance to extend his emergency command.

Qoram wanted vineyard terraces beside the eastern wall for a garrison estate. Their keeper, **Tobel bar Yamin**, refused to sell land cultivated by his mother's family. Zimra ordered two household officials to pay false witnesses, who accused Tobel of cursing the guardian and withholding water during mobilisation. Qoram accepted their testimony and confirmed the death sentence. Tobel was executed, and the guardian's household took the terraces. Other families unable to meet the wartime levy lost neighbouring plots through confiscation.

Prophetic teachers confronted these rulers over concrete acts. **Haviel of the Lower Stair** denounced the seizure of vineyard terraces from families unable to meet a wartime levy. The guardian **Qoram ben Teth** spared Haviel but destroyed his household workshop and sold two labourers who had sheltered him. Later court histories cite the spared prophet as evidence of Qoram's tolerance. The Lower Stair recitation preserves the labourers' names.

During the following drought, Zimra's diviners announced that the royal image would bring rain if Qiryath publicly accepted its rites. Haviel challenged them at an abandoned threshing yard outside the city. The oldest Lower Stair account says that a brief white flame consumed Haviel's soaked offering while the court lamps failed, and that rain reached the shelf before the crowd returned to the gate. The flame and rain were genuine signs given for the exposure of the court cult. The fire left no active relic and conferred no continuing power upon the site.

The confrontation ended in killing. The oldest recitation says that Haviel ordered the court diviners seized and killed beside a dry channel, and that he remained there while his followers carried out the sentence. Later Qiryath versions transfer the order to Qoram or omit the deaths. Zimra swore to kill Haviel, while Qoram allowed his soldiers to search the road houses. Haviel fled into the broken country and spent part of the drought dependent upon households belonging to the same river people as Zimra. He returned when an Arqeshite-backed force threatened Qiryath in 835 BR.

During that siege, Haviel ordered the eastern gate opened for families whom Qoram intended to abandon outside the wall. The gate beam had swollen in the rain and resisted the men working its levers. It rose when six women took hold of it, allowing the stranded families to enter. This second sign answered their rescue. Qoram had chosen to leave the families outside; Zimra and Qoram arranged Tobel's death; Haviel ordered the killings beside the dry channel. None of those acts acquired the authority of the signs.

## Arqeshite pressure and the siege of 742 BR

Arqesh first approached Qiryath through tribute, road inspection and demands for skilled service. Some merchants favoured accommodation because their relatives lived in Arqeshite cities. Rural households feared that a siege would consume their fields. Sanctuary factions interpreted any royal image as an unlawful claim over worship.

King **Neshkar II Ubar** demanded priestly hostages and a royal image within the upper sanctuary in 746 BR. The chief guardian **Elior bar Hadan** accepted increased tribute but refused both demands. An opposing councillor, **Maraiah bat Qesem**, argued that hostages might preserve the city. Her supporters opened negotiations without authority, and militants killed an Arqeshite envoy when the talks became known.

The siege lasted through two harvests. Arqeshite engineers diverted a spring channel and raised an earth ramp against the eastern wall. Qiryath's defenders stripped roofs for timber and expelled some foreign residents to conserve grain. Several were killed between the lines. Villagers carried food through burial passages until soldiers found the route and executed captured carriers beside its entrance.

Arqesh entered the city in 742 BR. Soldiers killed defenders and inhabitants in the upper streets, stripped the sanctuary and broke the crown of the tower. Officers selected judges, engineers, metalworkers, musicians, scribes and leading families for deportation. Other captives were divided among estates and military households. Executed defenders were denied burial. The Washers of Nahal Or recovered bodies at night; three were caught, mutilated and displayed at the river road.

Most villages around Qiryath survived the conquest. Arqesh appointed local collaborators, settled military dependants near the ford and collected grain from the remaining farmers. Refugees travelled west and north, while road houses already living beyond Qiryath continued their older circuits.

## Exile in the river cities, 742–690 BR

Deported households were distributed among Arqesh, Nemeru, Khalbar and royal agricultural settlements. Their circumstances differed. Skilled workers could receive rations, housing and limited property while remaining unable to leave. Some entered palace service. Others worked under private masters or canal gangs.

The musician **Sameth bar Yorin** became celebrated at Nemeru for adapting Jirahar lament to the long Arqeshite reed pipe. He refused to perform before Neshkar's image and lost two fingers under punishment. His daughter continued at court and used her position to ransom relatives. Later songs disagree over whether her service represented courage or submission.

Burial fraternities recovered the bodies of executed people and maintained household teaching. Engineers learned Arqeshite surveying and water management. Merchants acquired partners throughout the riverlands. A census compiled to organise grain and labour was later used to identify and seize households suspected of supporting a provincial revolt. Some deportees gained skill, property or influence, but guards, labour assignments and masters still determined where they could live and whether their families remained together.

## Return and Second Qiryath, c. 690–660 BR

Arqesh fell in 690 BR after a succession war divided House Ubar and the palace army. **Arezan I Tiravan** led a coalition of Vashari highland houses, Nemeru boat crews, rebellious Arqeshite governors, Qahuri rulers seeking the return of their eastern districts and Tarkhan leaders attacking the imperial remount stations. Forced workers inside Khalbar opened one of the coalition's routes. Arezan's army seized the eastern gates and the water stations serving the capital.

A second command came from the western sea. **Eryndor III of Ortheia**, the Far-Road King, had crossed with an Atherian-speaking army in 702 BR and taken the coast after a destructive siege at Terys. His agreement with Arezan brought the principal siege army, allied fleet and western treasury into the war. Nemeru boats carried Eryndor's soldiers across a channel believed to be closed. The two rulers entered different quarters of Arqesh and competed for its palace, stores and officials. Their allies shared an enemy while pursuing independence, land, plunder, office or revenge.

Eryndor died at Arqesh in 684 BR. His generals, Arezan and the households of his nominal heirs divided the conquered army and provinces. Their Wars of the Inheritance lasted until about 650 BR. Returning Jirahar therefore travelled through garrisons and districts whose rulers were still changing.

In 684 BR, Arezan issued the **Decree of Roads and Houses**. It allowed displaced communities to restore named sanctuaries and return with property recognised by Vasharan officials. Returned households owed taxes, road service and political obedience to the new empire. The decree weakened surviving Arqeshite military households and reopened neglected routes while denying a general restoration to people displaced by Arezan's own conquest.

Many Jirahar remained in the river cities. Those who returned found fields cultivated by families who had survived the conquest or descended from imperial settlers. The widow **Yaelith bat Coren**, keeper of the principal surviving cistern, married **Neram bar Elior**, an exile official sent to supervise reconstruction. Her neighbours feared the returned houses would dispossess them. His relatives accused him of surrendering ancestral claims through marriage.

Their household organised water and labour for the new sanctuary. When a severe restoration faction later demanded the expulsion of spouses associated with Arqeshite settlers, Yaelith refused to let its judges dismiss her workers' children as foreigners. The resulting agreement recognised families whose members had received public instruction, accepted the community's duties and been admitted before witnesses. Several old houses rejected the ruling and departed.

Second Qiryath was consecrated around 660 BR, while the inheritance wars continued along the coast and lower river. Arezan's government protected the rebuilding because the sanctuary drew trade, supplied interpreters and strengthened a route under Vasharan control. Returned households supplied money and labour; river communities sent craftworkers; villages around the site provided food and stone. The city possessed a larger sanctuary, schools, workshops and underground accommodation. It remained one centre within a dispersed civilisation. The road companies, river quarters and eastern settlements established before and during exile continued to develop.

## Qiryath under Vasharan and Atherian pressure, c. 660–350 BR

The Vasharan kings confirmed Qiryath's sanctuary and internal courts in exchange for taxes, road service and political quiet. Governors intervened in appointments when local factions threatened supply. Jirahar interpreters and relay keepers served the royal roads, while other Jirahar communities suffered collective punishment when governors suspected them of carrying messages or sheltering fugitives.

Wars between Vasharan and Atherian powers divided the dispersed people. Western towers traded with Atherian cities. Qiryath remained within the Vasharan imperial system. Sea companies supplied ports on both sides. Each government suspected the route networks of carrying information for the other.

By 350 BR, Qiryath was a populous pilgrimage and market city under Vasharan supremacy. It governed its sanctuary and communal affairs but possessed no independent kingdom. Jirahar communities stretched from the Glassward roads to Arqeshite river cities and Atherian ports. Their settlements, schools, burial fraternities and trading households had survived the rise and fall of several governments, giving the dispersed people institutions that no single city controlled.

## Principal received traditions

- **The Mother of Measures** remembers Mareh's public division of food and the family left behind.
- **The Lamps beneath the Jars** remembers Tovan's night victory, refused title and murderous heirs.
- **The Braids at Gedran** preserves Hador's strength, appetites, betrayals, mutilation and final mass killing.
- **Four Nights for Seleth** preserves the lethal version of Othan's vow.
- **The Goat-Keeper's Crown** recounts Yedran's rise, Uriad's death and Rava's accusation.
- **The Rain at the Dry Channel** remembers Haviel's contest, the killing of the diviners and his flight.
- **The Six Women at the Gate** remembers the lifting of the rain-swollen gate and the families who escaped the siege.
- **The Fingers of Sameth** concerns artistic achievement and compromise in exile.
- **The Keeper Who Married the Return** remembers Yaelith and Neram through incompatible descendant traditions.

These traditions belong to different centuries and districts. Their continued performance is established; details concerning divine signs, private motives and later embellishment remain matters of interpretation.
