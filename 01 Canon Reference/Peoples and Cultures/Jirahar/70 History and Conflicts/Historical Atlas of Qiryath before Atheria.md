# Historical Atlas of Qiryath before Atheria

The early history of **Qiryath** follows Jirahar communities from the Near-Glass road camps to a populous sanctuary city under Vasharan rule. Between about 2165 and 350 BR, travelling households developed public stores, burial duties and portable judgement; local protectors defended or exploited settlements; and towers gathered workshops, markets and schools around dependable water. Arqesh destroyed the first city in 742 BR. Deportees, surviving villages and road families later rebuilt it without recovering its former sovereignty.

The city's altar gave a sign that could be touched. An accepted offering burned without smoke, and afterwards the stones were cool under the hand. In the first winter of the Arqeshite siege the smoke rose black and the stones were hot. No offering was answered again for nearly ninety years, through the city's fall, the exile and the consecration of the rebuilt sanctuary. The stones went cool at last under an offering carried by mixed households whom a severe faction wanted expelled. Jirahar call those years **the Withholding**, and they remember that it ended.

## Historical setting

Qiryath arose on the **Reqa shelf**, a broken limestone country between the Near-Glass roads and the western approaches to the eastern riverlands. Seasonal streams descended from the shelf into the cultivated valleys below. Three dependable springs, several older cistern fields and a ford across the lower **Ubar River** made the district useful long before it possessed a sacred city.

The country supported villages, orchards, grazing grounds and caravan yards. Its people cultivated barley, lentils, onions and hardy fruit, kept goats and pack animals, made coarse cloth and repaired goods carried between richer countries. Timber remained scarce. Large roofs, siege works and the upper storeys of the first tower therefore depended upon imported beams. Qiryath's later prominence rested upon the labour and food of this surrounding country as much as upon pilgrimage.

The ancestors of the Jirahar entered this inhabited region gradually. They descended principally from the Near-Glass camp continuum, but their households lived beside Orphaned well keepers, river farmers and survivors of former Giant estates. Marriage, adoption and conversion drew some of these neighbours into the developing people. Other communities remained distinct and negotiated with the road houses as trading partners, rivals or landlords.

## The road-house centuries, c. 2165–1750 BR

The Prophet's death left the One Glass People with several necessary offices and no universal human successor. Judges heard disputes between families and camps. Provisioners counted food and decided when a common store could be opened. Captains organised guards and chose how a travelling column would meet an attack. Archivists preserved teachings and the names of the living and the dead. Each office answered a particular need; the Prophet's commission passed to none of them.

The southern leagues departed first. Households remaining near the Glass continued to share camps, marriages and worship for generations. Drought and distance gradually changed this common life. Some communities fortified reliable wells and treated the surrounding ground as a territory. Others occupied several routes and needed a judgement made in one camp to be recognised in another. Their judges began travelling between agreed stopping places, hearing disputes in the presence of household elders and carrying their decisions to the next camp. This practice became an ancestor of the Jirahar travelling bench.

The camps were made up of extended households rather than isolated families. A household might contain married kin, widows, servants, clients, converts and enslaved people. Most grain, goats and pack animals belonged to particular households. Each camp also maintained a common store for guards, sick people and families whose food had failed. Grain borrowed from that store was normally repaid after the next harvest, while the owners of pack animals expected labour or a share of the cargo in return for carrying another family's goods.

The oldest road traditions name **Mareh bat Sivan**, a widow who managed three related camps after a failed harvest and a fever killed their senior provisioners around the eighteenth century BR. She had worked beside the dead provisioners and knew what the store contained. Mareh weighed the household sacks in public, postponed repayment of grain loans until the next harvest and divided the common reserve among the fever tents. She kept the sound pack animals from being slaughtered because they would have to carry water, seed grain and people too weak to walk across the next waterless stretch of road.

An armed captain attempted to take the remaining grain for his followers. Mareh required every person seeking a portion to carry food to the sick before receiving it. When the captain struck her brother, the household elders and camp guards drove him out. Two families followed him and later founded a fortified well community. Mareh's rationing carried the three camps through the fever and preserved enough seed and transport for their departure. The public weighing of every sack gave her the later name **Mother of Measures**.

Her road tradition also remembers the people whom the camps failed. During the departure, the cart of a servant household split its axle. The household comprised a hired driver, his wife, their two children and his widowed mother. The camps had already lost animals and were trying to cross the waterless ground before their remaining supply ran out. After an unsuccessful attempt to repair the cart, the elders ordered the column onward. Some recitations say that Mareh opposed the decision; others say that she gave the deciding order. Raiders found the stranded family and sold them farther east. A later road house paid for two descendants' release, while the others disappeared into the river cities.

Work supplied many households with their public names. The **Bronze-Pan House** repaired cooking vessels and scales. The **Night-Rein House** guided animals between distant water. The **Washers of Nahal Or** prepared bodies and negotiated burial ground. These names passed through kinship, apprenticeship and adoption and could endure after the original occupation changed.

Burial carried unusual importance. The people who named and buried a dead traveller accepted responsibility for mourning, for notifying distant kin and for obligations that the death had left unfinished. Jirahar households carried these duties with them when they moved. Proto-Ahdari well coalitions increasingly based inheritance and authority upon control of the surrounding land. Disputes over whether a grave created a travelling family duty or a claim to the ground helped widen the division between the two peoples. Families continued to cross it by marriage for generations.

## The Bronze Roads, c. 1750–1325 BR

Between the eighteenth and fifteenth centuries BR, the northern routes became part of a broad exchange system. Qahuri grain and linen travelled east. Copper and worked bronze came from several upland and coastal sources. Gateway ships brought salt, timber and foreign cloth. Arqeshite canal cities exported grain, glazed wares and writing materials. Tarkhan carriers supplied hides, animals and knowledge of the open country. Captives moved with the same traffic.

Proto-Jirahar households found work between jurisdictions. They repaired carts, treated animals, translated bargains, sounded river landings and washed the dead. The fair at **Mattan Yard**, held after the first autumn rain, became known for mule races, marriage negotiations and a stew so heavily seasoned that later cooks claimed it could reveal a dishonest guest. Children learned route sequences as cumulative songs. Musicians returning from Arqesh added reed instruments to older frame drums.

Trade passed through a patchwork of royal districts, temple lands, fortified wells and independent towns. The ruling households of palace towns requisitioned animals and drivers. A successful merchant could claim the labour of a debtor's family. Escort companies sold information to raiders. Town guilds hired travelling smiths during shortages and expelled them when permanent workshops recovered. Road elders used coded phrases to protect children and routes, and powerful elders sometimes used the same secrecy to conceal violence within their households.

The **Breaking of the Bronze Roads**, conventionally dated c. 1450–1325 BR, unfolded district by district. Harvests declined in different years. Palace wars closed crossings. Armed sailors seized coastal stores. Refugees travelled beside mercenaries and slave dealers. Royal officials took pack animals faster than breeders could replace them. By the time the principal palace routes failed, many households had already shortened their journeys or abandoned them.

Road-house songs add a stranger cause, which they call **the Reclaiming**. Much of the copper and bronze on the long roads was old metal from the Serpent Kingdom's buried estates, dug out by ruin traders and recast, and some ingots still carried the marks of their first owners. When traders broke into deeper vaults, the old estates woke. Gilded guardians, great coiled processions sliding on runners and the bound dead of the estates came up the ceremonial roads that the Bronze Roads had reused as trunk routes, and they took back whatever bore their marks. Caravans were found stripped of metal, their drivers dead or gone; palace storerooms were opened in the night with the doors left whole. The trunk roads that followed the old ceremonial ways failed first. Short roads between towers survived, because they carried barley, goats and cloth. Jirahar smiths of later centuries asked where a piece of scrap had come from before they would put it in the fire.

Tower settlements expanded as long journeys became dangerous. Stores, cisterns and workshops gathered around visible refuges. Travelling families shortened their circuits and relied upon settled branches. A tower could keep a district alive through a poor season. It could also refuse grain to families unwilling to accept its preferred judge.

## Judges of reed and bronze, c. 1400–1050 BR

Later Jirahar teaching arranges this period around judges and deliverers who lived in different districts and generations. Their stories follow communities that entrusted military, judicial or religious authority to a successful protector and then had to restrain the power they had granted. The shelf's own tellers join three of the cycles into one chain: a deliverer's grandsons became the raiders whom the next cycle drowned, and the water that drowned them passed by blood into the last.

The oldest cycle belongs to **Barakhel of the Reed Cloak**, placed around 1335 BR, in the last years of the Reclaiming, when the trunk roads were failing and armed households lived by raiding the short ones. Barakhel broke one such household by its water. He hired Tarkhan camel riders to hold the three wells on the raiders' road home and let the raiders come on with their plunder until their skins were empty. They surrendered at the wells on his promise of safe conduct, and he executed them there. The victory freed captives and ended several years of raids. His sons kept the wells as a toll station and sold water to the families he had rescued. In dry years they took the price in pledges of grain, animals and children, and within two generations the eastern shelf knew his descendants as the armed **House Sereq**.

By about 1290 BR House Sereq came down after each harvest to collect from three villages, and it penned the pledged children in its camp in a dry wadi below the shelf until their families paid. **Tovan bar Rameq**, a herd boy of about twelve, was on that year's list. His mother lowered him into an abandoned cistern older than the villages and told the collectors he had died of fever, and they took two of his cousins in his place. Road tradition says that on the third night something in the deep water answered his name and offered him a bargain: the shelf's streams would run where he named for as long as his line went down at the first autumn rain and called each stream by its name.

Tovan named the Sereq camp. That night the seasonal stream came down the dry wadi months out of season and took the camp, its animals and its pens. The pledged children drowned with their captors, his cousins among them. Raiders who crawled out of the mud asking for mercy were killed by villagers with threshing flails, and the winter stores were dug out of the silt. Some recitations say Tovan knew the children were in the pens and judged them already sold; others say he named only the raiders and the water could not tell one from another.

The families of the drowned children brought Tovan before the road judges for blood-price. The judges found that he owed the three villages more lives than a herdsman could pay, and they laid the debt on the gift. He and his line would call the streams for the villages every year and take their bread for it, and his house would hold no office, lead no levy and judge no case, because the Prophet's people founded nothing on a bargain. Tovan lived out his life as a debtor fed by his creditors. The water kept a stricter law than the judges. The shelf villages reckoned a household's line through its mothers, and when Tovan died the streams answered the children of his sister **Imra** and ignored his sons, who had expected to inherit the villages' bread. At the next first rain they seized Imra's youngest son and held him on the old threshing floor, meaning to make him call the water in their name. He was out of the cistern when the rain came. The stream left its bed in the dark and came down across the threshing floor, and it drowned the boy and four of Tovan's sons. Imra's daughter went down at the following rain. Her descendants kept the rite for more than four centuries, fed by the villages and obeyed by no one, and the shelf proverb *feed the cistern house; do not follow it* comes from them.

**Tirzah bat Oren**, an animal physician from the Night-Rein House, became a judge after diagnosing deliberate poisoning at a chain of cisterns around 1210 BR. The armed household paid to defend the wells blamed foreign drovers and prepared to seize their children as hostages. Tirzah demonstrated that the poison came from an allied orchard owner who expected to claim the grazing land after the drovers fled. She then helped organise the pursuit when the owner withdrew behind armed retainers. The fighting killed two of her brothers and several field workers who had taken no part in the poisoning. The drovers recovered their surviving animals, buried the dead and left the district.

The longest cycle belongs to the orchard villages under the toll fortress of **Gedran**, which weighed every cart going down to the river markets on scales the villages knew were false. Its weigher, **Dovan**, kept a set of honest stones under the weigh-house floor and gave the orchard carts true weight whenever the lords' steward was away. His daughter **Nareh bat Dovan** kept his tallies. She was courted by **Hador bar Mavin**, a drover's son of great size and strength, whom the songs remember for his appetite, pride and anger, and who had already killed toll guards on the road below the fortress in a quarrel over his father's mules.

Around 1171 BR Gedran's lords found Dovan out. They laid him on the pan of his own scale and loaded the fortress weights onto him until he died, and they shut Nareh in the grain pit beneath the store-house to be sold with the autumn caravans. Hador came by night with a band of drovers and fired the store-house to draw off the guards. The fire took the labourers' huts under the wall, and families burned in them. Nareh came out of the pit in the confusion carrying her father's stones in her apron. Hador's songs say he freed her. The orchard lament says she walked out by herself while he was busy at the huts.

Hador then made a private war on Gedran, killing guards, labourers and villagers, and Gedran answered by closing its road. No orchard fruit went down to the river and no grain came up. Nareh carried the honest stones from village to village through both hungry springs and weighed each household's old toll tallies against them, so that every family learned by how much it had been robbed and in which years. In the second spring the elders of the orchard villages declared Hador unhoused, so that no household might feed or shelter him, and they had Nareh speak the sentence, because the villages trusted nobody else's measure. Gedran's lords had lost more guards to him than they could replace, and they offered him the captaincy of the fortress gate. He took it. For forty years the drover who had fought Gedran weighed the orchard carts on its scales. The villagers his band had taken prisoner during the war were sold east to clear the fortress's debts, and many died on the winter roads. Later heroic songs count only the guards he killed in his youth. The orchard lament names Dovan, the families in the huts and the prisoners Hador sold.

**Othan bar Raqel** commanded a coalition of the orchard and shelf villages against Gedran around 1128 BR. To bring the harvest in before the campaign he swore a harvest truce with the fortress and gave sureties for it, by the road custom that a surety's life was forfeit if her side broke the peace. Gedran's lords asked for his young daughter **Seleth** by name. Her mother was a daughter of the cistern house, and a girl of that line inside the fortress was a hold on the shelf's water as well as on her father. Nareh, by then an old woman who weighed for every village and judged for none, begged Othan to send a judge in the child's place. He sent Seleth, with two children of the coalition's elders.

Late in the harvest a herd boy brought word that Gedran's gate stood half-manned on the night of the fortress's harvest feast. Othan refused to ransom the sureties first, because a ransom would warn the fortress, and he attacked before the truce ran out. Gedran's gate captain was Hador, an old man now, and he kept the custom as far as it paid him. He killed the two elders' children at the gate. He took Seleth out by the postern while the gate was falling and went down the river road with her, as he had once sent his prisoners. Neither was seen on the shelf again. Gedran's tolls ended that night. Nareh had her father's stones set into the fallen gateway, where carts were weighed free for as long as the road was used, and Qiryath's market weights were called Nareh's stones until the first city fell.

Othan's house later said that Seleth had been ransomed in secret and had died honoured among the river people. Gedran's descendants said the captain killed all three, as custom required. Women in several Reqa villages keep a harsher account, in which Othan knew what the river houses would pay for a girl of the cistern line and counted her into the price of the fortress. The cistern house struck his name from its first-rain song. Since that harvest no Reqa village will accept a commander's child as surety, and a commander who swears a truce stands surety himself.

Repeated raids and the slow assembly of local councils encouraged settlements to retain successful protectors for longer terms. A captain with loyal followers could recover captives and guard harvests after a travelling judge had departed; the same captain could massacre surrendered enemies, take service with the fortress he had fought or turn rescued villages into hereditary clients.

## The rise of First Qiryath, c. 1050–800 BR

Qiryath grew around the largest of the Reqa refuges. Its earliest monumental works were practical: a deep communal cistern, grain rooms, animal courts and a paved yard where several travelling benches could sit at once. The sanctuary developed beside them. It housed readings associated with the Prophet's road, objects carried from older camps and an altar served by several custodial families.

The altar gave the city its reach. At the appointed festival offerings, an offering that was accepted burned without smoke, and when the fire was out the stones were cool to the hand. Elders of three old custodial houses laid their palms on the stone in front of the crowd, and their names were written beside the day. The custodians taught that the sign answered the appointed offering itself. Pilgrims read it as a verdict on whatever the city was doing that season, and judgements given in the paved yard on a day of cool stones carried an authority no road court could equal.

**Amariah bat Qoreb**, remembered as the first Keeper of the Upper Room, brought rival calendars into a common sequence around 1020 BR. Her calendar fixed the weeks of common pilgrimage while allowing remote towers to begin agricultural observances according to local conditions. Large gatherings became easier to provision and Qiryath's teachers gained a wider audience. Its custodians also acquired influence over market dates and the public standing of communities accused of observing the wrong week.

Permanent neighbourhoods spread below the tower and along the slope. Gardeners used carefully rationed spring water. Potters and metalworkers occupied the smoke quarter. Porters lived near the caravan yard. Foreign merchants maintained guest houses. The surrounding villages supplied grain, oil, fruit, animals and labour. These permanent workshops, markets, councils and waterworks made Qiryath the first large Jirahar city.

Qiryath's authority remained uneven. Some towers accepted its teachers but rejected its tax collectors. Road houses feared that the custodians of sacred objects would also claim their property. The councils answered military danger by granting longer commands to protector families, and by custom the guardian of the walls carried the city's offering up the sanctuary stair at each appointed festival. When the elders laid their hands on cool stones, the crowd in the paved yard cheered the guardian standing beside them. The custodians taught that the sign answered the offering; soldiers and pilgrims heard a command renewed. The stones were cool at every appointed offering anyone could remember, so a guardian who carried the basket could be confirmed festival after festival, and an emergency command became a reign. Within several generations the chief protector commanded soldiers, collected grain and arranged alliances in the manner of a king, although later sources disagree over the title.

## Yedran at the stair

The most successful protector house was **House Oram**, which was also one of the three custodial houses. For three generations an Oram carried the basket up the stair and an Oram elder laid a hand on the stone. The house ruled through the city's grain as well as its altar, and each of its senior men held one of the grain rooms with his own guards. In a run of dry years around 962 BR the keeper of the lower rooms, a brother of the guardian **Shaqel Oram**, let his guards take women who came to draw their households' portions. In the next hungry month the women of the lower town broke down the lower rooms' door and killed the keeper on its threshold. Shaqel hanged four of them at the gate. The lower town went on climbing to the festivals and cheering beside the cool stones, because the stones belonged to the whole city, and it waited.

**Yedran bar Aqev** was born around 990 BR into a lower-town household of altar wood-carriers. The Reqa shelf had little timber, and the wood-carriers bought split cedar and tamarisk in the river cities and carried it up the long road for the altar fire. Around 953 BR Shaqel collapsed with fever on the sanctuary stair at the autumn festival with the offering basket in his arms. Yedran, climbing behind him with the last load of wood, caught the basket before it reached the steps. An appointed offering could not be halted once it had started up the stair, and the custodians let the wood-carrier carry it on. It burned without smoke. When the three elders laid their hands on the stone and found it cool, the crowd cheered the man standing beside it, and the lower town carried him to the council on its shoulders. Shaqel's sons were away at the river posts and raiders were on the Ubar road. The council looked at the crowd in the paved yard and named the wood-carrier **Guardian of Qiryath** for the season's emergency. Songs written two centuries later call him king. Road-house accounts insist that he was only the strongest protector recognised by a frightened city.

Shaqel lived. The fever left him unable to climb, and for thirteen years the old guardian sat in the upper house with his custodial seat and his sons' spearmen while the new one carried the basket. Yedran opened the lower grain rooms at harvest price, restored cisterns, admitted foreign artisans and established a regular grain contribution for defence. He carried the offering at every appointed festival, and the stones were cool every time. He could never be away from the city on a festival day, and the river raiders learned the calendar.

Shaqel died in the winter of about 941 BR. His sons marched their spearmen up from the river posts to bury him, and at the spring festival they came to the foot of the stair to claim the basket as their house's inheritance. They had left a handful of men at the Ubar ford. Raiders took it that week, killed the garrison and carried off the villagers behind it for sale. On the stair the Oram spearmen met the lower town with the offering between them. Neither side could stop the offering, and neither would let the other carry it. People were crushed against the stair wall and thrown from the steps. The basket went up over the heads of the crowd from hand to hand, and the last hands belonged to **Hanel bar Sem**, a lamp-filler of the sanctuary, who carried it to the altar with his ribs broken and died three days later. The offering burned without smoke. The three elders laid their hands on the stone and found it cool.

At the autumn festival Hanel's widow, **Rava bat Horel**, climbed the stair ahead of the offering and asked the three custodial elders, in front of the crowd, whom the stones had answered in the spring: the house of Oram, the wood-carrier, or her husband. The eldest custodian was **Mikalet bat Oram**, Shaqel's daughter and the sister of the men who had fought for the basket. She answered that the stones had only ever answered the offering. Yedran's partisans said she spoke to unseat the wood-carrier; her brothers said she had sold her own house; the custodians said she had answered as they had always taught. From that festival the custodial houses chose the offering's bearer by lot from the households of the city, and the guardian stood in the yard with everyone else.

Neither claim outlived the answer. The council let Yedran's command lapse when the raiding season ended, and he went back to the wood road. Road-house accounts have him die an old man in a river timber-yard, still arguing the price of cedar; the royalist songs say Oram men killed him on the road within the year. Shaqel's sons held the grain rooms a little longer. Village councils and road houses stopped sending grain to the upper house and fed the city's quarters directly, and when the rooms were empty the spearmen went back to the river posts. House Oram kept its custodial seat and never held the walls again. Later royalists remember Yedran as the founder of civic order. Rava's lament ends with the offering burning clean on the altar and a lamp-filler dying at the foot of it, and it asks the crowd which of the two the city had cheered.

## Councils, prophets and the last protector houses

Qiryath's government after Rava's question combined a council of neighbourhoods, sanctuary custodians, judges representing recognised roads and a military guardian elected for a stated emergency. The bearer of each appointed offering was chosen by lot. Wealthy houses repeatedly extended emergency commands and used marriage to bring foreign money and soldiers into their factions.

### Qoram, Zimra and Haviel

The guardian **Qoram ben Teth** married **Zimra bat Keresh** of an Arqeshite river house during a succession crisis around 829 BR. House Keresh supplied grain credit, metal and soldiers at a time when raids had emptied Qiryath's military stores, and it took shelf terraces in pledge. Qoram raised a wartime levy to service the debt. Families unable to meet it lost their terraces to the guardian's garrison estate or to Keresh's stewards. The three villages of the eastern shelf, stripped by the levy, stopped sending bread to the cistern house. Its last woman, old and childless, went down to kin in the river towns and died there, and at the first rain of the next autumn nobody went down the cistern.

The streams went astray. For four autumns the rain fell on the shelf and the water ran down the wrong wadis, past the terraces and cisterns it had fed since Tovan's day, and orchards died within sight of flooded scrub. Zimra had brought her own estate staff and religious household to Qiryath. She secured a court shrine for the portable royal image through which the **Crowned Countenance** had begun to answer Arqeshite royal ceremonies, and appointed its diviners to advise the guardian. The diviners announced that the image would bring the streams home if Qiryath received it at the altar. Meanwhile they drew water every morning from the lower town's cistern to wash it, and the lower town's ration fell to a cup a day.

They asked that the image attend the autumn offering of about 826, standing on the stair below the altar as river kings stood at their own festivals. Qoram argued from Mikalet's answer: if the stones answered only the offering, an image on the stair could change nothing. The elder of the third custodial house, **Tobel bar Yamin**, refused to lay his hand on a stone with an image beneath it, and a day could be written only over the witness-staffs of all three houses. Qoram's soldiers came to Tobel's house for its staff. His wife and his brother held the door while he went out over the roofs with the staff under his cloak, and the soldiers killed them both on the threshold. Qoram gave the house's seat to a cousin who would climb. At the festival the image stood on the stair, the offering burned without smoke and three hands found the stones cool, one of them without a staff. The custodians' roll gives that day two names and a blank. Zimra's diviners proclaimed in the paved yard that heaven had received the river king's image beside its altar, and the news went down the river road. The streams stayed where they were.

Prophetic teachers confronted these rulers over concrete acts. **Haviel of the Lower Stair** denounced the killing at Tobel's door and the seizure of the terraces, and taught that the stones had answered the offering and had answered neither the image nor Qoram. Qoram spared Haviel but destroyed his household workshop and sold two labourers who had sheltered him. Later court histories cite the spared prophet as evidence of Qoram's tolerance. The Lower Stair recitation preserves the labourers' names.

The water came back through Zimra. River houses bought bargains as other houses bought land, and House Keresh had kept one for three hundred years: a line of daughters descended from a shelf girl sold down the river road in the year Gedran fell, married back into the house in every generation, each taught a song of stream-names that none of them had ever used. In the fourth dry autumn, around 824, Zimra went up to the eastern shelf with two servants. She found Tobel living in the empty cistern house with his staff. At the first rain she went down, and the streams answered her. They ran where the song sent them, to the beds they had kept in Seleth's day. Orchards abandoned for generations flooded, and terraces cut since then stayed dry until their villages paid Keresh's stewards to have a new name added to the song.

The road judges came to the cistern house and asked Tobel what he had seen. He told them that she went down, she called the names and the water came. They ruled as their predecessors had ruled for Tovan's house: the villages should feed her for the rite and follow her in nothing. Qoram's court counted the shelf's water as his wife's dowry all the same, and the diviners proclaimed in the paved yard that the Countenance had brought the streams home.

Haviel answered the proclamation. He led the lower town's water-carriers into the image-house, where they seized the diviners at their basins and, on Haviel's word, threw them down the shaft of the cistern they had drained for four summers. It was filling again, and Haviel stood at its mouth until it was done. Later Qiryath versions transfer the order to the water-carriers or omit the deaths. The villages had their water back and did not rise for him. The road judges heard the case on witnesses and condemned Haviel for killing without trial. They declared him unhoused, so that no household within the walls might feed him, and half his followers left him. He lived outside the walls among the Washers of Nahal Or and the families of the burial caves. Tobel refused to return to a city with an image-house in it and died at the cistern house, which Zimra's daughters kept after her.

Around 817 BR an Arqeshite-backed force threatened Qiryath, and Qoram closed the gates on the families living outside them. Haviel was among them, and so was Tobel's granddaughter, carrying the third house's staff. Haviel led them to the eastern gate and demanded that it be opened. The gate beam had swollen in the autumn rains and resisted the men working its levers. It rose when six women took hold of it, and the stranded families came in. The Lower Stair recitation counts the lifting of the gate as a sign. Haviel's followers have argued ever since over whether it answered the families' rescue or pardoned him.

## Arqeshite pressure and the siege of 742 BR

Arqesh first approached Qiryath through tribute, road inspection and demands for skilled service. Some merchants favoured accommodation because their relatives lived in Arqeshite cities. Rural households feared that a siege would consume their fields. Sanctuary factions interpreted any royal image as an unlawful claim over worship.

The quarrel was also about where the river provinces took their disputes. Canal districts had begun carrying quarrels over water, debt and boundary stones up the road to Qiryath, to be settled at the festival offering, because a judgement given over cool stones felt beyond appeal. Every case heard in the paved yard was a case the river-king did not hear. River courts also remembered the autumn of Qoram's image, when an Arqeshite royal image had stood on the sanctuary stair and the offering had burned without smoke. Jirahar teachers later said that King **Neshkar II Ubar** chose a demand he could not lose: if the sign went on answering beside his image, as it had once before, his kingship shared the altar's authority; if it stopped, the rival court was broken. His sister **Iltara Ubar**, who governed Khalbar, is remembered as urging him to buy Qiryath's merchants instead.

Neshkar demanded priestly hostages and a royal image within the upper sanctuary in 746 BR. The chief guardian **Elior bar Hadan**, whose custodians still kept a roll with a blank in it for that morning, accepted increased tribute but refused both demands. An opposing councillor, **Maraiah bat Qesem**, argued that hostages might preserve the city. Her supporters opened negotiations without authority, and militants killed an Arqeshite envoy when the talks became known.

The siege lasted through two harvests. Arqeshite engineers raised an earth ramp against the eastern wall, and Qiryath's defenders stripped roofs for timber. A Keresh woman of Zimra's line came up from Khalbar with the army. At the first rain of the siege she went down the old cistern on the eastern shelf, and the streams ran past the city's cisterns and into the besiegers' camp. In that first winter the council moved to save what the city still held. It declared the foreign residents unhoused: the canal-country litigants, the guest-house merchants and their families, whom no household within the walls might now feed or shelter. The besiegers would not pass them through the lines. They died through the winter in the caravan yard and on the steps of the houses they had rented, in sight of the sanctuary stair, and the Washers of Nahal Or carried them to the burial caves.

At the next appointed offering the smoke rose black over the sanctuary, and when the three elders laid their hands on the altar the stones were hot. No one living had seen it. The council split. Maraiah's party said heaven had answered the envoy's murder and gained hearers in every quarter. The burial fraternities, who had carried the unhoused out of the caravan yard, said it answered them. Elior's followers said it foretold a destruction already decided and called for the walls to be held. Households began slipping out by night through the burial passages. Villagers carried food in by the same passages until soldiers found the route and executed captured carriers beside its entrance. Every appointed offering for the rest of the siege smoked.

Arqesh entered the city in 742 BR. Soldiers killed defenders and inhabitants in the upper streets, stripped the sanctuary and broke the crown of the tower. Officers selected judges, engineers, metalworkers, musicians, scribes and leading families for deportation. Other captives were divided among estates and military households. Executed defenders were denied burial. The Washers of Nahal Or recovered bodies at night; three were caught, mutilated and displayed at the river road.

Most villages around Qiryath survived the conquest. Arqesh appointed local collaborators, settled military dependants near the ford and collected grain from the remaining farmers. Refugees travelled west and north, while road houses already living beyond Qiryath continued their older circuits.

## Exile in the river cities, 742–690 BR

Deported households were distributed among Arqesh, Nemeru, Khalbar and royal agricultural settlements. Their circumstances differed. Skilled workers could receive rations, housing and limited property while remaining unable to leave. Some entered palace service. Others worked under private masters or canal gangs.

Burial fraternities recovered the bodies of executed people and maintained household teaching. Engineers learned Arqeshite surveying and water management. Merchants acquired partners throughout the riverlands. A census compiled to organise grain and labour was later used to identify and seize households suspected of supporting a provincial revolt. Some deportees gained skill, property or influence, but guards, labour assignments and masters still determined where they could live and whether their families remained together.

The exiles learned to live without the sign. Their judges ruled on witnesses alone and never claimed to speak with the altar's voice. They prayed facing the ruined city. At each appointed festival, when no offering could be made, an elder wrote one line in the **Book of the Unanswered Years**: the year, and the words "no answer". Teachers in the canal settlements said the sign would come back to an altar and never to a king. Children born in exile learned their letters from its lengthening column.

The musician **Sameth bar Yorin** lost two fingers of his left hand to a sluice winch in a Nemeru canal gang, and the overseers sent him back to the winch when the stumps had closed. He worked out a new fingering for the long Arqeshite reed pipe with the hand he had left. Nemeru's taverns, and then its court, paid to hear Jirahar lament played on it, and the tune the deportees knew best was his setting of the Book's empty lines. His daughter sang at Neshkar's court and used her position to ransom relatives. Later songs disagree over whether her service represented courage or submission.

## Return and Second Qiryath, c. 690–654 BR

Arqesh fell in 690 BR after a succession war divided House Ubar and the palace army. **Arezan I Tiravan** led a coalition of Vashari highland houses, rebellious Arqeshite governors, Qahuri rulers seeking the return of their eastern districts and Tarkhan leaders attacking the imperial remount stations. Forced workers inside Khalbar opened one of the coalition's routes. Arezan's army seized the eastern gates and the water stations serving the capital.

A second command came from the western sea. **Eryndor III of Ortheia**, the Far-Road King, had crossed with an Atherian-speaking army in 702 BR and taken the coast, leaving his fleet to blockade Terys. His agreement with Arezan brought the principal siege army, allied fleet and western treasury into the war. The capital's dredging gangs and deportee quarters, Jirahar households among them, rose when Kassur cut their rations and opened the gates; deported Terys families in the Nemeru yards carried Eryndor's soldiers across a channel believed to be closed. Both armies set the gangs back to work under guard, and the return waited six years for Arezan's decree. The two rulers entered different quarters of Arqesh and competed for its palace, stores and officials. Their allies shared an enemy while pursuing independence, land, plunder, office or revenge.

Eryndor died in 684 BR aboard ship at Nemeru, below Arqesh, as he made ready to sail home. His generals and Arezan divided the conquered army and provinces. Their Wars of the Inheritance lasted until about 650 BR. Returning Jirahar therefore travelled through garrisons and districts whose rulers were still changing.

Slave dealers made one stretch of the eastern road between the river cities and the shelf into a trap for returning families, with way-stations that sold the guests they sheltered. Road-house tradition remembers the **Choir at the Eastern Road**. Drovers saw winged shapes standing across the road at dusk, and from that night no caravan could pass along it: animals stopped and would not be driven, and men who went on alone came back unable to say what had turned them. An ordinary caravan carried the last freed captives round by the hill tracks. The dealers lost their stations. Traders who later reopened stretches of the road died on it, and Jirahar caravans kept to the hill tracks for generations.

In 684 BR, Arezan issued the **Decree of Roads and Houses**. It allowed displaced communities to restore named sanctuaries and return with property recognised by Vasharan officials. Returned households owed taxes, road service and political obedience to the new empire. The decree weakened surviving Arqeshite military households and reopened neglected routes while denying a general restoration to people displaced by Arezan's own conquest.

Arezan also paid for stone. His first grants and road labour raised the sanctuary's platform and its lower courses. Qiryath's sign was the one answer in the east that anyone could put a hand on, and Vasharan inscriptions already claimed that the Right Order had chosen Arezan to correct the Lie of Arqesh. If the stones went cool in a sanctuary rebuilt by the crown, every province would know whose side heaven had taken. If they did not, the crown had still bought a road, a market and a city of interpreters. Then the Wars of the Inheritance took the road gangs to the coast, and under his son **Daryav I** the grant lapsed with the walls at shoulder height. Jirahar teachers said afterwards that the house of Tiravan had wagered on the altar and stopped paying before the roof.

Many Jirahar remained in the river cities. Those who returned found fields cultivated by families who had survived the conquest or descended from imperial settlers. The widow **Yaelith bat Coren**, keeper of the principal surviving cistern, married **Neram bar Elior**, an exile official sent to supervise reconstruction. Her neighbours feared the returned houses would dispossess them. His relatives accused him of surrendering ancestral claims through marriage. Their household organised water and labour for the new sanctuary, and much of that labour was done by the spouses and children of Arqeshite settlers.

Second Qiryath was consecrated around 660 BR, while the inheritance wars continued along the coast and lower river. The city finished the building itself. Returned households supplied money and labour; river communities sent craftworkers; villages around the site provided food and stone. Daryav confirmed the sanctuary's estate and sent an offering, and the consecration offering was carried up in the king's name before Vasharan officers and the returned houses. It smoked, and the stones were hot. Another line went into the Book of the Unanswered Years.

The severe restoration faction blamed the continuing silence on the settler households and demanded their expulsion. Yaelith refused to let its judges dismiss her workers' children as foreigners. The resulting agreement recognised families whose members had received public instruction, accepted the community's duties and been admitted before witnesses. Several old houses rejected the ruling and left the city.

Around 654 BR, at the next appointed festival, the households the severe faction had wanted gone carried the offering up the stair. It burned without smoke, and the stones were cool under the elders' hands. The Book records the order without comment: the crown's offering unanswered, the households' offering answered. The departing houses said the witnesses at the altar were Neram's kin and could not be trusted. Vasharan inscriptions credited the king. Yaelith's admission rules held.

The Vasharan crown protected the rebuilt city because the sanctuary drew trade, supplied interpreters and strengthened a route under its control. The city possessed a larger sanctuary, schools, workshops and underground accommodation. It remained one centre within a dispersed civilisation. The road companies, river quarters and eastern settlements established before and during exile continued to develop.

## Qiryath under Vasharan and Atherian pressure, c. 660–350 BR

The Vasharan kings confirmed Qiryath's sanctuary and internal courts in exchange for taxes, road service and political quiet. Governors intervened in appointments when local factions threatened supply. After the cool stones, the sign answered the appointed offerings of a city that had no king, under a crown it had not answered. Governors could confirm a Keeper and count the pilgrim dues; none could cool the altar, and every disputed appointment came to be argued over what the three elders felt under their hands. Jirahar interpreters and relay keepers served the royal roads, while other Jirahar communities suffered collective punishment when governors suspected them of carrying messages or sheltering fugitives.

Wars between Vasharan and Atherian powers divided the dispersed people. Western towers traded with Atherian cities. Qiryath remained within the Vasharan imperial system. Sea companies supplied ports on both sides. Each government suspected the route networks of carrying information for the other.

By 350 BR, Qiryath was a populous pilgrimage and market city under Vasharan supremacy. It governed its sanctuary and communal affairs but possessed no independent kingdom. Jirahar communities stretched from the Glassward roads to Arqeshite river cities and Atherian ports. Their settlements, schools, burial fraternities and trading households had survived the rise and fall of several governments, giving the dispersed people institutions that no single city controlled.

## Principal received traditions

- **The Mother of Measures** remembers Mareh's public division of food and the family left behind.
- **The Three Wells** remembers Barakhel's water trap, the men he executed after promising them the road and the toll house his sons made of the victory.
- **The Water in the Wrong Season** remembers Tovan's bargain, the drowned pens, the judges' sentence and the sons who drowned on the threshing floor.
- **The Smoke over Gedran** is Hador's own song and stops with his youth. **Nareh's Stones**, the orchard lament, preserves Dovan's death under the weights, the families burned in the huts, the unhousing and the prisoners Hador sold as Gedran's gate captain.
- **The Girl at the Postern** keeps three accounts of Seleth: ransomed, killed, or counted by her father into the price of the fortress.
- **The Wood-Carrier at the Stair** recounts Yedran's rise, the fight for the basket, Hanel's death and Rava's question.
- **Tobel's Door** remembers the two people killed for a witness-staff and the morning the image stood on the stair.
- **The Queen at the Cistern** remembers the wandering streams, the image's lustration water, Zimra's descent, the killing of the diviners and Haviel's banishment.
- **The Six Women at the Gate** remembers the lifting of the rain-swollen gate and the families who escaped the siege.
- **The Three-Fingered Pipe** concerns Sameth's music, his daughter's court service and compromise in exile.
- **The Choir at the Eastern Road** remembers the closed slavers' road and the caravan that carried the last captives round it.
- **The Keeper Who Married the Return** remembers Yaelith and Neram through incompatible descendant traditions.
- **The Book of the Unanswered Years** preserves the exile's empty lines and the morning the stones went cool. Jirahar keep the name *the Withholding* for those years and speak of *the Silence* for the sign's ending after the Redeemer's death. Those who wait for acceptance to return to the Empty City cite the cool stones of the second city as proof that a withholding can end.

These traditions belong to different centuries and districts.
