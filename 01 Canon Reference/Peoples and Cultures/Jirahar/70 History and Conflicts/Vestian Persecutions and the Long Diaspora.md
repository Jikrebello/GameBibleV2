# Vestian Persecutions and the Long Diaspora

The Vestian persecutions of 377–398 AR turned intermittent hostility towards the Jirahar into an imperial programme of restriction, confiscation, expulsion and destruction. Court officials and churchmen used the guilt of Qiryath's ancient council to accuse an entire people. When the lamps at Vestian's compulsory professions of 385 failed to kindle, his court blamed hidden rites and "Jirahar blindness", and debtors and neighbouring landholders profited from the searches and seizures that followed. Most surviving eastern towers were destroyed or taken, Qiryath was emptied under a charter that forbids rebuilding "before the Mesakha", and the refugees enlarged the road, sea and host-city communities that became the foundation of the long Jirahar diaspora.

## Before Vestian

The destruction of 72 AR had ended Qiryath's charter government and driven many displaced families beyond direct Atherian rule. It had also left every Jirahar household in the empire on the rolls of the Litter Debt, charged by the head for the sanctuary vessels that had gone through Qiryath's gate before the city fell. Older diaspora communities received them. Other Jirahar remained in imperial towns, surviving towers and the diminished settlement at Qiryath. During the Underground Centuries, Jirahar and Redeemer congregations sometimes sheltered one another and sometimes denounced one another. Local violence existed, and imperial policy remained inconsistent.

Some Redeemer congregations were themselves largely of Jirahar descent, especially in Sermora's harbours and the gateway ports such as Qabir. Their families had joined the faith under the first Qiryath agreement and kept their mothers' registers beside their baptism rolls. Several still prayed in the Jirahar dialect.

Under Astrion, Jirahar petitioners sought access to the sanctuary precinct and graves, repairs to the cisterns and relief from the debt. The survey works of 349–350 AR listed who kept each cistern and who held each house and field. They stopped when wardens of the Red Star came to load the old galleries and the Jirahar crews, who knew what that rite had done at Shahar and Qiryath, put down their tools. The garrison flogged forty of them. The lists, the petitions and the crews' claims for wages survived in the provincial records that Vestian later inherited. ## The yellow lamps

Vestian made the Redeemer's faith compulsory in 385. Every officeholder, and then every household head in the directly governed provinces, had to profess the faith before recognised clergy and light a reception lamp from a church flame. The chancery promised the towns that the lamps would kindle white, as lamps sometimes did at willing baptisms, and show the Flame confirming the realm.

In square after square the lamps burned yellow, while willing baptisms in the parish churches went on kindling as before. In the gateway ports the congregations of Jirahar descent said in public that they had heard this silence before. Their families had argued for nearly four centuries over the sign of acceptance that ceased at Qiryath while the sacrifices went on; they knew how to say that a rite had been performed and its answer had not come. They called the failure of the lamps the **Second Silence**. Vestian's court heard its own worship compared to a rejected sacrifice, and called the comparison Jirahar slander.

The court needed a culprit, and found two. Its preachers blamed Star images hidden in cupboards and what they called "Jirahar blindness" in the descendants of Ezrahel's council. About 387 Vestian banned household rites. Officers with warrants to search homes for forbidden images used the old rolls of the Litter Debt to find Jirahar households, and Astrion's survey lists and petitions to find the people who kept the cisterns and held houses and fields in the eastern towns. Governors treated descent as evidence even against the baptised. A register kept to prove a child's mother could also tell an officer which door to break.

## Raveth

When the bishops' courts refused to hear sworn accounts of the unkindled professions, baptised families of Jirahar descent lodged copies in the Jirahar hearing house at Raveth. Its clerks took each account in before witnesses and shelved it beside the town's leases and debt records. One account followed a single lamp that burned yellow at a compulsory profession and kindled weeks later at a willing baptism in the same parish. Another set the baptismal rolls beside the governor's summonses, showing families searched as suspect after their professions had been accepted.

About 389 a crowd burned the hearing house. Some of the crowd wanted their rent and debt records gone; the clergy who rallied it wanted the sworn accounts destroyed. The governor promised an inquiry and compensation, and Jirahar leaseholders walked in from the villages with whatever receipts the fire had spared. Allied bishops at Caleran argued that rebuilding the house would restore a rival record of the Profession. Vestian withdrew the rebuilding order and let the inquiry lapse. The clerks carried what survived along the roads they used for marriage contracts and appeals, and some of it reached Qabir. [[The Conversion of the Empire]] · [[Amaryah the Fire-Bearer]]

## From Riot to Policy

Once the Redeemer's faith had imperial power, theologians and courtiers had turned the deed of Ezrahel's council into an inherited accusation. Under Vestian it met other appetites: neighbours who wanted tower fields, debtors who wanted their debts burned, and preachers who thought the Redeemer's return was near and wanted the empire clean for it. His reign turned all of them into policy.

From about 388 his court called in the charters of the eastern towers and let governors refuse to renew them. A tower that wanted to keep its charter had to open its doors to inspectors and give up most of its own courts, and a tower that refused, or missed a date, lost its common property to the officers. The treasury took tower stores for the garrisons, neighbours took fields, and some church foundations received rents. How much was taken depended on local alliances, exemptions and the willingness of provincial officers to honour older protections. Between about 388 and 396 most surviving eastern towers were seized or razed. Children, scrolls, tools and the dead moved by mule and boat along road and sea routes built long before the crisis. Enslaved people and dependants held against debts were among those least able to leave.

## The Empty Charter

About 396 Vestian ordered the last inhabitants of Qiryath removed. The garrison shut the entrances and recorded which homes, waterworks and plots had been vacated. Jirahar petitioners secured limited access by undertaking to tend graves, clear the paths and receive pilgrims; their families had to live elsewhere.

The chancery's draft forbade habitation and rebuilding "in perpetuity". Vestian struck the phrase out and wrote in his own hand *before the Mesakha*, using the Jirahar word. Courtiers said he expected the Redeemer's return and wanted the city kept empty for it. Caleran's canonists after his death read the clause as "never": the Mesakha had come, and his coming again would end history. The custodians at the gate read it as a condition in their persecutor's handwriting, and said it aloud at the gate stair. Jirahar jurists gloss it as "before recognition of the Mesakha". The charter does not say who recognises him. [[Qiryath Reqa]] · [[Mesakha Traditions]]

## The Long Diaspora

Refugees established or enlarged tower communities, host-city quarters, Sea Fleets and Turning Companies. Some households secured safe conduct to Vashar or the independent dry-margin towns through kin who had left after 72 AR. Others entered port quarters or remained under local protections within the empire. A few departures used fixed Way-Gates with the agreement of the receiving community; Qiryath's gate stayed shut. A host community needed to know whom it owed shelter, so refugees carried their mothers' lines, their leases and their burial rights written out. Charters became the stuff of diaspora politics. The mothers' registers held scattered households together as one people, and the conversion courts kept that people open to newcomers by choice as well as by birth.

## Inherited Wound

Church cultures remember the Jirahar through Isfir, suspicion, and collective blame. The Jirahar remember that a persecuted faith became an imperial persecutor, and that its lamps went yellow while it searched their houses. Sincere repentance and genuine protection exist alongside the legal and liturgical inheritance of the violence.
