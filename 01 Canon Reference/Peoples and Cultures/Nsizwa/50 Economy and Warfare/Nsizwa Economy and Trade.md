# Nsizwa Economy and Trade

Cattle, cultivation and skilled labour form the foundation of the Nsizwa economy. Herds provide food, hides, traction and a widely recognised measure of wealth, while river agriculture supplies the grain required by towns and armies. Metalwork, ivory carving, medicines and beadwork connect inland households with Ukukhanya markets and the difficult maritime trade of the island-continent. Warfare enters this economy through tribute, cattle raiding and slavery.

Coin circulates through foreign trade, but most local obligations are reckoned in animals, goods, labour and remembered service. A household's prosperity therefore depends upon relationships as much as stored wealth: access to pasture, kin willing to share losses and captains able to protect a route.

## Cattle and Pasture

Cattle supply milk, meat, blood, hides and horn. Oxen carry goods and assist with cultivation. Herd ownership gives a family food security and the ability to support dependants, arrange marriages or meet compensation after injury.

Bridewealth commonly includes cattle, rare hides and crafted weapons. The exchange joins households and gives material weight to their obligations. Wealthy families can demand larger settlements, making marriage more difficult for people without herds unless kin or a regiment contributes.

Cattle also enter sacrifice and diplomacy. A ruler displays generosity by slaughtering animals for a public feast; a peace agreement may move breeding stock between former enemies. These transfers matter because an animal given away cannot also feed or enrich the household that raised it.

## Cattle Lords and Unequal Wealth

The largest herd owners, known collectively as cattle lords, control extensive grazing rights and can maintain personal bands of armed retainers. Their animals range across land used by poorer households, creating disputes over water and damage to crops. A cattle lord's support can decide a succession or sustain a regiment through a dry season.

Royal authority restrains them through tribute, marriage and military obligation. War-Kings must also bargain with them because confiscating too many animals would disrupt food supply and provoke armed resistance. Several periods of internal weakness began when captains and cattle lords joined against the throne.

Public teaching associates wealth with provision. A prosperous person should feed dependants, support festivals and restore herds lost in communal defence. Praise-singers remember generosity alongside herd size. Families that hoard during famine may retain animals and lose honour, clients and political safety.

## Farming, Fishing and Seasonal Work

Fields along the Mfula produce grain and garden crops. Communities maintain channels and small reservoirs against the dry season. Upland plots depend more heavily upon rainfall and frequently combine cultivation with herding.

Fishing supports river settlements and provides food when cattle must be preserved. Meat and fish are dried or smoked for military and trading journeys. Seasonal abundance demands storage, and control of granaries gives town authorities leverage during shortages.

The wet season concentrates work in fields, channels and repairs. Dry weather opens the long roads and increases market traffic. Herding households move between pastures throughout the year, exchanging labour and access with settled kin.

## Smiths and Other Artisans

Nsizwa smiths produce the inkandu thrusting spear, throwing spears, axes, knives, tools and metal fittings. Imported copper and iron supplement local supplies. Weapons of exceptional lineage are made with Spirit-Keeper rites, although the quality of blade and haft still depends upon material and craft.

Shield makers work reinforced hide and selected woods into the large isihlangu. Beadworkers use colour and pattern to mark household, regiment and remembered deed. Potters, leatherworkers and carvers supply daily goods and funerary objects. Ivory carving remains both a prestige craft and an important export.

Inkatho talismans carry praise-names, blessings and occasionally organised residue. Foreign buyers often exaggerate them as vessels containing ancestral souls. Nsizwa custodians regard that claim as offensive and restrict the sale of objects strongly associated with a named dead person.

## Markets and Exchange

Mzalu's Hold and the river settlements maintain the largest markets. Traders bring cattle products, grain, weapons, shields, ivory, beads and medicines. Imported goods include gold, copper, iron, dyes, fine cloth and unfamiliar herbs.

Ukukhanya merchants dominate much of the river trade. Their written accounts and standard measures allow large transactions, while also creating opportunities for debt and fraud unfamiliar to households accustomed to witnessed exchange. Mixed settlements have produced brokers who understand both systems.

Foreign ships reach only a limited number of coastal anchorages. Difficult currents and guarded inland routes keep the maritime trade small relative to local exchange. Goods from the Ahl-e-Ahd world arrive through long chains of intermediaries as well as occasional direct merchants.

## Raiding and Tribute

Cattle raiding can enrich a young warrior, weaken a rival and demonstrate the reach of a captain. It also destroys households. Theft of breeding animals may leave children hungry and provoke reprisals lasting several generations.

Some regiments specialise in raids and receive a share of captured animals. The War-King claims tribute from victories conducted under royal authority. Captains who conceal spoils or attack a protected community risk punishment, although political strength often determines whether a raid is later described as lawful war or private theft.

Tribute from subordinate districts includes cattle, grain, crafted goods and military service. Heavy demands can turn defence into extraction, particularly when a distant governor knows little about local harvests.

## Slavery and Captivity

Most enslaved people enter Nsizwa households through war, raiding or punishment after rebellion. They work as herders, agricultural labourers, domestic servants and porters. Some are forced into military service. Sale separates spouses, parents and children, especially when captives pass to foreign merchants.

The taking of a first captive has traditionally brought honour to a young warrior. This custom makes domination itself part of martial reputation. Captives may obtain freedom through exceptional service, adoption or a grant from their owner, but release remains dependent upon power held by the captor.

Chiefs and raiding leaders sell people to Ahl-e-Ahd merchants for metal, cloth, weapons and gold. Nsizwa critics condemn contract enslavement as a form of captivity acquired through words rather than battle while continuing to accept people seized by violence. The distinction protects local honour without protecting the enslaved.

Demand from foreign buyers encourages further raiding. Some chiefs have attacked weaker Nsizwa settlements and relabelled the captives as rebels or outsiders. Resistance to this practice has caused feuds and contributed to desertion into the Black-Tooth Clans.

## Trade Restrictions and Sacred Property

Izivukwa bodies, wrappings and tomb regalia may not be sold or displayed to outsiders. Foreign attempts to purchase fragments are treated as grave robbery. Routes to important tombs are concealed, and traders who question custodians too closely may be expelled.

Weapons and talismans attached to a specific ancestor also face restrictions. Ordinary objects of the same craft circulate widely. The distinction protects sacred relationships while allowing artisans to profit from respected Nsizwa techniques.

## Wealth and Political Power

The royal economy depends upon tribute, military shares and access to the principal markets. Horn-Captains convert office into wealth through herds, tolls and gifts; wealthy households convert material resources back into office by supporting regiments and marriages.

This circulation sustains the kingdom and produces recurrent abuse. Cattle, captives and ancestral objects all carry relationships that resist treatment as interchangeable property. Nsizwa economic disputes commonly ask who provided, who suffered the loss and which obligation survives after ownership changes.
