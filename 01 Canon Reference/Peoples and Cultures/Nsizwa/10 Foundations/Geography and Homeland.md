# Nsizwa Geography and Homeland

The Nsizwa inhabit a separate island-continent in the far southwestern ocean. Its settled interior consists of high savannas, escarpments and the valleys of the Mfula River, while salt country and difficult coasts limit easy approach. A hard voyage of roughly a month connects its waters with the Ättar coasts or Qazmarid. Nsizwa rulers prefer foreign access to remain difficult and closely watched.

Rainfall divides the year into a wet season of planting, pasture growth and difficult travel and a dry season in which water contracts, roads improve and conflict over cattle becomes more likely. Settlement follows defensible heights, dependable river bends and the routes by which herds can move between them.

## Highland Savannas

Open grassland forms the principal cattle country. Acacia, baobab and rocky outcrops break long slopes of golden grass, with smaller streams appearing after the rains. Wildebeest, zebra and buffalo share the grazing lands with domestic herds; lions, hyenas and leopards remain an ordinary danger.

During the wet season, households disperse livestock across renewed pasture. Herders repair temporary enclosures and follow water that will disappear later in the year. Cultivation expands in suitable ground near permanent settlements. Flowers, insects and migrating game support hunting and the collection of medicines.

The dry season concentrates animals and people around surviving water. Loss of one well or grazing agreement can force a household into another community's territory. Cattle theft, retaliation and military levies consequently rise as the grass thins. Dust makes distant movement visible, giving watch posts and fast riders greater importance.

## Escarpments and Gorges

A broken belt of cliffs, gorges and steep passes protects much of the settled plateau. Villages occupy defensible shelves or the level ground above them. Rope bridges, carved stairs and narrow paths permit daily movement while slowing an attacking force.

Stone watch posts overlook the principal approaches. Fires and reflected light carry warnings between heights; drums serve where mist or broken ground obstructs sight. Caves and hidden ledges provided refuge during the collapse of Giant power and still hold graves, abandoned stores and older structures whose builders are unknown.

Wind passing through the gorges produces voices and rhythmic tones. Nsizwa communities associate particular sounds with fallen warriors or rulers. Travellers also learn the practical differences between a familiar echo, an animal call and a voice that answers a name.

## The Mfula River Valleys

The Mfula and its tributaries support the densest settlement. River water irrigates grain and garden plots, supplies fish and allows the movement of heavy goods that would travel slowly across the uplands. Communities maintain channels and shallow reservoirs to carry water into the dry months.

Settlements cluster at fords, navigable bends and the mouths of smaller valleys. Ferry families, fishers, cultivators and market households possess influence distinct from that of the cattle lords. Control of a crossing can enrich a captain or town, particularly where goods from the Ukukhanya cities enter Nsizwa country.

Many river bends have ancestral associations. Families pour beer or blood at recognised stones before crossing with a corpse, bridal party or royal messenger. Apparitions occur often enough for local practice to distinguish a sacred bend from an ordinary landing, although the identity of a presence may remain disputed.

## The Great Salt Flats

The flats beyond the settled valleys are remnants of an ancient inland sea. White salt, hard mud and shallow seasonal water produce powerful mirages. Reliable routes depend upon marked wells and knowledge of ground that can bear the weight of animals after rain.

Nsizwa funerary tradition describes the dishonoured dead wandering here without shade or recognition. Exiles and the Haze-Marked use the same landscape as refuge and approach road. Bones, abandoned camp circles and false fires make it difficult to separate human danger from supernatural report.

Salt has economic value, and guarded expeditions travel onto the flats despite the risk. A successful party returns before its water fails or arranges support from communities familiar with the margins. Captains who underestimate distance have lost whole columns there.

## Seasons and Movement

The first rains begin a period of renewal. Fields are prepared, cattle move towards fresh grazing and households arrange many marriages and festivals. Heavy water can close roads and flood river crossings, so major campaigns are uncommon. Communities use the season to repair walls, roofs and channels.

As the land dries, travel becomes easier and the strategic value of wells increases. Markets enlarge, military patrols move farther and raiding intensifies. Izivukwa are most often awakened during this season because war and scarcity produce the crises named in their tomb duties.

Seasonal movement keeps political boundaries flexible. A captain may govern a fortress and surrounding fields while herding households range far beyond them under negotiated access. The kingdom therefore controls people, obligations and routes more consistently than a surveyed frontier.

## Mzalu's Hold

Mzalu's Hold occupies a fortified plateau above the central savannas. The Great Serpent Stairway provides the principal ceremonial approach; tunnels and smaller paths connect the city with water, pasture and military routes. High walls and steep cliffs make direct assault difficult.

The Warrior's Quarter contains training grounds, armouries and forges. The Ancestral Court holds the Uhlanga Throne, royal shrines and the tombs of earlier rulers. Artisans, healers and traders occupy a broad district around the principal markets. The regimental arena hosts training, public trials and major duels.

The city depends upon the country below. Food, water, fuel and cattle arrive along guarded routes, and prolonged isolation would threaten it despite its fortifications. Control of these approaches has shaped every serious succession dispute.

## Valley of Echoes and the Silent Legion

The Valley of Echoes contains the tombs of ten generals collectively called the Silent Legion. Stone chambers preserve bodies, wrappings, regalia, praise-names and individual duties. Wards keep the tombs dormant until Spirit-Keepers perform the appropriate call.

Warriors visit the valley for training, meditation and ancestral counsel. Public access stops before the sealed chambers. Differences among the tombs suggest that the ten guardians may not share one continuity or identical form of awakening.

## The Fire Keepers' Ascent

The Fire Keepers' Ascent is a high mountain centre of ritual learning. Spirit-Keepers study medicine, astronomy, preservation and accounts of dangerous manifestations there. Pilgrimage is normally limited to recognised Keepers, rulers and warriors carrying a specific charge.

A maintained flame marks the summit precinct. Its unexplained dimming is treated as a warning and has preceded several periods of danger in received history. Weather and fuel are examined before a supernatural interpretation is accepted.

## Coasts and Foreign Approach

The island-continent's coasts are unevenly known to outsiders. Difficult currents, limited safe anchorages and Nsizwa control of inland routes prevent foreign ships from moving freely beyond a few trading places. Imported metals, cloth, dyes and unusual medicines nevertheless reach inland markets.

The Ukukhanya river world, Haze-Marked salt-country and Black-Tooth military communities shape the nearest political geography. [[Neighboring Peoples of the Nsizwa]] describes these relationships in detail.
