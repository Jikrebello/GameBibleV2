# Historical Atlas of the Nsizwa

The history of the Nsizwa began among human communities that survived the fall of the Giants beyond the refuge of the Glass People. Cattle camps, cultivators, smiths, burial custodians and armed households rebuilt society on the far southwestern plateau. Their descendants gradually formed fortified settlements and military alliances, then joined much of the central highland under the office of the War-King. Nsizwa tradition attributes that union to Mzalu, whose received Life remains the principal account of the kingdom's foundation.

The history can be established most securely through settlement remains, changes in herding and cultivation, metalwork, graves and the traditions shared by communities with different political interests. The sequence of early kings is much less certain. Praise-songs preserve relationships and memorable acts with considerable force, but they often gather the work of several generations around one famous ruler or Keeper.

## The World after the Giants

The ancestors of the Nsizwa lived outside the Glass migration. When Giant power failed during the Great Unbinding, no Ark carried them to safety and no Prophet organised their escape. They survived as Orphaned peoples: human communities who endured the collapse where they already lived, then rebuilt from the households, skills and local memory that remained.

Conditions differed across the island-continent. Plateau herders could move animals away from a failing estate or violent successor, but drought and blocked water made flight dangerous. River settlements possessed cultivated fields and boats, while their fixed stores attracted raiders. Highland communities controlled mines, narrow passes and defensible caves. Burial refuges sheltered children, medicines and the bodies of respected dead when exposed settlements became unsafe.

The old order did not vanish in one season. Detached Giant households, human clients and armed scavengers continued to demand food and labour in some districts. Elsewhere, abandoned roads and waterworks remained useful long after their rulers had gone. Survivors stripped workshops for metal, repaired channels and learned which ruins had become inhabited by predators or dangerous residues.

## Orphaned Reassembly, c. 2165–1900 BR

Between about 2165 and 1900 BR, related survivor populations established more durable communities across the plateaus, Mfula valleys and mountain approaches. Herding routes became agreements between households rather than the property of a Giant estate. Cultivators restored riverbanks and exchanged grain for cattle, hides and protection. Smiths recovered old techniques while adapting them to smaller furnaces and scattered sources of ore.

Burial had unusual importance during this recovery. Families carried bones or preserved bodies when they abandoned a settlement, because the dead supplied identity in a world where titles and boundaries had failed. Praise-names recorded where a household had lived, whom it had fed and which companions it had lost. Custodians who could preserve a corpse, treat illness and remember kinship acquired authority beside herders and war leaders.

These communities did not yet constitute one Nsizwa kingdom. Strongholds competed for pasture and river access. Mobile warbands defended cattle, took captives and sometimes became the principal danger faced by the settlements that had hired them. Marriage, fosterage and seasonal markets nevertheless joined people across political divisions. The language, regimental customs and mortuary practices later recognised as Nsizwa grew from this long exchange.

## Strongholds and Cattle Roads

As population recovered, prominent households fortified the heights above water and pasture. A successful stronghold offered storage, a smithy, a defended grave enclosure and shelter during a raid. Its leader distributed grazing access and organised armed service. In return, dependants supplied cattle, grain and labour.

The arrangement protected communities while concentrating power. A chief able to close a stair or monopolise a reservoir could turn refuge into tribute. Raids aimed at breeding animals and stored grain, and captives were incorporated, exchanged or sold. Families driven from one stronghold founded new settlements or entered the following of a rival.

Long cattle roads connected this divided landscape. People met at watering places to exchange news, settle marriages and arrange the return of stolen animals. Praise-singers learned versions of the same feud from both sides. The practice of recounting failure beside victory emerged partly from such encounters: an account that ignored every surviving witness could not secure passage through country where those witnesses still controlled the wells.

## Mzalu and the First War-King

Mzalu is remembered as the ruler who transformed a coalition of strongholds and mobile cattle communities into the first durable Nsizwa kingdom. The date of his reign remains uncertain. Independent praise cycles agree that he negotiated with some rivals, defeated others and placed regiments, cattle tribute and ancestral ceremony beneath a single crown.

The Battle of the Seven Hills forms the centre of his public Life. Mzalu defeated three chiefs in one day. Two followings entered his settlement after submission; the third was destroyed after its leader broke an offered truce. Royal versions describe the battle as the end of a ruinous cycle of raiding. Descendants of the defeated remember captives taken from households that had not participated in the broken agreement.

Victory alone did not hold the new kingdom together. Mzalu used cattle settlements, marriages and shared feasts to bind former enemies to his household. Smiths armed the regiments, herders supported the enlarged royal following and local leaders retained authority beneath the crown. Mzalu's achievement lay in making these relationships survive his campaigns.

His successors ruled from Mzalu's Hold, a fortified plateau settlement that controlled important roads through the central savanna. The War-King became commander, judge and principal patron of ancestral rites. Horn-Captains governed districts and led regiments, but their loyalty depended upon marriage, wealth and the ruler's ability to distribute the cost and rewards of war.

## The Royal and Regimental Order

The kingdom expanded through a mixture of alliance, tribute and conquest. Communities that accepted royal protection supplied animals, grain and warriors. Those defeated in war lost cattle and captives, and some settlements were relocated to weaken an older chief. Regiments gave the crown reach beyond Mzalu's Hold while providing their members with training, companionship and a path to office.

The system never eliminated local society. Cattle lords retained large herds and armed followers. River towns depended upon cultivators, boat crews and merchants whose interests differed from those of plateau captains. Spirit-Keepers controlled knowledge of medicines, graves and dangerous rites. An effective War-King balanced these powers; a weak or predatory one allowed them to become competing governments.

Dambuza the Coward-King represents the political failure most feared in royal teaching. Received tradition says that he sent regiments into repeated wars, protected himself from their dangers and claimed every victory as his own. When fighting reached his gates, exhausted captains abandoned him. Whether the songs enlarged his caution into cowardice, they preserve a period in which military demands exceeded the relationships sustaining them.

## Mahlezi and the Tomb Traditions

Nsizwa tradition credits Mahlezi the Spirit-Tamer with joining funerary medicine, praise-name, regalia and a declared duty in the first complete izivukwa wrappings. Her teaching gave preserved bodies a durable public role. A tomb could answer a later emergency through a bounded task, and the living acquired procedures for ending the awakening once that task was complete.

The Children of Mahlezi developed these practices in several schools. Their work required skilled embalmers, weavers, smiths and singers as well as Spirit-Keepers. Failed preparations injured craftspeople, damaged bodies and sometimes produced guardians unable to recognise the end of their duty. Differences among the schools account for some of the variation found in surviving tombs.

Royal patronage gathered important guardians near Mzalu's Hold and in the Valley of Echoes. The Silent Legion, ten preserved generals in separate stone chambers, became both a defence and a source of political authority. Control of access to such tombs could strengthen a ruler's claim or allow a Keeper to obstruct one.

Nsizwa worshippers commonly understand an awakened body's speech and judgement as the return of an ancestor. The surviving evidence does not establish one explanation for every case. Preserved bodies, organised residues, inherited offices and genuine personal manifestations remain distinct possibilities. Human souls cannot be divided, manufactured or compelled into a corpse by funerary craft.

## Mzikhona's Five Awakenings

The received Life of Mzikhona the Unyielding spans five centuries. During his mortal life he held a narrow approach after the surrounding warband broke. His companions preserved him in the belief that such service should remain available to later generations.

Spirit-Keepers are said to have awakened the tomb five times under named military duties. The fifth appearance held an approach while civilians and cattle crossed into defensible country. After the battle, Mzikhona asked to rest because his fight was done. His tomb has not been called again.

Differences in armour, speech and temperament among the five accounts have never ended the tradition. They have instead made Mzikhona central to arguments about the rights of the honoured dead. Some captains reserve the right to call any guardian in the kingdom's final necessity. Others hold that his refusal binds the living, whatever the nature of the presence that spoke.

## Rivers, Salt and Neighbouring Powers

The kingdom developed beside other surviving societies. The Ukukhanya cities grew along the eastern Mfula, where river transport, cultivation and foreign shipping supported dense settlements. Trade brought Nsizwa cattle products, weapons and ivory into their markets and returned metal, beads, medicines and gold. Rival rulers also fought over crossings and tolls, then repaired relations through marriage and shared defence.

Beyond the Great Salt Flats, Haze-Marked clans controlled wells, caravan routes and knowledge of country that could kill an unfamiliar traveller. Some traded salt and guidance; others raided cattle settlements or served as mercenaries. Nsizwa expansion towards the flats repeatedly failed when armies outran their supplies or trusted a guide whose household they had previously harmed.

The Black-Tooth Clans formed from Nsizwa exiles, deserters and defeated military households. Their leaders preserved the Bull Horn and other regimental methods while rejecting the authority of the War-King. They offered service to neighbouring rulers, raided royal districts and sheltered people fleeing punishment. Nsizwa governments erased the names of condemned captains from public praise, but their families and new companions continued to remember them.

These relationships prevented the kingdom from becoming an isolated military culture. Merchants, interpreters, mixed households and returned captives carried techniques and beliefs across every border. Hostility remained genuine, yet no boundary prevented a useful custom, weapon or marriage from acquiring a local form.

## Captivity, Trade and Royal Wealth

Expansion increased the herds and tribute available to the crown. Horn-Captains and cattle lords accumulated followers by distributing animals, arranging marriages and supplying regiments. Town markets connected this wealth with specialised craft and river exchange.

War also supplied captives. Enslaved people worked as herders, cultivators, domestic servants, porters and soldiers. Sale to foreign merchants separated families and encouraged chiefs to raid weaker communities. Some leaders attacked Nsizwa settlements and described the captives as outsiders in order to preserve the honour attached to lawful war.

Criticism developed within the same society. Households that had lost relatives challenged the distinction between a person seized by a spear and one enslaved through contract. Praise-singers named captains who enriched themselves by exhausting their own districts. These disputes restrained particular rulers and contributed to desertion, but they did not abolish slavery.

## The Nsizwa Kingdom in 1360 AR

In 1360 AR, the War-King rules from Mzalu's Hold through royal households, Horn-Captains, regiments and alliances with local leaders. The kingdom commands much of the central plateau without exercising the same authority in every valley, river town or distant pasture. Cattle remain the chief measure of stored wealth, while grain, metalwork and trade sustain the permanent settlements.

The principal political tensions arise from the same institutions that built the kingdom. Cattle lords can support or obstruct royal policy. Regiments provide defence and social belonging while consuming labour and encouraging raids. Spirit-Keepers protect medicine and ancestral practice while control of famous tombs gives them influence over succession. River commerce enriches mixed settlements and exposes them to Ukukhanya power and foreign demand for slaves.

The memory of Mzalu offers rulers a model of conquest joined to settlement. Mahlezi's tradition gives custodians a means of preserving duty and a warning about binding it too tightly. Mzikhona's last request asks whether inherited service can become captivity. These histories remain active because Nsizwa institutions still depend upon the answers given to them.

## History, Praise and Uncertainty

Material remains establish the antiquity of cattle keeping, cultivation, metalwork and protected burial on the island-continent. Separate traditions also support a long history of political contact among the plateau, river and salt-flat societies. The broad formation of the Nsizwa as an Orphaned people is therefore distinct from the sacred history of the Glass descendants.

Praise-song supplies much of the personal history. Its performers preserve affection, ridicule, guilt and claims upon the powerful with an immediacy absent from lists of rulers. The form can also combine several victories, turn a defeated rival into a monster or give one founder the work of a generation. Historians compare songs from royal, household and enemy traditions before treating a detail as established.

The Last Houses belong to a separate distant history. Their mortuary government developed independently, and no hidden journey or teaching lineage connects them with the Nsizwa. Similar concern for the dead does not make the two societies branches of one civilisation.
