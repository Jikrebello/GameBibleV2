# Thalmyric Daily Life and the Burning Year

Thalmyric daily life is organized around households, parishes, seasonal labor, memorial observance, military obligation, and fire kept alive by hand. The ceremonial language is grand; the practice is intimate: trimming a wick, sharing fast-day food, repairing an aqueduct branch, walking to a grave, or carrying a lamp to a new home.

## The Burning Year (the shared calendar, quarreled over)

Thalmyria keeps the same twelve months as Eclessia — the calendar predates the Split by seven centuries — but the **feasts have diverged**, and so, maddeningly, have some of the *dates*: a computational quarrel over the Risen-reckoning means Thalmyric and Eclessian parishes celebrate the resurrection feasts up to nine days apart in most years. Border parishes celebrate twice; polemicists on both sides have written books; everyone's grandmother knows which date is real.

The Thalmyric year's great days are its own:

- **The Feast of the Rekindling** (1052 AR) — the supreme feast: every lamp in the empire extinguished at dusk and relit from one flame carried on foot from Caleran, hearth to hearth, hand to hand, across a thousand miles of relay. For one night the empire is a single nervous system of fire. (A household whose lamp goes out before the runner comes is held to a year's bad luck; a village that *misses* the relay is held to something worse, and there are three folktales about villages that did.)
- **The Feast of the Veil** (906 AR) — the Mother's protection over Veridun; the army's feast, kept under arms, the one night of the year soldiers stand vigil *instead of* watch.
- **The Saint-Emperor's Day** — Caedros I's translation at the Pillar; imperial, liturgical, and diplomatically radioactive (Eclessian envoys develop scheduling conflicts every year).
- **The Dormition of the Fire-Bearer** — the tenderest feast, kept at dusk with bread and cedar-smoke, per the tomb tradition.
- **The Great Fast** — longer and harder than Eclessia's Emberfast, and worn as a badge: "the West fasts from meat; we fast from comfort."

## The Hearth-Lamp (the household's heart)

Every Thalmyric home keeps a **lamp-niche**: an icon, a wick, and a fire that is *tended, not lit* — ideally never extinguished between Rekindlings, fed by the household's women in a duty passed mother to daughter. The lamp is the family's chapel, witness, and record: oaths are sworn on it, the dying face it, newborns are presented to it before they are presented to the priest. The Lamp Controversy was not an abstraction here; when the Breakers came, ordinary households hid their lamps in wells and bread-ovens for a generation, and the phrase **"oven-fire"** still means a faith kept secretly. To let the lamp die through neglect is the Thalmyric shorthand for a family's moral collapse — courts have cited it in disinheritance cases.

## Food

The land is volcanic highland over warm lowland: **ash-soil vineyards** (Thalmyric black wine, exported everywhere the Church argues), olives and hard wheat along the Aurin lowlands, mountain barley, sheep and goat above the tree line, honey from crater-meadows, and the fast-day cuisine that defines the palate — beans, greens, smoked octopus and salt-fish from the Thalassian coast, and the lentil dishes soldiers call "the long war." Meat is festival food; bread is liturgy (loaves are stamped with the flame-seal, and the heel of the loaf goes to the lamp-niche shelf, then to the birds — "the hearth eats first"). Meals begin with the youngest child lighting a taper from the hearth-lamp to the table: no flame, no food.

## Weddings

The rite is **crowning**: bride and groom are crowned with iron circlets warmed at the church's lamp (nobles use silver; soldiers famously use two arrowheads bent to rings), walked three times around the flame while the Standing Hymn's wedding verses are sung, and sent home carrying a taper lit from the ceremony — with which they light their first household lamp *together*, the marriage's true completion. Divorce exists (the East is pragmatic where the West is juridical) but requires the lamp's formal division, a small grim ceremony nobody attends twice willingly. Border-folk note the tell: an Eclessian bride shows her ring; a Thalmyric bride shows her burn-scar callus, the mark of a hand that carries fire.

## Funerals (the ember-rite)

Death faces the lamp. The dying are turned toward the niche; the **viaticum ember** — a coal from the household fire, carried in a bronze spoon — is held before them at the last breath, "so the soul leaves looking at the light." Burial follows the shared soul-routing doctrine (consecrated ground, three days), but the East adds the **memorial ladder**: prayers at the third, ninth, and fortieth days and the year's mind, each with kolyva-style boiled wheat and honey shared at the grave — the fortieth-day feast being the moment a household formally stops wearing grief. Soldiers' rites are the empire's tenderest: a man's unit keeps his fortieth-day even decades later if he has no family left, and veteran villages hold a common fortieth for the missing of Qarath's Well to this day, one hundred and forty-six years on.

**The funeral verse (sung by one voice, then silence):**
> *Carry the ember, not the ash; / the road is bright, the water black.*
> *Heed no voice but One, my soul — / the lamp stays lit till you come back.*

(The same buried warning-line as the Eclessian and Folk keens. The East, too, has forgotten it is counter-smuggling doctrine.)

## Soldiers, Widows, and the Long Memory

Thalmyria is a veteran culture the way Sestara is a merchant one. Frontier military districts contain farms held by soldier-tenure, where a plough-team can double as a lance-team. War widows' guilds run much of the wool trade and charitable relief. Veteran villages keep regimental feast-days like saints' days. The shame of Qarath's Well is taught as a warning against both defeat and the abuses committed afterward. The gap between the war party's appetite and the silence of people who have already served is one of the empire's quietest fault lines.

## Songs, Sayings, Jokes

**Soldiers' marching verse (the "Lentil Song," countless verses):**
> *The West sends prayers, the Bank sends bills, / the Guard sends steel and slaughter —*
> *but the Fire sends us lentils, lads, / and marches us to water.*
> *One lamp, one road, one bowl, one grave — / and still the wick burns brighter:*
> *the Carpenter was poor as us, / and He outlasted fire.*

**Sayings:** *"They speak while we burn"* is the standard unfair verdict on the West. *"Oven-fire"* means faith kept secretly. *"He let his lamp die"* describes moral collapse. *"Bright as the ninth day"* means grief pretending to be fine. *"Don't sell your crowns"* warns a married couple not to cheapen their vows.

**The joke the East tells about the Split:** An Eclessian cardinal and a Thalmyric monk argue theology for three days. Finally the cardinal, exasperated: "Brother, we have documents, councils, protocols — what do *you* have?" The monk holds up his lamp. "A light. Would you like to *file* it?"

## In the Borderlands (and toward Grenzburg)

Along the schism border the two daily lives interleave: valleys that crown their brides *and* cry their banns, double-dated feast years, and parishes that keep an Eclessian register and a Thalmyric lamp. Thalmyric merchants, pilgrims, and exile-monks appear in eastern settings (a lamp-tender in a Grenzburg winter camp is instantly recognizable — the one refugee whose first bundle is a burning pot), and the Velian Church's bare prayer-halls sit at the third corner of every border town's quiet religious triangle.
