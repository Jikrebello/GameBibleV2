# Eclessian and Thalmyric Holy Warriors

“Paladin” is a broad translators' word for several vows of armed religious service. Neither Eclessia nor Thalmyria maintains a single universal paladin class, and neither tradition guarantees miraculous power merely because someone wears a holy badge.

## Shared Foundation

Both traditions require confession, discipline, protection of noncombatants, obedience to lawful authority, and refusal of Adversary-aligned practices. Genuine workings of the White Flame remain gifts rather than techniques. A devout warrior may serve faithfully for a lifetime without producing visible miracles.

## Eclessian Traditions

Eclessian holy warriors usually belong to a chartered order, princely household, cathedral foundation, or confessional militia. Their vows are written, witnessed, and interpreted through institutional law. Old-Church orders emphasize sacrament, relic custody, pilgrimage, and episcopal command. Freeflame orders emphasize scripture, congregational accountability, and resistance to fraudulent spiritual claims.

Their strength is clear jurisdiction and a developed law of arms. Their danger is allowing office, paperwork, or confessional allegiance to stand in for holiness.

## Thalmyric Traditions

Thalmyric religious soldiers serve through the [[01 Canon Reference/Peoples and Cultures/Thalmyric Empire/50 Economy and Warfare/Thalmyric Military Orders.md|Thalmyric Military Orders]], provincial armies, monastery guards, and temporary penitential vows. Prayer ropes, icons, vigils, and anointing accompany service, but physical fire is not presumed sacred. The Returning Standard tradition stresses recovery and lawful return; the Long Watch defends exposed communities; the Iron Martyrs care for wounded veterans and guard authenticated relics.

Their strength is endurance, communal discipline, and integration with frontier life. Their danger is treating military suffering as proof of divine approval or confusing warlock Shell-fire with the White Flame.

## How the Traditions Parted

Both traditions descend from the refuge and frontier armies of the Fall, and they parted over what a holy warrior carries.

Thalmyric soldiers carried lamps. The war-lamps that kindled before Caleran in the 950s went dark in the hands of bought chaplains, were broken over their own bearers' knees when the Breaking reached the army (984), were gathered into crown lamp-houses and sealed there after a Master of Lamps burned to death forcing one before the walls of Meliton (1029), were left to die when their keepers sold the oil, and were relit from the Ember at the Rekindling of 1052. Thalmyric orders learned from those seventy years that the Fire answers whom it will and never for the asking, punishes the hand that forces it, and does not survive being locked up for its own good. The military districts of Caedros III kept a door-lamp beside the lance, and those lamps kindled when district families held their own houses in the War of the Southern Deeds (1131–1139), which is why Thalmyric veterans distrust a lamp carried on campaign more than a lamp hung by a door. At Qarath's Well in 1214 the Kedravan commander read the well-curb aloud and was overruled, and his monastic contingents kept the refuge rule against drinking sworn water and came home when the army that drank did not; the Long Watch and the Iron Martyrs still teach that rule to recruits.

Eclessian warriors carried examined banners. Adalmar's crown rested on the banner that kindled at Kaltfurt in 974, which the Sanctum's court examined and credited to him, although the miller's widow who held it testified otherwise. No heir of his ever carried a banner the Sanctum would record as answering, and the Crown of Embers was never worn again. In the wars of Ludovar's sons (1027–1041) banners were examined by rival bishops and some carried Shell-fire in the staff-head, and Eclessian orders have sworn their banners before witnesses ever since. The Knights of Saint Aveline rode in the southern campaigns that followed the Folk Wars, the First Southern Crusade (1060–1067) and the Great Southern Purge (1158–1163), and lost a chapter to the riders who went through the marcher camps in the winter of 1163. Their penitential memory begins there.

## Recognition Across the Split

The two traditions can recognize courage, valid baptism, and sincere service in one another while disputing authority and doctrine. Joint action is easiest against an obvious supernatural evil and hardest where land, relic, or jurisdiction is at stake. An Eclessian knight may consider a Thalmyric command too entangled with bishops and empire; a Thalmyric veteran may consider an Eclessian order too dependent on charters issued by divided princes.
