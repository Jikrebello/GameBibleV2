# Historical Atlas of Eclessia

Eclessia is a family of eastern successor kingdoms and duchies whose peoples formed from provincial Atherian, Folk, Markish, Vesari and other regional communities. Its common civilisation grew through the Church, local lordship and the memory of Adalmar's crown, while political authority remained divided. The Great Split, the Grey Sweat and the Reformation left the Eclessian world of 1360 with rival rulers, contested rites and a strong shared historical identity.

## Historical Atlas — wider inheritance

Eclessians are a later Braided people formed from provincial Atherian, Folk, Markish, Vesari and other regional layers. Conversion did not erase those histories. Church institutions connected them while warfare, landholding and dynastic loyalty kept their political worlds plural.

During the [[Historical Atlas of the Atherian Age of Glory|Age of Glory]], eastern river cities and mountain corridors belonged among the empire's richest and most politically important provinces. The Veloran and Serrat houses reached imperial rule through eastern commands and municipal alliances. Raveth's famine estates and Arcelon's military road left agricultural and transport inheritances later absorbed into Eclessian regions, without making modern Eclessians one ancient provincial population.

Adalmar's rise and coronation in 1002 produced an imperial claim alongside Veridun. Partition, the Great Split and the Crown of Embers' trust in 1104 left a world of kingdoms and duchies rather than a temporary failure to become one state. Forced conversion during the Folk Wars, including Wolfsmere's drowned oath-keepers, remains part of the inheritance.

The Grey Sweat changed labour, tenancy and religious memory unevenly. Reform challenged genuine abuses and generated new persecutions. The Korrheim Accord protected selected territorial rites without settling the older schism, radical communities or reunion. In 1360, a shared civilizational identity therefore coexists with incompatible churches and rulers. Its myths remember friendships broken by institutions and poor households whose lives no settlement restored.
