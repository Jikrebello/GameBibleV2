# Status, Slavery, and Manumission in the Covenant

Covenant law distinguishes free subjects, protected communities, debt-bound laborers, household servants, military bondspeople, war captives, name-bound persons, and the enslaved. These categories carry different rights and are frequently manipulated.

## Sources of Bondage

Bondage arises through war, birth under inherited instruments, criminal sentence, unpaid debt, sale by a lawful custodian, and military levy. Kidnapping a free protected person is illegal; converting kidnapping into a valid-looking instrument is a profitable profession.

Foreign war captives include Ättar raiders taken along the western strait. Some are sold as oarsmen, guards, spectacle fighters, or gladiators because foreign buyers prize their size and rumored rage. Contract handlers attempt to trigger that rage for profit despite the deaths it causes.

## Rights Within Bondage

Enslaved people may petition over food, injury, separation forbidden by an instrument, withheld manumission, or an owner's breach. Such rights save lives but do not make ownership just. Name custody can prevent flight or resistance through metaphysical force and is among the Covenant's gravest institutions.

## Manumission

Freedom may be purchased, granted, inherited through a clause, earned after fixed service, ordered for breach, or secured by sanctuary foundations. A valid release returns legal control of the person's name. Freed households often remain clients of former owners because land, work, and witnesses are still controlled by the same network.

## Reform and Resistance

Reformist jurists attack perpetual and inherited bondage, arguing that obligation without a possible completion is ownership disguised as covenant. Their opponents claim that abolishing old instruments would collapse estates, armies, and public works. The conflict is political, moral, and gameable; the institution itself remains genuinely evil.
