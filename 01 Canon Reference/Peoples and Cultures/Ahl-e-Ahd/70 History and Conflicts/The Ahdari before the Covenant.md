# The Ahdari before the Covenant

The **ancient Ahdari** formed in the Near-Glass and western well country between approximately 1950 and 1400 BR. Their principal ancestors belonged to the camp continuum which remained after the southern Glass-descended leagues departed. Older Orphaned well keepers, Narashi caravan houses, Tarkhan families, captives and immigrants entered the same communities over subsequent generations.

Ahdari households recognised common forms of guest protection, water contribution, rescue debt, compensation and public oath. Their related dialects became the principal speech of the western camel roads. Marriage, adoption, manumission and admission at a protected well allowed outsiders to belong. This shared life made the Ahdari a durable people without requiring descent from one ancestor or obedience to one ruler.

Al-Ahdiya and the Covenant did not yet exist. Ancient households preserved portions of the Great Prophet's teaching, forgot others and combined inherited practice with local cults. Their history therefore belongs to the pre-Covenant well country and its own political and religious world.

## The Near-Glass camps

The Prophet's death left several practical offices among the One Glass People. Judges settled disputes. Provisioners divided common stores. Captains defended particular roads. Elders spoke for households and remembered kin. Each office possessed limited authority, and none carried the Prophet's universal commission.

The southern departures removed craftsmen, animals and armed households from camps near the Glass. The people who remained faced recurring choices whenever water failed. A travelling court could carry judgement and family records towards another well. A settled group could deepen one source, build walls and bind nearby households to its defence. Families used both responses for generations.

Contact with Orphaned neighbours expanded their knowledge and their disputes. Narashi guides knew western water which the Glass descendants had never used. Tarkhan hunters crossed country where caravan animals found little pasture. Older oasis families possessed gardens, graves and shrine claims. These people became allies, spouses, rivals, servants, slaves and hosts.

The first communities later called Ahdari arose where defence of a particular well required continuing obligations. Households supplied labour, animals, weapons and stored food. In return they received water, refuge and a recognised place in public decisions. Strong wells protected their dependants and attracted trade. Their keepers could also exclude travellers, compel service and turn emergency control of water into hereditary power.

## The northern fission

The division between early Jirahar and Ahdari institutions unfolded across many settlements between about 1950 and 1750 BR. Jirahar communities invested in travelling judges, route memory and teaching which could cross political frontiers. Ahdari communities invested in walls, draw gear, guarded stores and compensation enforced at a fixed place.

Violence exposed the strengths and costs of each choice. A judge who departed with a travelling court preserved names and decisions while leaving fields behind. A well keeper who remained could protect grain and graves while losing contact with distant families.

One remembered episode concerns a camp whose travelling custodians ordered a departure while water and food remained. Several households refused because their dead were buried beside the well and a second journey threatened their sick relatives. They built a wall and survived the raid. The travellers preserved the camp's decisions; those who stayed kept its graves and stores. The tale is known as *The Court That Walked and the Wall That Stayed*.

Marriage and cooperation continued across the developing division. A Jirahar judge might marry into an Ahdari water house and retain the right to travel. Ahdari caravans asked road courts to hear disputes involving several wells. The fission produced overlapping families and related institutions across two increasingly distinct peoples.

## Belonging

Early communities named themselves by wells, markets and houses. **Ahdari** became a broad self-name during the Bronze-Road centuries. It first described households admitted to a shared body of protection across several western waters. By the ninth century BR, neighbours used it for camp and caravan populations speaking related dialects and recognising those obligations.

A child born into an Ahdari household ordinarily belonged. A spouse entered through a recognised marriage and acquired the protection of the receiving house. Adoption joined an outsider to burial, compensation and rescue duties. A freed slave needed a household, craft association or well community willing to admit them, since release alone provided no animals, shelter or protection.

Rank shaped these rights. Wealthy camel houses controlled transport and held dependent families in debt. Water keepers possessed essential knowledge and public responsibility. Armed protectors expected shares from caravans and raids. Gardeners, artisans, hired drivers, slaves and people without recognised descent held weaker claims on common stores.

Women exercised durable authority where property and expertise supported it. Water keepers controlled keys, draw schedules and emergency reserves. Caravan widows inherited route shares and animals. Senior women arranged marriages and fosterage. Captive women and poor dependants remained exposed to forced marriage, concubinage and sale.

## Wells and camps

A major well formed a complete settlement. Its shaft and draw gear stood at the centre. Cisterns held water when the source flowed; gardens occupied lower ground where runoff could be used. Storehouses contained grain, dates, salt and fodder. Graves recorded the labour and tenure of households which had maintained the place across generations.

Water workers, gardeners, potters, leatherworkers and guards lived there throughout the year. Seasonal visitors brought tents and livestock. A market drew merchants, poets, judges, healers and foreign envoys, sometimes enlarging the population several times over. A ruler's control depended upon the workers who maintained the equipment and the armed households which accepted his allocation.

Claims to water arose from digging, repair, inherited use, burial, payment, hospitality and negotiated season. Several claims could apply to the same well. Drought sharpened their contradictions, and powerful houses sometimes converted emergency priority into permanent ownership.

Many Ahdari lived for part or all of the year in mobile camps. One household might tend dates at a well, carry Qahuri grain during the dry season and move goats after rain. Another specialised in long-distance transport and owned no fields.

A caravan joined investors, drivers, guards, guides, cooks, healers and repairers. Slaves performed much of the loading and water work in wealthy trains. Camels extended the range of travel and made western houses valuable to settled states, although a large caravan could empty a minor cistern and leave its residents unable to survive. Passage therefore depended upon negotiation, controlled herd size and payment for damage.

Caravan traffic also created towns. **Mazar** began around secondary wells bypassed by prestigious routes. Merchants diverted traffic there after the massacre at Saqara. Storehouses, animal markets and repair yards followed, and Mazar's importance grew from this movement rather than from a royal foundation.

## Hospitality, raiding and ransom

Hospitality protected travellers beyond the reach of a dependable water source. A host supplied shade, drink, food and a period of safety. The guest accepted limits upon violence and conduct. This exchange expressed affection, religious duty, political display and practical alliance.

Sanctuary attached to tents, shrines, wells, markets and recognised seasons. Reputation, allied retaliation and supernatural fear sustained it. A household which betrayed a guest could lose traffic and marriage partners for generations. The custom also sheltered wealthy criminals and allowed hosts to turn rescue debt into years of forced service.

Raiding formed part of the open-country economy. Leaders gathered followers and promised shares of animals, goods or captives. Raids answered theft, enforced pasture claims, displayed strength or supplied a camp after drought. Ambition and insult caused others.

The violence reached whole households. Raiders killed herders, removed animals required for milk and transport, and carried away women, children and defeated men. Captives were ransomed, exchanged, sold, enslaved or forced into marriage and concubinage. Sexual violence accompanied attack and captivity. Later descendants sometimes valued a line born from such a union; their pride did not make the original act consensual.

Blood vengeance deterred murder where no ruler protected a family. It also transferred punishment to relatives who had opposed the killer. Compensation, fosterage, marriage and public submission could end a feud, although rich houses pressed poorer kin to accept terms which preserved an offender.

Poetry had practical force in these disputes. Praise advertised a protector's generosity. Satire exposed cowardice or failure to defend a guest. Elegy named the dead and demanded remembrance. A verse could close negotiations or humiliate a chief into further bloodshed.

## The Silent Month at Saqara

Saqara held a protected market during a season when participating groups entered without armour. Merchants sold dates, salt, cloth, tools and animals. Families arranged marriages and ransoms. People brought disputes which could not be heard safely in an armed camp.

Around 1280 BR, a satirist accused the protector **Harun Savel** of purchasing peace because he lacked the courage to defend his clients. Harun concealed spearheads beneath date sacks and allowed his rivals to enter the market. His followers armed themselves after the public recitation and attacked.

They killed merchants, pilgrims, slaves, children and dependants who had no part in the insult. Survivors carried wounded relatives towards wells which lacked food and water for them, and several died on the road. Harun seized the market goods and described the massacre as a defence of honour.

Caravan houses abandoned Saqara and transferred their business to Mazar. Harun's family lost the revenue it had tried to protect and divided over his succession. Later communities prohibited concealed weapons at protected markets. The law arose from the massacre and the flight which followed it.

## Religion before the Covenant

Ancient Ahdari religion contained several overlapping traditions.

**Name-Keeping households** preserved prayers to the Lord, portions of the Prophet's teaching and a refusal to bow before images. Their knowledge varied. Some recited inherited words whose meaning had become obscure. Others used descent from the Glass people to claim superiority over Orphaned neighbours.

**High-place rites** sought rain, safe birth, animal health and protection on the road. Worshippers sacrificed valuable animals, poured water or oil and left marked cloth. Particular rites addressed local court persons or Menhir correspondences. Their physical effects remained limited to the encounter itself and supplied no judgement upon the worshippers' claims.

**Stones and standards** carried household histories. Some were memorials or empty cult objects. Dependent Idol Masks gathered around a smaller number. Wrath fed through vengeance standards demanding blood beyond the original offence; Greed through stones at which water, ransom and debt were pledged as property; Pride through genealogical objects said to prove an untouchable line.

**Patron bargains** maintained particular cisterns, warnings or paths through anchors to the Egg White. Patrons demanded goods, secrecy or service. Predatory agreements turned rescue into inherited debt. These relationships remained separate from human water custom and well government.

Neighbouring camps therefore used similar rites while addressing different powers. No priesthood or cult governed all Ahdari communities.

## The boy beneath Ashal Stone

The oasis of **Kor Ashal** endured three failed seasons around the eleventh century BR. Its ruler revived a local claim that burying a firstborn beneath the high stone would restore the spring. Priests selected the son of a debtor household without armed kin able to protect him.

The child was killed and buried. Rain fell on the eastern road, while Kor Ashal's water remained brackish. The priests blamed an error in the family's descent and demanded another victim. The child's mother broke the altar face with a mason's hammer. The ruler executed her and imprisoned the workers who had helped her.

Several caravan houses abandoned Kor Ashal. Two sheltered the woman's surviving daughter; another sold information about the fugitives. Traffic moved elsewhere and the oasis declined. Later reformers described the murder as representative of all ancient Ahdari religion, although contemporary Ahdari had resisted the cult and protected its survivors.

No surviving account establishes that a supernatural power inhabited the stone or answered the sacrifice.

## Foreign clients and neighbouring powers

Qahuri, Arqeshite and Vasharan rulers rarely governed the deep well country directly. They paid local houses to protect roads, took hostages from client families and supplied grain or weapons to favoured leaders. These arrangements created small courts around caravan stages.

A client king drew followers from camps, towns, poets, priests, guards and foreign specialists. His royal titles came from the state which supported him. His practical authority rested upon feeding followers and protecting traffic. When payment failed, soldiers deserted, raided the client population or demanded land.

The cousins **Savan Uruz** and **Demer Khol** were raised in the same foster camp. Savan accepted an Arqeshite crown and garrison at an eastern station. Demer controlled western camel roads with Qahuri support. Each claimed to protect the country from the other, and each raided communities beyond the districts whose tribute he received.

Savan employed Taleri smiths and Arqeshite deportees. Demer supported poets, camel races and a sanctuary for fugitives from Savan's taxes. He also sold captured eastern families into Qahuri estates. When Arqeshite payments stopped after 706 BR, Savan seized grain from his soldiers' families and the soldiers killed him. Demer tried to recruit the unpaid companies and was defeated by former captives.

The client courts disappeared, while their mixed households and craftspeople remained. Their history encouraged several wells to strengthen assemblies which could survive a patron's death.

### Jirahar and Tarkhan

Ahdari and Jirahar shared Near-Glass family histories, prayer fragments and road institutions. Jirahar judges travelled beyond particular jurisdictions; Ahdari keepers organised enforcement around place. Both peoples cooperated and fought across this institutional boundary.

Tarkhan neighbours supplied camels, hunting knowledge, animal medicine, escorts and fighters. Ahdari houses supplied grain, market access and relationships with western towns. Marriage and fosterage produced mixed households. Captivity and slave sale produced other mixtures which descendants remembered with unease.

### Qahur, the Gateway and the eastern empires

Qahuri crowns bought animals and transport, employed scouts and sought control of northern grain routes. Ahdari caravans carried river produce. Qahuri military houses also seized wells and settled captives.

Between about 1700 and 1200 BR, Ahdari households entered established coastal settlements around the western gateway. Together with local cultivators, maritime settlers, captives and freed people, they formed the Braided coastal society described in [[Gateway Peoples, Qarzeth and the Spared Towns]]. Gateway civic history remained independent of the well country.

Arqesh demanded scouts, hostages and route access. Its deportations placed Ahdari families in river cities and foreign artisans at northern stations. Vasharan rulers inherited parts of this network after Arqesh fell and maintained influence through clients rather than continuous occupation.

## Tavisha and Rethmar

By the fourth century BR, **Rethmar** served as a winter gathering where Tarkhan and Ahdari circuits met. Gardeners, smiths and water keepers lived there permanently. Visiting households occupied seasonal compounds around its reservoirs and market.

**Tavisha** belonged to a family which maintained three reservoirs and supervised emergency distribution. Her authority rested upon knowledge, public service and the willingness of visiting houses to accept her measures.

Lethren Ostar approached in 381 BR with an Atherian army and demands for water, transport and passage. Tavisha inventoried the stores, moved filled skins away from the road and sent gardeners and children towards secondary wells. She marked empty camps where Ostar's scouts expected supplies.

**Kharuz of the Stone Wells** organised camel scouts and foot archers. He drew the Atherian columns apart through separate negotiations and attacked the detachments moving between wells. Ostar and his son died during the retreat. Northern forces captured officers, soldiers, servants and carriers.

Fever entered the camp before ransom could be arranged. Tavisha ordered the release of sick captives and non-combatant water workers with enough water to reach the first southern station. Kharuz exchanged healthy officers for weapons and animals. Captives whose families sent no payment were exchanged, admitted to local households or enslaved.

The coalition dispersed after securing Rethmar. Tavisha governed the water upon which the gathering depended; Kharuz commanded a wartime following. Neither office gave its holder sovereignty over the other or over the northern peoples.

## The Ahdari in 350 BR

By 350 BR, Ahdari communities occupied the western camel circuits, defended wells, market towns, gardens and settled quarters in foreign cities. Related dialects and common obligations sustained a broad identity, while a person still named a particular well, house or market when those details mattered.

The people included pastoral families, caravan investors, drivers, artisans, poets, protectors, farmers, slaves and urban labourers. Some communities maintained Lordward prayer. Others followed local cults or combined several traditions. No ruler or priest spoke for them all.

Rethmar's defenders had checked one Atherian expedition. Caleran merchants continued to seek northern goods and routes. Qahuri and Vasharan agents supported clients, while Tarkhan houses shared several western circuits. The well country at the threshold of Atherian unification remained connected, armed and capable of refusing foreign passage.
