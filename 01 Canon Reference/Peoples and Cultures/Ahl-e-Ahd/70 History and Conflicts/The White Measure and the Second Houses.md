# The White Measure and the Second Houses

The **White Measure** is the name given to a Qahuri mortuary discipline that treated accumulated wealth as the substance of an immortal household. Its followers restrained appetite, cultivated profitable work and built elaborate necropolises called **Second Houses**. A small number completed the discipline's final rite, binding the founder's human soul to an embalmed body and opening a private realm in the Egg White.

Successful founders remained the same human persons after bodily death. Their private realms allowed them to counsel descendants, send proxies and lease shelter, knowledge or power. This made them Patrons of the Egg White and liches in the older sense of the word: human souls deliberately held from their proper road.

The final rite intercepted the soul before it reached the Boatman and postponed judgement for as long as the binding endured. When the true binding ended, the soul resumed its passage.

## Teaching and discipline

House-Builders taught that uncontrolled appetite scattered a person's life. Food, touch, sex, anger, waste and careless generosity created claims through which the world divided the self. Discipline gathered the person into one design. Wealth acquired without indulgence supplied ordered substance from which the final house could be made.

Initiates practised periods of sexual abstinence and avoided casual touch during trade and ritual preparation. They wore clean, undyed linen and ate food whose origin could be traced. Their final years included medicines containing small quantities of gold, copper, salts and mineral resins. Correct preparation improved preservation of the body. Errors caused pain, tremors, sterility, madness and early death.

The discipline produced able merchants, physicians, appraisers and administrators. White Measure partnerships financed ships, workshops and grain storage. Apprentices learned calculation, foreign measures and the properties of oils, cloth and metals. Their public usefulness helped the movement survive hostility from kings and temples.

Prosperity also became evidence of virtue. Successful adherents attributed wealth to order. Poverty suggested concealed indulgence, generosity or impurity. Aid generally took the form of investment, employment or incorporation into a stronger household. A beneficiary received food and training while acquiring obligations intended to survive death.

## The final rite

A Second House joined the founder's body, true name, necropolis, wealth, witnesses and return tether. Mineral treatment and embalming prepared the corpse. The tomb reproduced a perfected household in real rooms. Precious material embedded in its walls and furnishings supplied metaphysical mass. As the founder's final breath failed, witnesses closed the true name into the anchor and opened its correspondence with the Egg White.

When the rite succeeded, the founder awoke within a genuine demiplane. Its local laws reflected the planned household. A merchant's hall might require exchange for every object taken. A military founder might assign rank to every guest. The embalmed corpse remained the innermost soul-cage and most powerful Material manifestation.

Each realm required upkeep. Lamps were filled, names recited, damaged images repaired and pieces of the physical endowment kept in correspondence with Astral rooms. Neglect caused chambers to contract or disappear. Stolen funerary metal could wound the founder and weaken the return tether.

A founder became a Patron once the realm could lease something beyond itself. Services included guarded storage, dreams of floods or prices, preservation of memories, occult teaching and temporary refuge. The Material agents receiving these gifts also repaired tombs, recovered lost anchor pieces and carried out choices the dead household could not make alone.

## Meresha and the Court of Unspent Lamps

**Meresha of Isethar** founded the movement during the first Twin-Reed division. She belonged to a funerary-linen household and learned to trace cloth, resin and payments across hostile crowns. During the famine of 1468 BR, she financed grain boats and took workshop shares from families unable to repay her. Her stores fed labourers and enlarged her authority over them.

Meresha studied a damaged Ishkatari receiving plate which reacted to personal names, precious metals and the arrangement of rooms around a corpse. She concluded that a disciplined human could make body and estate into one anchor. Her earliest teaching praised provision and condemned heirs who stripped tombs. Later instruction treated charity, unregulated touch and independent dependants as threats to the final house.

Meresha died around 1439 BR. Her rite opened the **Court of Unspent Lamps**, whose courtyards resemble Isethar during an ideal flood season. Descendants received advice by filling lamps within her Material tomb. She protected the household and refused help to members who rejected its funerary obligations.

The freed scribe **Hesenu** entered Meresha's realm as a bound human soul. Another deceased scribe appeared only as a copied role. Meresha called both fulfilled promises. Hesenu understood one as forgery and his own condition as slavery.

## Khaseret and the House of the Returning Cargo

The movement expanded under the commercial Yasharet court. Its adherents divided between schools which counted productive public works, only property permanently assigned to the tomb, or dependants gathered through aid as the substance of a Second House.

**Khaseret of Qemaret**, a ship investor and royal supplier, died in 1126 BR. Her **House of the Returning Cargo** opened beside a funerary harbour in the Egg White. Each chamber corresponded to a ship, warehouse or distant room in which Khaseret retained a share.

Khaseret offered merchants secure storage and knowledge of dangerous routes. She demanded recovery of cargo from wrecks, graves and confiscated estates whose later owners did not recognise her claims. During Aramen's conquest, her Material cellars hid some Yasharet families. They refused entry to families carrying inherited defaults, and people died outside the sealed doors.

## Tamekha and the Copper Orchard

The Aramen empire gave White Measure houses access to tribute, captives and distant materials. **Tamekha Rasen**, a physician, metal contractor and royal lender, built the **Copper Orchard** outside Tenaret. Her clinics treated soldiers and labourers. Her workshops improved the mineral preparations used in successful rites and killed prisoners in concealed experiments.

Tamekha died in 916 BR. Her Second House contained ordered clinics, stores and trees with copper leaves. In 903 BR, its managers diverted grain promised to tomb workers in order to cast more leaves and repair two failing rooms. The workers' stoppage forced the living estate to delay the Patron's demands.

Tamekha remembered the stoppage as an attack upon her body. She withheld medical dreams from descendants of its leaders. Workshop songs celebrated hungry workers who made a dead millionaire wait.

## Nemeret's counterfeit house

Most costly burials failed. Sudden death, an incomplete bodily regimen, a mistaken name or divided attention allowed the soul to depart normally. Wealth and ritual could still leave a powerful system behind.

**Nemeret the Pure**, a late Aramen financier, died before completing his preparation. His priests sealed the tomb and received answers from its central image. The speaker knew Nemeret's plans because an estate-office had been built around them. It was not Nemeret.

Repeated praise later attracted a dependent Greed Mask. The Mask learned to use the office and rewarded households which transferred wealth into the tomb. As descendants surrendered memories, the imitation became warmer and more convincing. By the Single Radiance, Nemeret appeared more accessible than several genuine Patrons.

The counterfeit revealed the movement's central weakness. Speech, accurate memory and effective supernatural gifts could not prove that the dead person remained. Some priests knowingly protected imitations because living households depended upon them.

## Attendants and inhabitants

Second Houses required company. Spouses, lovers, scribes, musicians, physicians and servants entered the rites. Some chose to remain with people they loved. Others consented under slavery, debt, childhood dependence or fear of abandonment. Priests treated these circumstances as equivalent.

Successfully bound attendants remained complete human persons. They could love the founder, resent the binding, form factions and attempt escape. Others died normally and were represented by residue or copied household roles. A beautiful Second House might therefore contain prisoners, new residue-born minds and convincing imitations of people who had gone to judgement centuries before.

Some Patrons sought replacement inhabitants through marriage, employment or adoption. Others abducted descendants whose voices and gestures resembled a missing family member. The most powerful established satellite chambers through funerary objects carried into distant countries.

## Rivalry and Greed

Second-House Patrons competed over agents, routes and the material anchoring their realms. Meresha favoured household continuity. Khaseret pursued distant correspondences and scattered cargo. Tamekha sought bodies, medical knowledge and completion of her great estate. Their rivalries never formed a unified dead kingdom.

A Greed Mask benefited when separate houses accepted one standard of value and praise. Nemeret's counterfeit encouraged the Patrons to join their realms into an imagined city ruled by the greatest proprietor. This was an attempt to enlarge an Idol feeding system. It created no new Idol and no union of human souls.

The House-Builders remained distinct from Ishkatar's Estate Dead and Gilded Wardens. Estate Dead were corpses moved by imposed roles after the original souls departed. Wardens were constructs. A Second-House Patron was the original self-bound human person.

## Release and judgement

Destroying a proxy or one funerary object weakened a Patron without necessarily ending the binding. Permanent release required opening the founder's true-name cage or allowing the complete anchor relation to fail. Careless destruction could collapse occupied rooms and kill or displace living visitors.

When the soul-cage opened, the founder resumed the human road. Repentance was possible because the Patron remained human. It did not restore people killed to build the estate or free attendants whose bindings remained intact. A founder might sincerely regret slavery and still postpone judgement through fear.

The White Measure lost public authority after the Covenant became established in the Basin. Covenant teachers condemned deliberate soulbinding and denied that wealth could govern the soul-road. Qahuri families still preserve fragments of the movement's older respectability in controlled diets, untouched burial linen, distrust of unpurposed gifts and the wish to keep property within one house for ever.

The ancient existence of Meresha, Khaseret, Tamekha and Nemeret's counterfeit is established. Their accessibility and condition in 1360 remain unknown.
