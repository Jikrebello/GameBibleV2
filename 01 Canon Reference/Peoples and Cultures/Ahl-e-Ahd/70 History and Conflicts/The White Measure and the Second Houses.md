# The White Measure and the Second Houses

The **White Measure** is the name given to a Qahuri mortuary discipline that treated accumulated wealth as the substance of an immortal household. Its followers restrained appetite, cultivated profitable work and built elaborate necropolises called **Second Houses**. A small number completed the discipline's final rite, binding the founder's human soul to an embalmed body and opening a private realm in the Egg White.

Successful founders remained the same human persons after bodily death. Their private realms allowed them to counsel descendants, send proxies and lease shelter, knowledge or power. This made them Patrons of the Egg White and liches in the older sense of the word: human souls deliberately held from their proper road.

## Teaching and discipline

House-Builders taught that uncontrolled appetite scattered a person's life. Food, touch, sex, anger, waste and careless generosity created claims through which the world divided the self. Discipline gathered the person into one design. Wealth acquired without indulgence supplied ordered substance from which the final house could be made.

Initiates practised periods of sexual abstinence and avoided casual touch during trade and ritual preparation. They wore clean, undyed linen and ate food whose origin could be traced. Their final years included medicines containing small quantities of gold, copper, salts and mineral resins. Correct preparation improved preservation of the body. Errors caused pain, tremors, sterility, madness and early death.

The discipline produced able merchants, physicians, appraisers and administrators. White Measure partnerships financed ships, workshops and grain storage. Apprentices learned calculation, foreign measures and the properties of oils, cloth and metals. Their public usefulness helped the movement survive hostility from kings and temples.

Prosperity also became evidence of virtue. Successful adherents attributed wealth to order. Poverty suggested concealed indulgence, generosity or impurity. Aid generally took the form of investment, employment or incorporation into a stronger household. A beneficiary received food and training while acquiring obligations intended to survive death.

## The final rite

A Second House joined the founder's body, true name, necropolis, wealth, witnesses and return tether. Mineral treatment and embalming prepared the corpse. The tomb reproduced a perfected household in real rooms. Precious material embedded in its walls and furnishings supplied metaphysical mass. As the founder's final breath failed, witnesses closed the true name into the anchor and opened its correspondence with the Egg White.

When the rite succeeded, the founder awoke within a genuine demiplane. Its local laws reflected the planned household. A merchant's hall might require exchange for every object taken. A military founder might assign rank to every guest. The embalmed corpse remained the innermost soul-cage and most powerful Material manifestation.

Each realm required upkeep. Lamps were filled, names recited, damaged images repaired and pieces of the physical endowment kept in correspondence with Astral rooms. Neglect caused chambers to contract or disappear. Stolen funerary metal could wound the founder and weaken the return tether.

A founder became a Patron once the realm could lease something beyond itself. Services included guarded storage, dreams of floods or prices, preservation of memories, occult teaching and temporary refuge. The Material agents receiving these gifts also repaired tombs, recovered lost anchor pieces and carried out choices the dead household could not make alone.

## Meresha and the Court of Unspent Lamps

**Meresha of Isethar** founded the movement during the first Twin-Reed division. She belonged to a funerary-linen household and learned to trace cloth, resin and payments across hostile crowns. A day's sail upriver the statues of the Senareth kings governed Tenaret through the priests who carried their answers, and Meresha's household sold those priests their linen. During the famine of about 1468 BR, she financed grain boats and took workshop shares from families unable to repay her. Her stores fed labourers and enlarged her authority over them.

Meresha studied a damaged Ishkatari receiving plate which reacted to personal names, precious metals and the arrangement of rooms around a corpse. She concluded that a disciplined human could make body and estate into one anchor. Her earliest teaching praised provision and condemned heirs who stripped tombs. Later instruction treated charity, unregulated touch and independent dependants as threats to the final house.

Meresha died around 1439 BR. Her rite opened the **Court of Unspent Lamps**, whose courtyards resemble Isethar during an ideal flood season. Descendants received advice by filling lamps within her Material tomb. She protected the household and refused help to members who rejected its funerary obligations.

The freed scribe **Hesenu** entered Meresha's realm as a bound human soul. Another deceased scribe appeared only as a copied role. Meresha called both fulfilled promises. Hesenu understood one as forgery and his own condition as slavery.

## Khaseret and the House of the Returning Cargo

The movement expanded under the commercial Yasharet court. Its adherents divided between schools which counted productive public works, only property permanently assigned to the tomb, or dependants gathered through aid as the substance of a Second House.

**Khaseret of Qemaret**, a ship investor and royal supplier, died in 1126 BR. Her **House of the Returning Cargo** opened beside a funerary harbour in the Egg White. Each chamber corresponded to a ship, warehouse or distant room in which Khaseret retained a share.

Khaseret offered merchants secure storage and knowledge of dangerous routes. She demanded recovery of cargo from wrecks, graves and confiscated estates whose later owners did not recognise her claims. Her stewards lent to the Yasharet crown against its grain fleet, its store courts and the river-gate tolls. When two low floods around 1084 BR left the pledge unharvested and the king sealed his stores against her, the House's factor **Aramen of Sakhret** fed the unpaid southern garrison from its warehouses and took Qemaret around 1076 to recover what was owed. Her Material cellars sheltered clients whose accounts were clear, some Yasharet cousins among them. They refused entry to families carrying inherited defaults, and people died outside the sealed doors. The last Yasharet king, **Iddar**, was walled alive into the House's harbour vault and entered in its ledger as returned cargo. Qemaret singers say he still keeps the House's accounts of the river gates; House-Builders say he paid and went on.

Aramen married Iddar's sister. The crown their heirs held spent its first generation paying the House, and its conquests paid the dead long after. Four centuries on, the House's most famous borrower was a foreign king, Eryndor III of Ortheia, in 695 BR.

## Tamekha and the Copper Orchard

The Aramen empire gave White Measure houses access to tribute, captives and distant materials. **Tamekha Rasen**, a physician, metal contractor and royal lender, built the **Copper Orchard** outside Tenaret. Her clinics treated soldiers and labourers. Her workshops improved the mineral preparations used in successful rites and killed prisoners in concealed experiments.

Tamekha died around 916 BR. Her Second House contained ordered clinics, stores and trees with copper leaves. In 903 BR, its managers diverted grain promised to tomb workers in order to cast more leaves and repair two failing rooms. The workers' stoppage forced the living estate to delay the Patron's demands.

Tamekha remembered the stoppage as an attack upon her body. She withheld medical dreams from descendants of its leaders. Workshop songs celebrated hungry workers who made a dead millionaire wait.

By then the dead were the Basin's greatest landlords. Second Houses lent grain and copper for the crown's campaigns and took repayment in fields, quarries and the villages that worked them. Land that passed to a necropolis left the royal levy, because its owner was dead, and still owed upkeep, because some owners were awake. When the Aramen kings ran out of living land to give their soldiers, garrisons sent to take up allotments found the fields already tilled for a tomb. The empire broke apart around 858 BR with the best of its land in the hands of the dead (see [[Ancient Qahur and the Twin-Reed Crowns]]).

## Nemeret's counterfeit house

Most costly burials failed. Sudden death, an incomplete bodily regimen, a mistaken name or divided attention allowed the soul to depart normally. Wealth and ritual could still leave a powerful system behind.

**Nemeret the Pure**, a late Aramen financier, died before completing his preparation. His priests sealed the tomb and went on receiving answers from its central image, which knew his plans and spoke with his memories. Qemaret's masters later held that the speaker was only an estate-office built around those plans, and that Nemeret had gone the road of all the dead. Basin exorcists added that the praise of his descendants had drawn a Greed Mask into the office, which learned to reward households that moved their wealth into the tomb. As descendants surrendered memories, the voice grew warmer and more convincing. By the Single Radiance, Nemeret seemed more accessible than several founders whose houses no one doubted.

The case exposed the movement's central weakness. Speech, accurate memory and effective supernatural gifts could not prove that the dead person remained. Some priests knowingly protected tombs they believed empty because living households depended upon them.

## The king of Meheret

The movement's strangest founder was raised as a weapon against it. The metal in the walls of the Second Houses was older than the houses. Most of it had been dug from buried Giant estates and recast, and under the casting it kept its first owners' marks. **Hesren III**, who took the middle crown around 842 BR, dug the seal of one such estate out of the vaults under Meheret and set its gilded servants on the Basin's tombs. Garrison songs say the voice in Nemeret's tomb told him where to dig. The procession the middle river called the Single Radiance clawed marked plate out of necropolis walls for eleven years, the crown gave the cleared fields to its garrisons, and Tamekha's medical dreams stopped along the whole middle river.

The estate claimed whoever wore its seal, and Hesren had hung it on his son **Deshren** at six years old. The boy wasted. Around 829, at about seventeen, he sent for the Copper Orchard's masters himself, returned the Orchard's fields and began the regimen of gold, salts and resins in the estate-house at Meheret. The metal stripped from the Basin's tombs, heaped in its vaults, became his endowment, and the tallies of the cleared land became the substance of his house. He died within the year. The masters performed the final rite and sealed the inner courts with the seal inside. The Radiance coiled itself around the palace and stopped.

Within a season the dead prince's stewards were collecting rent from the soldiers who farmed the cleared fields, and from his father. Deshren was never crowned, and Qahuri songs call him the king of Meheret because a king paid him rent. After Hesren's death the stewards claimed the middle crown for their proprietor, until the boat households and soldier-farmers rose under Zaheret of Tenaret in 815. Around 810 the Arqeshite prince **Ashkur Ubar** entered the sealed courts with three of the dead prince's former stewards and came out alone on the fourth morning, carrying a tally that made his father's house the collector of Deshren's rents east of the river. House-Builders count Deshren among the founders whose rite succeeded and call his realm the Court at Noon. Exorcists compare the stewards' rent demands to Nemeret's voice. The palace has stayed shut since Ashkur.

## The king at Delkos

The most famous rite of the White Measure was performed on a foreigner. In 695 BR Eryndor III of Ortheia fed his army up the river on grain from the store courts of Khaseret's House of the Returning Cargo and pledged in return the right of her masters to prepare his body. In 685 he came back from the farther east with a wound that would not close. **Neresh of Qemaret**, a Qahuri officer in his army and the House's man in the camp, brought masters of the discipline to Arqesh under the pledge, and **Serevan of Delkos** paid for the regimen of gold, salts and resins. A founder normally prepared for years. Eryndor had months. He died in 684 aboard the flagship of the allied fleet in the roads of Nemeru, waiting to sail home, and the masters performed the final rite in the ship's hold.

The ship's crew, descendants of the Belyra deportees, sailed the embalmed body to Serevan's harbour at Delkos, together with the gilded images of the Ubar kings that Eryndor had kept as regalia. Within months a voice in the tomb-hall spoke in Eryndor's accent, knew his companions' secrets, declared him alive and heirless and promised provinces. For nearly thirty years its promises helped keep the [[Wars of the Inheritance]] burning. Around 657 BR, after the voice refused Neresh a grant, the same Qemaret masters testified that their rite had failed in the ship's hold, with no house to close him into, and compared the speaker to Nemeret's counterfeit. Serevan's grandson walled up the tomb.

The case gave the White Measure a reputation it never lost outside the Basin. Atherian and Vashari writers called any convincing speaking tomb "a Delkos voice". House-Builders argued for generations over whether their masters had told the truth in 657 or simply changed sides when Neresh did.

## Attendants and inhabitants

Second Houses required company. Spouses, lovers, scribes, musicians, physicians and servants entered the rites. Some chose to remain with people they loved. Others consented under slavery, debt, childhood dependence or fear of abandonment. Priests treated these circumstances as equivalent.

Successfully bound attendants could love the founder, resent the binding, form factions and attempt escape. Others died normally and were represented by residue or copied household roles. A beautiful Second House might therefore contain prisoners, new residue-born minds and convincing imitations of people who had gone to judgement centuries before.

Some Patrons sought replacement inhabitants through marriage, employment or adoption. Others abducted descendants whose voices and gestures resembled a missing family member. The most powerful established satellite chambers through funerary objects carried into distant countries.

## Rivalry and Greed

Second-House Patrons competed over agents, routes and the material anchoring their realms. Meresha favoured household continuity. Khaseret pursued distant correspondences and scattered cargo. Tamekha sought bodies, medical knowledge and completion of her great estate. Their rivalries never formed a unified dead kingdom.

Exorcists warned that a Greed Mask profited whenever separate houses accepted one standard of value and praise, and Nemeret's tomb urged the Patrons to join their realms into an imagined city ruled by the greatest proprietor.

## Release and judgement

Destroying a proxy or one funerary object weakened a Patron without necessarily ending the binding. Permanent release required opening the founder's true-name cage or allowing the complete anchor relation to fail. Careless destruction could collapse occupied rooms and kill or displace living visitors.

When the soul-cage opened, the founder resumed the human road. Repentance was possible because the Patron remained human. It did not restore people killed to build the estate or free attendants whose bindings remained intact. A founder might sincerely regret slavery and still postpone judgement through fear.

The White Measure lost public authority after the Covenant became established in the Basin. Covenant teachers condemned deliberate soulbinding and denied that wealth could govern the soul-road. Qahuri families still preserve fragments of the movement's older respectability in controlled diets, untouched burial linen, distrust of unpurposed gifts and the wish to keep property within one house for ever.
