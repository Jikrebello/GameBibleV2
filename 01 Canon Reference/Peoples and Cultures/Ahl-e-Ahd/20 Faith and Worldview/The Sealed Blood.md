# The Sealed Blood

The Sealed Blood holds that Mujalib entrusted succession through his daughter Ruqaiya and that no scribal college could replace the Prophet's household witness. Its living authorities are the Wasiyin: guides claiming descent, instruction, or appointment through Ruqaiya's hidden line.

The tradition's spiritual centre is the Mourning of the Wells, which repeats Husan's reading at Bir Qassim: the safe-conduct recited clause by clause, each answered with *And yet the well was dry*, and then the thirty-five names. The first is Ruqaiya, who died of thirst beside a full well that her father's bound servant would not draw from. The last are the drovers who were killed for giving the household water. Worship emphasises loyalty under persecution, hospitality to fugitives, lament, moral intention, and suspicion of state-controlled testimony. Sealed-Blood villages draw water by hand and let no bound office keep their wells, and on the day of the Mourning every household sets a filled skin outside its door for whoever passes. Husan lived, veiled, at Qazmarid until 631, and the Wasiyin have gone veiled before strangers ever since. Qazmarid's Caliph-Wasi claims to unite temporal rule with the concealed succession.

Sealed-Blood communities maintain lineage courts, mourning houses, shrine kitchens, and travelling teachers. Minorities elsewhere range from quiet congregations to revolutionary networks.

Its central temptation is to treat claimed blood and inherited suffering as proof against accountability. Qazmari reformers insist that Bir Qassim condemns unanswerable power even when exercised by Ruqaiya's descendants, and point out that the people the Mourning names last were on nobody's roll.
