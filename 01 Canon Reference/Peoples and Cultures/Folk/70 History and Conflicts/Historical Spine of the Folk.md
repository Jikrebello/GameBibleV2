# Historical Spine of the Folk

The Folk are related Orphaned peoples whose communities reassembled across the southern forests, river valleys, coasts and mountain approaches after the Giant collapse. Their history developed through independent hearths, clans and regional leagues, followed by long contact with Atherian settlers, Menhir courts, Grobi migrations and the successor states of Eclessia. In 1360 their lands still contain old-rite Enaidfae, converted Chapel-Folk and communities whose loyalties cross political and religious boundaries.

## Orphaned Reassembly and First Hearths

- **c. 2300-1900 BR:** subject villages, fugitives, local revolt communities and survivors of collapsing Giant client states entered, remained within or reconnected across the southern forests, coasts and mountain corridors. Escaped lesser Giants and giant-blooded prisoners from Nhaldur contributed war leaders and ancestry.
- **c. 2050-1900 BR:** separate proto-Atherian war-settlements reached occupied southern gates, river corridors and coasts. Their relations with different Folk communities included massacre, slavery, hostage exchange, land seizure, alliance, trade, intermarriage, and the destruction or absorption of forts. Forts took farming households with the fields; Folk memory calls it the time "the fed came for the farmers".
- **c. 1900-1400 BR:** Belathi, Hartheni and Odrani identities became legible; hearth-law, annual All-Things, winter stores, ringworks, grove boundaries and formal Grobi watches became durable. The Withheld Season's High Hunt in the Tidewood–Gale-Crown watershed left release cuts as southern custom; the hall that faced the Grobi divided Folk and Ättar cousins in command. - **c. 1400-800 BR:** river leagues, hillfort confederacies, coastal kindreds, and old-rite Enaidfae networks became regional powers.

## Atherian Centuries

- **c. 800-350 BR:** Atherian city-states renewed and greatly enlarged older contact through trade, recruitment, colonization and campaigns in the northern Folk lands.
- **c. 390–379 BR:** [[The Southern Campaigns of Teren Casvilar]] drew separate river and forest coalitions into conquest and resistance. Morveth sought support for local crossings; Drenoc led the defence at Kellovar, which fell in 383 after its households had crossed the river-court's ford on their tokens; Drenoc entered the ford and was not seen again. Velcara survived captivity and returned to rebuild storage through her maternal connections. The mutilations at Darneth in 380 left enduring burdens in local households.
- **c. 350 BR onward:** imperial roads and forts penetrated the marches. Some clans became federates and auxiliaries; some strongholds fell; others retained treaty autonomy.
- **0-540 AR:** converted and Atherianized Folk entered imperial institutions while old-rite territories remained beyond consistent rule. Decadence brought renewed raids, secessions, and abandoned frontier works.
- **c. 368–379 AR:** since Cassian's market treaties allied Folk guides had led imperial columns over the river-courts' fords, wading in first with a household token of iron, bone or plaited hair tied at the wrist, or of silver for a chief's house. Emperor Lucar's chaplains took the tokens as idols, burned the bone and hair ones in the fort yards and left the silver for officers to sell. In the hard winters of 369–371 the Grobi came down the frozen rivers and the guideless forts watched allied villages burn. The garrisons then crossed by the fords' older toll, a person delivered to the far bank who could not come back, and paid it in children taken from the allied households of their own districts; stewards sold some of the children. From 372 the fort-district households slipped away across the fords on hidden tokens, towards their children on the far banks. In spring 377 the last of them crossed below Kellovar by night; Lucar's army followed at dawn and drowned. Lucar rode out dry with a confiscated silver otter on his horse's breast-strap, was three days a guest at the Otter house's fire, and was killed by his own rearguard when Cathra, the mother of the house, sent him home. Folk elders say the river exacted its toll and judged nothing. Leaders who negotiated with his successor Vestian were settled under their own elders by 379. ## Fall and Successor Frontiers

- **905 AR onward:** the Fall removed the last common imperial frontier command. Clans reclaimed settlements and roads but lost dependable grain relief and coordinated winter defence.
- **961-985 AR:** Adalmar's eastern consolidation turned the southern border into a program of settlement, baptism, and crown title. The eastern bishops' registers still listed the imperial frontier forts and estates abandoned after 905, and Adalmar granted them to his Markish lords. The Green March clans had held the same ground for three generations by seer-oath and grove boundary. The registers said one thing and the groves another.
- **985-994 AR:** the **Folk Wars** combined settlement drives, forced baptism, clan civil war and missionary expansion. In **987** the Green March war-leader **Weylan** took baptism and entered his clans' fields in the bishops' register, and was named Oath-Bent.
- **993 AR:** the **Drowning at Wolfsmere** killed four thousand oath-keepers and seers, called to the lake under safe-conduct to recite the grove boundaries. The lake became a standing leftward thin space.
- **994 AR:** the **Standing of the Water**. The lake rose and took the Markish shore-fort.
- **999 AR:** the **Peace of the Bent Oak**. Weylan won the lake in the bishops' court. His descendants became the Bent Oak, the most prominent Chapel-Folk lineage.

### The Folk Wars and the lake, 985–999 AR

The clans fought the Markish settlers from ringforts, fords and forest edges for eight years, and fought one another over the baptized. Their title to the land lived in their oath-keepers, who could recite every grove boundary in the Green March; the settlers' title lived in the bishops' register. In 987 the war-leader Weylan took baptism and had his clans' fields written into that register under his own name, to hold them by the settlers' law. The clans named him Oath-Bent. In the summer of 993 Adalmar offered to make the two records one: the oath-keepers and seers would recite the boundaries at Wolfsmere, the Green March's oath-lake, and his clerks would write them down. Weylan carried the king's safe-conduct through the forest, and four thousand came. They recited for nine days, and what they recited covered every fort and field the king had granted away. On the tenth day Adalmar had the four thousand bound and drowned in the lake "to drown the oath with its keepers", and burned the sheets on the shore. Weylan watched under guard. Nine seers were held under and did not drown. They were pulled out breathing at dusk, pale-eyed and cold-handed, and from them descends the Lakekeeper line.

In the winter of 994, on a windless night, the lake stood up. A wall of water came down on the Lake Watch, the fort Adalmar had built on the shore, and in spring its garrison was found on the ice and its timbers in the reeds. The nine walked to the water's edge, and the lake lay down. No Markish lord took up a grant beyond the lake again, and the Eclessian frontier has stopped beside it ever since.

Weylan neither went back to the groves nor forgave the king. He was a baptized lord with fields in the register and a royal safe-conduct broken in front of him, and he sued Adalmar in the bishops' own court. In 999 the bishops, whose priests would not go near the shore, gave judgement against their king. Under the **Peace of the Bent Oak** the grants south of the lake were struck from the register, and the march charters forbade any bell within hearing of Wolfsmere, any boat on its water and any iron in it. The Lakekeepers had already laid down the same law. Old-rite clans say Weylan led four thousand to the water and kept the drowner's God; the Chapel-Folk answer that he made the drowner's own law fence the lake, that a bent oak remains alive and that the lake still sleeps.

### The marches, 1008–1232 AR

- **1060-1067 AR:** the **First Southern Crusade** carried marcher rule south after the Partition of Drossfeld, around the lake country rather than through it. Folk captives were sent north to the silver mines of the middle march. Their children were among the slaves who rose in the Heldenberg shafts around 1116 and founded Korrheim's first cantons with the Oath of the Shafts in 1124.
- **1101-1103 AR:** in the **Two Winters**, with ash-grey skies and no summer between, Markish penitents calling themselves the Debtors of the Lake went south to drown themselves in Wolfsmere for Adalmar's debt. The Lakekeepers turned them back at the shore and told them the lake did not want more. The Folk say the sky owed a debt in those years, and paid it with the Apology harvest of 1103.
- **1158-1163 AR:** the **Great Southern Purge**. Sylvan marcher lords and the Knights of Saint Aveline, under a relic-court ruling that the seers' shrines held unexamined fire, destroyed seers and shrine settlements and cut the boundary groves. The clans remember it as a campaign against land memory. In the midwinter of 1163 riders whose breath made no smoke went through the marcher camps on three nights. They took an Aveline chapter and the Folk households nearest the cut groves alike; the clans' seers say the boundaries walked back and took whoever stood on them. The Purge ended, and for a generation the missioners of the Elsin tradition, who opposed conversion by the sword, won the argument over Folk policy against the Aveline tradition.
- **By 1232 AR:** the frontier was a gradient from parish to clan-land, stopping at Wolfsmere. Old-rite clans rebuilt their seer-lines after the Purge; Chapel-Folk communities lived under the Bent Oak precedent; Markish settlers held the old registered forts; and every party still needed the others when the Grobi moved north.

### Plague, captives and reform, 1232–1360 AR

- **1232-1247 AR:** the **Grey Sweat** emptied whole parish belts in the marches. Runaways from Virelia's bound villages, discharged soldiers, Folk families and later Freeflame exiles resettled the gaps under titles that contradicted one another, and the missing registers have kept the marches a refuge ever since.
- **1258-1261 AR:** the **March of the Redeemer's Sword**. Southern lords whose estates the plague had emptied marched into the deep cold forest to take Folk captives as labour. Winter and the Grobi broke the march, and the clans count it among the few times the Grobi did them a service.
- **1290-1343 AR:** Freeflame preachers reached the march villages and found Chapel-Folk listeners who liked a church without a distant bishop. After the Korrheim Accord the Sylvan Marches took in Unsworn congregations that every signatory hunted.
- **1352 AR:** at **Vessing**, a hill parish of the southern marches, three people burying a runaway's child outside the churchyard wall saw a figure of banked fire in the sloe-field. The Sanctum has not ruled, and the clans have their own opinion of what walks in sloe-fields.

## Present Inheritance

By 1360 AR, the Eclessian frontier remains a gradient. Parish law, clan jurisdiction, ducal charters, and court bargains overlap. The old-rite clans dispute land with settlers and extraction companies; Chapel-Folk dispute whether the Church accepts them without erasing them; every faction needs cooperation when the Grobi move north.

## Tidewood's Regional History

[[History of the Tidewood Coast]] follows one coastal neighbourhood from Orphaned survival to 1360. ## Historical Atlas — wider inheritance

The Folk descend principally from Orphaned communities which rebuilt forests, fields and clan worlds outside the Glass. Atherian war-settlers encountered rival clans, surviving remnant towns and households with their own ideas of inheritance and place.

Conquest could destroy a fort, take hostages, produce a mixed harbour or redirect a clan's marriage network. Ark-derived settlers brought a particular witness and useful crafts. Folk societies themselves practised raiding, captivity and exclusion.

Menhir courts remained dangerous neighbours. Their gifts and exact prices shaped local hunting, fosterage, mining and seasonal customs. Conversion later produced Chapel-Folk as well as resistance, while Enaidfae remained an old-rite identity. The Folk and the Ättar shared ancestry and the annual All-Thing tradition, but geography, enemies and political choices made their histories diverge.
