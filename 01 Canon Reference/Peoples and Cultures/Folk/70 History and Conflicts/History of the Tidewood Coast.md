# History of the Tidewood Coast

The history of [[Tidewood Coast|Tidewood]] has no single founding voyage. People survived at river mouths, behind storm ridges and in the forests above ruined Giant works. Some had fled those works; some had supplied them; others had lived beyond their masters' dependable reach. Their descendants became the coastal Folk through generations of marriage, shelter, feud and shared labour. They did not come out of the Glass Desert.

The **Three Beaches**—[[Brackenmouth]], [[Rookstrand]] and [[Keldscar and Gannet Head|Keldscar]]—provide a connected view of this long history. The same sheltered water, hauling beach and exposed headland recur in very different struggles. Their stories are especially well preserved because their inhabitants kept visiting, marrying and quarrelling with one another. Other Tidewood communities have histories outside this account.

## The shore after the Giants, c. 2300–1900 BR

The collapse did not empty the shore at once. A stone loading wall above the Bracken estuary remained usable after the inland extraction estate stopped sending commands. Families initially camped inside its service sheds. When spring floods backed against blocked drains, the sheds became traps. Survivors shifted onto the shingle ridge and pulled apart sections of the wall to let water escape.

That choice shaped the later settlement. People needed the old stone for hearths and retaining banks, but they stopped treating the Giant landing as the safest place to live. Brackenmouth grew beside the ruin rather than inside it. Its narrow lanes followed successive ridges of gravel; its earliest storehouses stood high enough to keep fish and seed above ordinary floods.

Rookstrand offered less protection but more room to haul boats. There, surviving workshop families worked with households that knew the offshore fishing grounds. At Keldscar, people sheltered boats behind a low rocky spur while keeping dwellings above the winter spray. A household unable to keep grain could exchange dried fish or timber for it. Boats connected places that the flooded forest separated.

The earliest generations lost skills as well as recovering them. They could reuse a Giant metal fitting without being able to make another. A broken imported blade might be turned into several working knives. Bone hooks, wooden pegs and carefully fitted planks remained valuable long after iron became common in the larger yards. Later songs about a coast living entirely on gifts from the sea leave out the cold work of gathering fuel and carrying food uphill.

## Winter ties and the Three Beaches, c. 1900–1400 BR

As permanent halls multiplied, the coast became divided by who could feed a crew through winter. Brackenmouth's estuary households controlled sheltered landings and smokehouses. Rookstrand's builders required timber and provisions from beyond their own beach. Keldscar's lookout families could warn both settlements of an approaching fleet but could not grow enough food to endure a long blockade alone.

These dependencies produced the early **Three Beaches league**, conventionally dated to c. 1470 BR. It provided escort boats and winter shelter to participating households. A common expedition still needed crews, food and agreement from each shore. The league never ruled all Tidewood, and it sometimes ceased operating for years after a feud.

The related people who became the Ättar did not depart in one farewell fleet. Marriages and visits continued while their hall coalitions, southern enemies and later pact-kindreds pulled them toward a different political life. Tidewood households followed some relatives into the halls and received others back after quarrels. The coast's own seers remained influential without accepting one chief for the sea or forest.

The [[G-67 - The Marks of Necessary Release|withheld-season tradition]] came down from the Tidewood–Gale-Crown watershed during these formative centuries. Its release cuts spread along timber paths and marriage connections. At Brackenmouth they eventually marked the opening of spring storehouses; at Keldscar they appeared on burial cloth. The story of the woman, her mare and her missing son belonged to the inland catastrophe, not to a shipwreck newly claimed by the coastal league.

## Maelin and the dry fleet, c. 1280–1240 BR

Early Atherian traders brought bronze fittings, worked cloth and access to other markets. Their captains also took captives and sought places from which they could command a coast. One sea-house obtained a winter berth at Rookstrand by helping local rivals against a raiding fleet. By the time its captain **Aulon of the Red Sail** assumed command, temporary quarters had become a fenced compound, armed collectors and a demand for timber and labour.

[[Maelin Ropeburn]] had returned from captivity with knowledge of those ships. She first worked for the compound, where interpreting and repairs paid better than repairing nets. When the collectors began taking workers' children as security for timber deliveries, she organized boatwrights, estuary suppliers and dissident shore households against it.

The [[Taking of the Dry Fleet]], c. 1260 BR, began on land. Maelin's allies took away the rollers needed to launch the beached Atherian ships and opened a rear entrance through a working shed. They seized the compound before its main crews returned. Aulon escaped seaward; several surrendering retainers did not. Maelin ordered them killed rather than risk a rescue, and a fire set during the attack burned a locked sleeping shed before its captive labourers could all escape.

The victors gained ships, tools and access to foreign markets. They also inherited hungry former captives, widowed residents and workers whom their allies still wanted to hold as property. Maelin forced the release of the compound's surviving labourers but could not make every participating household free its own slaves. Some freed people joined Rookstrand's yards; some demanded passage away from the coast that had nearly killed them twice.

The **Tarred Hand** became the name of the mixed working kindred formed around these yards. Later ceremonial genealogies made it an ancient local bloodline. Its varied origins remained easier to see in the different names carved on family chests and in the households whose first known ancestor had arrived in chains.

## The age of useful strangers, c. 1240–350 BR

Possession of captured ships did not create an enduring sea empire. The Three Beaches lacked the resources to maintain every hull, and several were dismantled for parts. Yet their builders kept experimenting. Atherian fastenings, Ättar experience of exposed crossings and local knowledge of hauling beaches met in yards where people argued over wood rather than ancestry.

By c. 620 BR, Rookstrand builders were known along this part of the coast for repairable cargo boats with working parts that could be replaced without stripping the whole hull. The development came through many workshops, not one miraculous inventor. It widened trade in salt fish and timber, and allowed crews to winter away from home more safely. Households with boats gained wealth; households that supplied unpaid sons and daughters did not necessarily share it.

The shore also became a place where a foreign sailor could remain. Marriage and adoption brought Atherian and Ättar relatives into coastal kin groups without making Tidewood an Ark-derived people. Jirahar visiting crews exchanged goods and route knowledge; their regular presence increased as later maritime networks grew. Stories of the [[The Boat Too Fine for Water|boat too fine for water]] mock the owners who admired a vessel's ornaments but would not trust a patched sail. The joke made sense in a working port long before anyone wrote down a favourite version.

## Imperial neighbours and coastal independence, c. 350 BR–400 AR

Imperial Atheria offered longer markets, military wages and demands that could arrive on more ships than the coast could defeat. Some shore leaders supplied pilots and timber. Others attacked supply vessels, then traded their cargo through a different inlet. Atherian officers repeatedly mistook a bargain with one Speaker for submission by the whole region.

Brackenmouth accepted a small purchasing station and seasonal guards. Rookstrand supplied repair crews under its own masters. Keldscar households refused a permanent garrison but sold pilotage. These arrangements waxed and collapsed with imperial attention. They did not form a coastal province with one governor.

Redeemer-faith first travelled here in people rather than conquering fleets: sailors, captives, married immigrants and returning workers. Small congregations existed by the late imperial centuries. Their members could be excluded from offerings on which their neighbours believed the catch depended. Conversely, imperial visitors sometimes demanded the burning of harmless family carvings as proof of conversion. Local quarrels outlived both kinds of visitor.

## Orven's profitable voyages, c. 400–540 AR

The weakening empire and the wider Ättar keel-taking opened opportunities for coastal captains. [[Orven Blackkeel]], born at Rookstrand in 471 AR, began as a repair worker on hired voyages. By his forties he commanded a mixed fleet whose captains shared booty but not a king. He paid crews reliably, brought iron and grain home, and bought the release of some Tidewood captives. These achievements remained the foundation of his reputation.

He also sold captives. In 514 he rejected a ransom offered for **Hadrun**, an Ättar pilot fostered in Keldscar, because a foreign buyer would pay more for Hadrun and his adult daughter **Tova** together. Hadrun had guided Orven's ships before falling into the hands of another crew; Tova worked on nets and sails and had children living ashore. The sale divided the fleet: some captains left, others accepted their share. Keldscar boats stopped guiding Orven through the inner approach.

In 519 the estranged captains intercepted him while he waited for repairs off the coast. Orven's ship burned in the fighting; he drowned after jumping from the stern. His enemies recovered much of its cargo and refused to distribute any of it to his household. Families who had relied on his winter grain went hungry. The feud did not sort the coast neatly into slavers and liberators.

Rookstrand songs long praised the captain who never paid a sailor late. Keldscar songs answered with the name of the man he had sold. Both survived because people still had reasons to sing them.

## The summer without wrecks, 710–712 AR

The coast was connected to the wider Long Frontier through trade and hired service, but its most remembered catastrophe of these years came from a small inlet below Gannet Head. Fish appeared there in unnatural abundance after offerings to the **Guest with Shark Teeth**. Wreck cargo paid for better boats and houses. When safer pilotage reduced ship losses, the extraordinary catch failed.

Seer **Iven Teethstring** persuaded several prosperous households to set false lights. Ships struck the outer rocks; salvagers killed survivors who could identify the lights. There were nets, kitchens and children to feed behind the crime, not an isolated monster's cave. [[Brenna Half-Sail]], whose family had accepted the early catch, recognized a wrecked ship's rigging because she had repaired it during a visit to Brackenmouth.

She warned the other harbours and helped carry an armed party to the inlet. During the struggle Iven's followers drowned a captive sailor at the feeding stone. Brenna's younger brother died hauling survivors through the surf. The party overturned the stone and broke the structures used to draw the wrecks' cargo into the offering place. Fish dispersed. Winter followed without the promised abundance.

[[The Summer Without Wrecks]] destroyed the inlet's prosperity and dispersed its surviving households. Brenna spent later years making ordinary fishing and rescue voyages; she did not become mistress of the sea. Some families renounced all dealings with the Court of Salt and Teeth. Others distinguished the Guest from the Net-Mother encountered elsewhere and continued their offerings. The disagreement remains part of coastal life.

## After the Fall: boats instead of a frontier command, 905–1052 AR

The Fall disrupted markets and naval protection on which independent coastal communities had nevertheless relied. Crews returned without wages. Refugees brought tools, illnesses, news and demands for food. Some families welcomed relatives; some stole their cargo before allowing them ashore. The Three Beaches remained populated, but no settlement escaped the loss of distant buyers and grain.

[[Edris Sailmaker]], born at Brackenmouth in 961, grew up amid this unreliable trade. She became known for making durable sails from unevenly worn cloth and for teaching widowed householders to mend their own. Her marriage into a Keld family kept her working between the estuary and the exposed coast. Her household also carried fish to winter volunteers travelling inland.

News of Wolfsmere in 993 sharpened distrust of foreign Church-backed settlement. Edris, herself Redeemer-faithful, continued working with old-rite neighbours. Some would eat at her fire but refused to attend her son's baptism. The coast mourned relatives and feared similar violence without becoming the site of the Wolfsmere massacre.

In the winter of 1002, ice and driving water trapped several households beyond Keldscar's normal landing. Edris accepted passage from the being called the **Drowned Ferryman**, surrendering her stored sailcloth to carry people out. The price stripped her workshop before the trading season. The rescued included a rival who had refused her family shelter years earlier. Rescue did not restore their friendship: they shared a boat, then rebuilt separate lives.

The cloth lost in that crossing became the beginning of a practical custom. Neighbours contributed worn sail panels to rebuild Edris's stock. Later gifts to the communal loft were made for repairs and rescues, not as offerings to the Ferryman. A patch of good cloth remained useful even to a sailor who believed none of the story.

## Timber wealth and the Beam War, 1052–1291 AR

Naval rebuilding and the growing markets of Sestaran cities increased demand for long straight timber. Coastal households could earn more by selling a few good trees than by fishing for a season. New hauling tracks reached beyond the groves normally cut for local boats. Iron tools became easier to obtain; some forest households bought better livestock and roofs. Others lost access to water and gathering grounds before seeing any payment.

The [[The Beam War|Beam War of 1287–1291]] began when a group of Reedwake timber sellers and a Sestaran purchasing house widened a hauling cut into a side channel above Brackenmouth. Log rafts battered fish traps and diverted water needed by the drying yards. The sellers paid armed guards to keep the timber moving. Fishers cut retaining ropes; the next release of logs killed workers below.

Rookstrand's shipmasters initially sided with the sellers because they wanted the tools and overseas orders. Keldscar supplied boats to the fishers. The conflict changed when a guard company burned a storehouse still occupied by the households sheltering inside it. Rookstrand crews refused further transport for the guards. Fighting and reprisal continued, but the purchasing house could no longer move timber cheaply enough to pay its soldiers.

The eventual settlement closed the side channel and divided permitted cutting grounds. It left dead workers, burned homes and money owed to families on opposing sides. The **Broken Slide**, still visible above Brackenmouth, is the hauling structure abandoned after the war. Children climb its lower timbers; elders argue about which side began the killings.

## The coast in 1360

The Three Beaches are again trading, arguing and sending volunteers inland. Reedwake Speaker **Mera Brinecoat** wants foreign orders that will keep the estuary fed without reopening the old cutting ground. **Haldren of the Tarred Hand** needs good timber for promised hulls and resents paying for another harbour's quarrel. **Yarra Keld**, a pilot and War-Keeper, demands repairs to winter shelters before supplying another season of escorts. None can bind all the households whose work they need.

There are Chapel-Folk, old-rite families and people who change the subject when a seer approaches. There are restored storehouses beside empty sites whose owners never returned. Poor crews still take dangerous cargoes; affluent households still tell stories of ancestors who knew when to refuse one. The future of the timber trade, the revived false lights and the sea powers' current demands is unsettled.

Tidewood's inheritance is not only its wounds. It is also a repaired boat passed to a younger household, children learning to read weather from adults who disagree about everything else, the smell of the first spring fish, and the rude cheer when an overproud owner's new hull finally touches water.
