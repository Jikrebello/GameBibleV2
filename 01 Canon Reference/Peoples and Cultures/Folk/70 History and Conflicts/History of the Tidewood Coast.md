# History of the Tidewood Coast

The history of the [[Tidewood Coast]] follows the development of Orphaned shore communities from the collapse of the Giant world to the trading settlements and coastal leagues of 1360 AR. Early inhabitants included fugitives from Giant works, families that had supplied those estates and people living beyond their masters' dependable reach. Fishing, the reuse of abandoned structures and exchange between scattered settlements supported their survival. Marriage and shared labour gradually formed the coastal Folk communities.

The **Three Beaches**—[[Brackenmouth]], [[Rookstrand]] and [[Keldscar and Gannet Head|Keldscar]]—provide a connected account of this development. Sheltered water, a broad hauling beach and an exposed pilotage coast gave each settlement a different role. Continued visits, marriages and disputes preserved their intertwined histories. Other Tidewood inlets developed their own relationships beyond this neighbourhood.

## The shore after the Giants, c. 2300–1900 BR

The abandonment of Giant estates left buildings, tools and working ground that surviving households could use. At the Bracken estuary, a stone loading wall remained serviceable after the inland extraction estate ceased sending commands. Families occupied its sheds until spring floods backed against blocked drains and trapped people in the lower buildings.

Survivors moved onto the shingle ridge and pulled apart sections of the wall to release the water. This decision determined the position of the later settlement. Brackenmouth's lanes followed successive gravel ridges, and early storehouses kept fish and seed above ordinary floods. Old masonry was taken into hearths and retaining banks, allowing useful material to be recovered from the structures the inhabitants had abandoned.

Rookstrand offered less shelter but more space for hauling vessels. Workshop families there joined households familiar with offshore fishing grounds. At Keldscar, boats could be drawn behind a rocky spur while dwellings stood above winter spray. Dried fish and timber were exchanged for grain, and boats maintained connections between places separated by flooded forest.

Recovery was uneven. Metal fittings and imported blades could be reused or divided into smaller tools even when the skills to replace them had been lost. Bone hooks, wooden pegs and fitted planks remained important as iron gradually returned to larger yards. Fuel gathering, food transport and the upkeep of boats required sustained work alongside the fishing celebrated in later songs of survival.

## Winter ties and the Three Beaches, c. 1900–1400 BR

As permanent halls multiplied, the ability to provision crews through winter became an important source of influence. Brackenmouth's households maintained sheltered landings and smokehouses. Rookstrand's builders required food and timber from beyond their beach. Keldscar's lookouts could warn both settlements of an approaching fleet, but their exposed shore supplied insufficient food for a prolonged blockade.

These dependencies encouraged the **Three Beaches league**, conventionally dated to about 1470 BR. Participating households provided escort boats and winter shelter. A common voyage required contributions of crews, food and agreement from each shore. Feuds sometimes suspended its activity for years, after which renewed needs for transport or protection could bring households together again.

Related communities farther along the wider Folk–Ättar world developed different hall coalitions and responses to southern enemies. Later pact-kindreds strengthened this divergence. Marriage and visiting continued throughout it: Tidewood households sent relatives to the halls and received people returning after quarrels. Coastal seers retained influence within their own communities.

The [[G-67 - The Marks of Necessary Release|withheld-season tradition]] spread from the Tidewood–Gale-Crown watershed through timber routes and marriages during these formative centuries. Its story of a woman, her mare and her missing son remained associated with the inland catastrophe. Release cuts acquired local uses on the coast, marking the spring opening of Brackenmouth's storehouses and appearing on burial cloth at Keldscar.

## Maelin and the dry fleet, c. 1280–1240 BR

Early Atherian traders brought bronze fittings, worked cloth and access to overseas markets. Some captains also took captives and sought secure bases along the coast. One sea-house obtained a winter berth at Rookstrand by assisting local rivals against a raiding fleet. Under Captain **Aulon of the Red Sail**, temporary quarters developed into a fenced compound supplied by armed collectors demanding timber and labour.

[[Maelin Ropeburn]], who had returned from captivity with knowledge of the sea-house's ships and speech, worked there as an interpreter and repair worker. The employment paid better than much of the work available locally. When collectors began taking workers' children as security for timber deliveries, she organised opposition among boatwrights, estuary suppliers and shore households.

The [[Taking of the Dry Fleet]], around 1260 BR, relied on access to the working compound while Aulon and the principal sailing crews were absent. Workers removed launching rollers from beneath beached ships, and suppliers withheld carts that could replace them. An opened work entrance allowed attackers inside. Aulon returned offshore but declined to land at the hostile yard and withdrew.

Maelin ordered surrendered retainers killed to prevent their assistance to a returning force. Fire also spread into a locked sleeping shed before all its captive labourers could escape. These deaths accompanied the seizure of vessels, tools and stores, leaving bereaved households among those the revolt had released.

The victors needed to maintain their captured ships and provide food and housing for surviving workers. Maelin secured the freedom of the compound's remaining labourers, while some allied households continued holding slaves elsewhere. Some freed people stayed in the yards; others required passage away. The **Tarred Hand** formed among local boatwrights, released workers and foreign residents who joined households there. Later genealogies made the kindred an ancient local bloodline, simplifying origins also preserved in diverse names on family chests and memories of ancestors arriving in captivity.

## The age of useful strangers, c. 1240–350 BR

The Three Beaches could maintain only part of the fleet captured at Rookstrand. Several hulls were dismantled for parts, while local builders learned from the equipment and from continued contact with visiting crews. Atherian fastenings, Ättar experience of exposed crossings and knowledge of local hauling beaches contributed to workshop practice.

By about 620 BR, Rookstrand builders were known along this coast for cargo boats with working parts that could be replaced without stripping the entire hull. The technique developed across several workshops through repeated repairs and voyages. More serviceable vessels extended trade in salt fish and timber and allowed crews to winter away from home with greater confidence in their equipment.

Access to these advantages was unequal. Boat-owning households gained income and influence, while families supplying unpaid sons and daughters could receive a smaller share of the returns. Skilled work nevertheless provided routes into local households for visiting sailors and craftspeople. Marriage and adoption incorporated Atherian and Ättar relatives into communities whose principal ancestry remained that of the Orphaned coastal survivors.

Jirahar crews exchanged goods and route knowledge, becoming more frequent visitors as maritime networks expanded. These contacts connected local skills and markets with a wider seafaring world. The [[The Boat Too Fine for Water|tale of the overproud boatbuilder]], told around the yards, found a familiar subject in the owner who admired a vessel's ornaments while refusing to trust a patched sail. Its workshop humour circulated before favourite versions were written down.

## Imperial neighbours and coastal independence, c. 350 BR–400 AR

Imperial Atheria enlarged overseas markets and offered wages for military service. It could also enforce demands with fleets beyond the resources of an individual inlet. Shore leaders responded differently: some supplied pilots and timber, while others attacked imperial vessels and sold captured cargo through neighbouring landings.

Brackenmouth accepted a purchasing station and seasonal guards. Rookstrand supplied repair crews under local masters, and Keldscar sold pilotage while refusing a permanent garrison. Imperial attention varied, causing these arrangements to expand, lapse or be renewed. Officers sometimes interpreted a Speaker's bargain as the submission of the wider region, although that Speaker depended on the particular households willing to participate.

Redeemer-faith reached Tidewood through sailors, captives, married immigrants and returning workers. Small congregations existed by the late imperial centuries. Their members faced exclusion from offerings believed by neighbours to sustain the catch. Imperial visitors, in turn, sometimes demanded the destruction of harmless family carvings as evidence of conversion. The disputes left relationships within the settlements strained after the visitors departed.

## Orven's profitable voyages, c. 400–540 AR

Imperial weakening and the wider Ättar keel-taking created opportunities for captains able to assemble crews and distribute profitable returns. [[Orven Blackkeel]], born at Rookstrand in 471 AR, began as a repair worker on hired voyages. By his forties he commanded mixed fleets held together by shared booty, information and personal obligation.

Orven paid crews promptly and brought home iron and grain. He also bought the freedom of some Tidewood captives abroad. These activities established a reputation for generosity among households dependent on his voyages. Raids on ships and settlements, including the sale of captives, financed part of that provision.

In 514, Orven rejected a ransom raised for **Hadrun**, an Ättar pilot fostered at Keldscar, and sold him with his adult daughter **Tova** to a higher-paying foreign buyer. Hadrun had previously guided Orven's ships. Tova worked on nets and sails, and her children remained ashore. The sale divided the fleet: some captains left, others accepted shares, and Keldscar stopped guiding Orven through the inner approach.

Estranged captains attacked him while he awaited repairs off the coast in 519. His ship burned, and he drowned after jumping from the stern. The attackers recovered cargo but denied it to his household, leaving families that had relied on his winter grain hungry. Some of the victors had themselves sold captives on earlier voyages.

These relationships shaped the songs that survived him. Rookstrand singers praised the captain who paid his sailors reliably; Keldscar households recalled the people he had sold. Both accounts retained audiences among communities connected by work, marriage and the same coastal gatherings.

## The summer without wrecks, 710–712 AR

During the Long Frontier, Tidewood remained linked to wider affairs through trade and hired service. One of its most consequential local events began at a small inlet beneath Gannet Head, where offerings to the **Guest with Shark Teeth** were followed by extraordinary catches. Fishing households enlarged boats and houses, and wreck cargo contributed to their prosperity.

Improved pilotage reduced ship losses and the supply of offerings. When the catch failed, seer **Iven Teethstring** persuaded prosperous householders to set false lights. Vessels struck the rocks, and shore parties killed survivors able to identify the deception. Participation extended through households whose nets, kitchens and children had become dependent on the inlet's food.

[[Brenna Half-Sail]], whose family had accepted the early catch, recognised rigging from a ship she had repaired at Brackenmouth. Her enquiries and warning helped bring an armed party from other harbours in 712. Boats were prepared to take captives away while fighting took place along the steep approach and beside the feeding stone.

Iven's followers drowned a captive sailor at the stone during the struggle. Brenna's younger brother Teren died helping pull survivors through the surf. The attackers overturned the stone and broke the structures connecting it with the working shore. Iven was killed while resisting; some wreckers escaped inland.

The catch ceased. Ordinary fishing could no longer support every household drawn to the inlet, and winter brought further dispersal. [[The Summer Without Wrecks]] left refugees among neighbouring communities, including children rescued by people who had killed their relatives. Brenna continued fishing and repair voyages and sought food for displaced households. Some survivors renounced shore offerings, while others distinguished the Guest from the Net-Mother at a separate pool and continued those exchanges.

## After the Fall, 905–1052 AR

The Fall disrupted markets and naval protection on which Tidewood's independent settlements had relied. Crews returned unpaid, while refugees arrived with tools, illnesses, news and immediate needs for food. Some families sheltered relatives; others took arriving cargo before permitting its owners ashore. The Three Beaches remained inhabited through the loss of distant buyers and grain supplies.

[[Edris Sailmaker]], born at Brackenmouth in 961, learned her craft under these conditions of uncertain trade. She became skilled in joining unevenly worn cloth into durable sails and taught widowed householders and other owners to undertake their own repairs. Marriage into a Keld family kept her working between the estuary and the exposed shore. Her household also carried fish to winter volunteers travelling inland.

Reports of the Wolfsmere massacre in 993 intensified distrust of Church-backed settlement. Edris's Redeemer-faithful household continued working with old-rite neighbours, some of whom would share her fire but refused to attend her son's baptism. The news brought mourning for relatives and fear of similar violence along the coast.

In the winter of 1002, ice and driven water trapped several households beyond Keldscar's usual landing. Edris obtained passage from the being called the **Drowned Ferryman** by surrendering her stored sailcloth. The passengers emerged alive, while the price left her workshop short of material before the next trading season.

A rival who had previously denied Edris's household shelter was among those rescued. Household accounts describe assistance ashore and gifts of food, followed by continued separation. Neighbours subsequently contributed worn sail panels to replace Edris's stock. Further donations for repairs and rescues developed into the Common Loft, linking the remembered crossing with continuing assistance to working crews.

## Timber wealth and the Beam War, 1052–1291 AR

Naval rebuilding and expanding Sestaran markets increased demand for long, straight ship timbers. A household could earn more from a few suitable trees than from a season's fishing. Hauling tracks reached beyond groves used for local boats, iron tools became easier to obtain, and some forest families bought better livestock and roofs. Others lost access to water and gathering grounds without receiving a share of the purchases.

The [[The Beam War|Beam War of 1287–1291]] began when Reedwake sellers and a Sestaran purchasing house enlarged a hauling cut into a side channel above Brackenmouth. Timber rafts damaged fish traps and diverted water used by drying yards. Sellers continued delivery under armed protection. Fishers cut retaining ropes, releasing logs that killed workers below.

Rookstrand shipmasters initially supported the sellers because the trade supplied tools and orders. Keldscar provided boats to the fishers. Cargo seizures, beatings and reprisals spread the conflict. A guard company's burning of an occupied storehouse then caused Rookstrand crews to refuse transport and repairs for the guards.

The purchasing house could no longer move timber cheaply enough to sustain its armed protection. A settlement in 1291 closed the side channel and divided permitted cutting grounds. Debts, burned homes and deaths remained on both sides. The **Broken Slide**, abandoned above Brackenmouth, preserves the physical route at the centre of the conflict. Children climb its lower timbers while their elders dispute responsibility for the first killings.

## The coast in 1360

The Three Beaches continue to trade and provide winter volunteers. **Mera Brinecoat**, a Reedwake Speaker, seeks orders that will provision the estuary while leaving the old disputed cutting ground closed. **Haldren of the Tarred Hand** requires timber for promised hulls and resents bearing the cost of Brackenmouth's quarrels. Pilot and War-Keeper **Yarra Keld** wants winter shelters repaired before committing further escorts. Each depends on households whose resources and priorities differ.

Chapel-Folk and old-rite families share landings, work and family connections. Others avoid discussion with seers. Restored storehouses stand beside sites abandoned by owners who never returned. Poor crews accept dangerous cargoes for needed wages, while wealthy households preserve accounts of ancestors who prospered by refusing unsuitable voyages.

These circumstances also sustain ordinary exchanges between generations. Repaired boats pass to younger households; children learn to read weather from adults with different loyalties; the first spring fish brings neighbours together. At a launch, the teasing associated with the boat too fine for water accompanies another hull entering the work on which coastal life depends.
