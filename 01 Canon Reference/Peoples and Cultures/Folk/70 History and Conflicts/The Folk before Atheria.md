# The Folk before Atheria — Rivers, Forests and Upland Gords


The ancient Folk were related peoples of the southern forests, coasts, river valleys and mountain approaches. They did not possess one founding ruler, common capital or ancestral kingdom. Their oldest recoverable communities included settled southern populations, human subjects of several Giant states, fugitives, fishing and hunting households, rebels and people displaced from [[Nhaldur and the First Rewriting|Nhaldur]]. The Nhalduran refugees included genuine lesser Giants and giant-blooded human prisoners from southern cold-country courts. Later captives, spouses, fosterlings, traders and proto-Atherian settlers added further ancestry.

Modern scholarship reconstructs a **Fäskogarfolk** language-and-custom continuum from related speech, hearth law, winter obligations and material practice. The term is useful if it remains a reconstruction. No ancient person owed loyalty to a Fäskogarfolk state, and no living clan possesses a pure claim to the whole continuum.

Three constellations organise the evidence. **Belathi** was the broadest western river and coastal name. **Hartheni** described several middle-forest farm and assembly districts. **Odrani** belonged to an eastern river continuum whose Kreseni upland and Vezhari shore neighbours were closely connected but not interchangeable. Each name widened and narrowed over time. A person commonly gave a home river, fort, island, assembly or kindred before any broad people-name.

## Reassembly after the Giants, c. 2300–2050 BR

The Giant collapse reached different southern districts in different forms. Nhalduran refugees arrived with prison languages, metal tools, heat and sound craft. Elsewhere, local Giant estates lost guards and distant markets without suffering a single decisive battle. Human subjects seized stores, killed overseers, negotiated with bodily surviving Giant houses or continued familiar work until another authority appeared.

Recovery depended upon shelter and food. Communities repaired raised granaries, fish weirs, cattle pens, charcoal platforms and timber roads across marsh. A ring wall could protect a settlement while concentrating disease and hunger. Families abandoned low ground after flood, reopened it in drier years and built new halls on the same higher footprints. Later archaeology consequently shows repeated occupations rather than one clean foundation.

Violence accompanied redistribution. Some revolt leaders executed human clients who had served Giant houses. Armed refugees demanded land from older settlements. Giant and giant-blooded prisoners could become warband chiefs because inherited size, strength, endurance or bodily magic made them unusually dangerous after the old guards disappeared. Several took local wives and concubines or founded households through conquest. Communities sold strangers or surrendered rival households to avoid attack. Other villages received displaced children into households, shared seed and opened fisheries. These choices created local reputations which later genealogies often converted into claims of ancient virtue or treachery.

The resulting ancestry did not turn the Folk into lesser Giants. Most descendants were human and possessed complete human souls. Giant traits appeared irregularly among them: one generation might produce an immense fighter, a child able to endure cold beyond ordinary limits or a practitioner with unusual aptitude for a local art, while the next appeared ordinary. A rare true Giant birth retained Giant creaturely continuity and required its own classification. Repeated marriage spread inherited possibilities across the Folk and the related Ättar without creating a pure blood caste. Because the strongest southern contributors came from cold-country populations, the mixture reinforced rather than replaced the pale complexions already common in the region.

The first Grobi bands moved north and west from Kharvos and other ruined corridors during the same period. Human communities learnt that Grobi sought food, defensible warmth and captives rather than wandering without purpose. They marked migration roads, built beacons and placed common refuge beyond ordinary feud. A clan which refused a winter warning could be punished at assembly, although strong houses sometimes escaped the penalties they imposed on poorer neighbours.

## Proto-Atherian war-settlers, c. 2050–1900 BR

Proto-Atherian leagues entered occupied southern country in search of food, timber, ore, routes, harbours and defensible land. Their portable records and standard measures made stores and military work easier to coordinate. They also turned temporary allotment into permanent claim and described local resistance as an attack upon lawful road construction.

At **Brael Ford**, a war-settlement captain called **Calvos Arren** built a timber fort after a Caerethi coalition granted one season of protected storage. His surveyors then marked fields on both sides of the river and his soldiers took hostages after the winter levy failed. **Ivara of Brael**, a cattle holder and ferry organiser, united three ordinarily rival kindreds by promising that recovered fields would return to the households which had worked them. Her coalition starved the fort of fodder, opened a gate with help from enslaved local workers and destroyed it in c. 1972 BR.

The victory did not expel every settler. Ivara protected surrendered craftspeople and children who accepted local households, while her war leader **Derron** sold several captured soldiers through the coast. Families of the sold accused Ivara of permitting slavery for alliance. Two generations later, Brael contained Atherian-derived names, measures and marriages inside a Caerethi political community. The episode became the **Gate Measured Twice**, remembered by Folk singers as the defeat of foreign title and by Atherian descendants as the moment their families ceased to belong to a fort.

Other encounters ended differently. Dovren pilots entered mixed harbour agreements. Rauven leaders used settler weapons against Hartheni rivals and then faced garrisons on the captured crossings. Odrani villages hired smiths and scribes while refusing settlement beyond a marked market landing. No single response created the Folk.

## The western and Atlantic worlds

### Belathi and Caerethi river country

**Belathi** first appears as a family of western speech in later trade lists. It became politically useful when river and hill communities dealt with merchants who wanted one name for several markets. The **Caerethi** were among its strongest inland peoples. They occupied cultivated river corridors, pasture hills and defended heights linked by cattle roads.

**Brael Tor** grew above the ford reclaimed by Ivara's coalition. An inner earthwork held assembly space, principal storehouses and the compounds of learned families. Workshops and ordinary houses spread along the slopes. Surrounding farms, not the enclosed settlement alone, supplied its population. By c. 1450 BR, Brael's market accepted cattle tallies, weighed metal and several foreign coins without making coin the only measure of wealth.

Caerethi politics joined kindred councils, client households, learned judges, war leaders and seasonal assemblies. A successful ruler could raise tribute, host a great feast and command a coalition, but land claims remained entangled with burial, labour and household use. A ruler who attempted to sell a common wood without its users could find the boundary paths blocked and the sale ignored. Once each year, chiefs and free men from the wider river country met at an open-air All-Thing to settle disputes which crossed the boundaries of local assemblies.

### Dovren estuaries and Morlach islands

The **Dovren** lived around western estuaries and lower rivers. Their pilots, salt workers, boat-builders and fish merchants connected inland cattle and metal to the outer coast. Harbour leadership was often shared between a landing speaker, a war keeper and households owning boats. Wealthy pilot families could control access without ruling the farms which fed them.

The **Morlach** name belonged to island and exposed-coast kindreds linked by fosterage and seasonal sailing. Some islands supported cattle, barley and permanent halls. Others were summer fisheries or ritual places. Morlach rulers used foster children to bind sea roads, teach navigation and guarantee peace. A fosterling could be loved as kin and still serve as a hostage whose mistreatment threatened war.

Belathi learned houses preserved genealogy, law, praise, satire, healing and negotiation with local powers. Their authority followed teaching and recognition rather than one universal priesthood. Some maintained groves, head deposits or rites in which a ruler's fitness was tested through hospitality and land. Others condemned those practices. Later Enaidfae identities drew selectively from these houses without becoming a direct continuation of one ancient order.

### The War of the Three Fosters, c. 1342–1328 BR

The western heroic age included a cattle and succession war known as the **War of the Three Fosters**. **Maren Sorn**, ruler of a lower Dovren landing, had fostered one daughter among the Caerethi, one son in Morlach and a sister's child with a rival estuary house. When she died, each foster network claimed different boats, cattle and toll obligations.

The Caerethi claimant sought cattle sufficient to maintain Brael's drought stores. The Morlach claimant required harbour access after storms destroyed two island landings. The estuary claimant controlled Maren's household and refused to divide the fleet. Raids escalated after satirists mocked each side's inability to protect a dead woman's guests.

The war destroyed fields and carried people into slavery during the wider failure of Bronze Roads. It ended when boat crews refused a fourth season without repair and divided the remaining hulls according to the work each harbour could perform. The settlement gave foster kin standing in inheritance hearings while confirming that fosterage did not erase the claims of spouses, household workers or enslaved dependants. Songs celebrate single combats; repair lists show that shipwrights and food lenders forced the peace.

## The middle forests

### Hartheni districts

The **Hartheni** inhabited woodland farms, marsh edges and river crossings between western hill country and the eastern routes. Their settlements ranged from isolated longhouses to clustered villages and defended assembly places. A longhouse sheltered people, animals, tools and winter stores. Its repeated rebuilding could establish a stronger territorial claim than a ruler's distant grant.

Assemblies heard feud cases, set compensation, witnessed marriages and raised watch levies. Compensation was unequal. The death of a free householder with many kin drew a higher enforceable price than the death of a slave or unattached stranger. Some reforming assemblies allowed a household to claim for accepted guests and freed dependants. Others treated the same people as property. Hartheni districts also sent chiefs and free men to an annual All-Thing, where several assemblies could recognise the same compensation or outlawry and decide whether a common winter watch would be raised.

Personal retinues cut across kinship. A war leader fed and equipped young fighters in return for service. Success attracted followers and captives; failure scattered them. Retinues guarded roads and Grobi watches, enforced a chief's private debts and sometimes overthrew the assemblies which had first hired them.

### Ossen marshes and Rauven approaches

**Ossen** communities occupied reed country, wet woodland and islands of firm ground. They built raised paths, eel traps, bog-iron works and hidden winter stores. Outsiders found the landscape difficult because a route safe in autumn could disappear during thaw. Ossen guides consequently shaped every army entering their country.

The **Rauven** name spread among coalitions nearer the proto-Atherian and later republican approaches. Rauven farms and treaty strongholds dealt with foreign garrisons earlier and more continuously than most Hartheni districts. Some supplied auxiliaries or accepted military land grants. Others formed anti-Atherian coalitions. Rauven identity therefore never meant collaboration or resistance by itself.

### The War of the Three Crossings, c. 1096–1089 BR

The Hartheni war leader **Havel Ossen** built a following by defending three crossings used by ore and salt traffic. When the eastern crossing assembly tried to replace his collectors, Havel seized its hostages and claimed emergency command until the Grobi season ended. Neighbouring districts accused him of manufacturing a winter threat to preserve tolls.

Fighting moved through wet ground which neither side could feed for long. Havel's opponents cut raised paths and lured his retinue towards a false firm bank. His company was surrounded after three days without dry fuel. Many surrendered. The victors killed part of the captured force, broke their weapons and placed arms and bodies in **Ossen Fen** as a public offering.

The deposit became a regional victory place. It also preserved evidence of organised post-battle killing. Later assemblies cited the war when limiting the duration of emergency toll commands. Atherian authors used it as proof that all forest peoples sacrificed prisoners, ignoring both the local political cause and Hartheni criticism of the act.

## The eastern rivers and uplands

### Odrani river settlements

The **Odrani** lived along cold rivers joining interior forests, upland passes and eastern shores. Their early villages combined timber houses, ovens, mixed fields, animals, fishing and woodland work. Beekeeping supplied honey, wax and trade goods. Iron production expanded where ore, charcoal and food could be brought together.

Gords developed at portages, river meetings and defensible market landings. Their timber-and-earth walls enclosed wells, halls, workshops and refuges while fields remained outside. A gord prince depended on surrounding villages for grain and labour. Assemblies could recognise the same leader in war and refuse his judgement over household land. The eastern All-Things met beyond gord walls so that a prince could attend with his following but could not claim the gathering as a court held inside his own fortification.

**Varena** became an influential Odrani market gord after c. 1420 BR. Its river landing received furs, wax, honey, iron and enslaved captives. Foreign silver passed through without replacing weighed metal and obligation. The city's oven quarter was governed by contributing households because bread supply had survived an earlier siege when the prince's granary failed.

### Kreseni passes and Vezhari shores

The **Kreseni** occupied eastern uplands and the river sources below the Gale-Crown approaches. Their communities held mines, pasture valleys, carved passes and resonant chambers inherited from Nhalduran workers. Upland princes sought tribute from convoys, while village assemblies defended pasture and avalanche knowledge as local property.

Kreseni and Vardeni communities shared older southern ancestry, related work vocabulary and recurring marriage across the north-eastern approaches. Their political histories had separated before the strongest Atherian states formed. Vardeni households entered the Aurin and Atherian worlds through their own upland towns; Kreseni remained part of the eastern Folk continuum. Neither people should be described as a lost branch of the other.

The **Vezhari** occupied cold shores reached by eastern rivers. They fished, built shallow river craft and sea-going boats, and hosted seasonal traders from Odrani, Ättar and foreign ports. One Vezhari centre at **Suroth** gave unusual public authority to a local cult and its keepers. That institution never defined all shore religion, and its apparent signs do not yet identify whether the answering power was Menhir, Mask or another local continuity.

### The Deep Voice

Eastern workers developed [[The Deep Voice|the Deep Voice]] from the command galleries of Veyrun. Odrani and Kreseni schools aligned breath, meaningful language, stance and the resonance of air, earth and built chambers. Masters served villages, gords and princes as warning callers, engineers, messengers and weapons.

The art reached its military height during the War of Seven Gords in c. 1184–1161 BR. After the later disaster at Dramovar, Sarvek the Listener withdrew to the high Gale-Crown slopes and founded the Quiet Measure, a small order of mountain druids devoted to Zephyraxis. The order joined restraint and close terrain knowledge to prayer, but it remained a fringe tradition. Martial houses continued. Descent alone granted no ability, and people from other communities could learn if a school received them.

## Exchange and the southern material world, c. 1400–800 BR

The Breaking of the Bronze Roads weakened distant exchange without ending regional civilisation. Western cattle and salt, middle-forest iron and timber, eastern wax and furs, Ättar fish and hides and Atherian grain moved through shorter networks. Bronze became expensive where tin or copper routes failed. Iron tools spread unevenly because local ore still required charcoal, food and skilled work.

Markets gathered translators, judges, cooks, drovers, guards, healers, entertainers and ransom brokers. Slaves worked farms, households, mines, boats and workshops in every constellation, although law and routes to freedom varied. Captive labour connected wealth to war. A splendid torc, ship or gord wall could embody the work of people unable to leave.

Political ambition grew around control of those systems. Brael rulers wanted cattle roads; Dovren leaders wanted harbour dues; Hartheni chiefs wanted crossings and retinues; Odrani princes wanted tribute at river landings; Kreseni houses wanted mines and passes. No ambition joined the Folk under one crown.

## Atherian contact, c. 800–520 BR

Atherian city-states brought larger military markets, coins, wine, glass, worked metal and written agreements. Folk supplied timber, cattle, iron, furs, wax, soldiers and route knowledge. Permanent merchant wards formed at some northern towns. Elsewhere, local assemblies allowed seasonal exchange while forbidding survey, fortified warehouses or foreign courts.

Service changed households. An auxiliary might return with wages and an Atherian spouse, a captive and a claim to land. A person sold abroad could later be manumitted into an Atherian city and maintain kin ties southward. Chiefs used foreign support against rivals, while foreign commanders described those rivals as enemies of public order.

Belathi sea roads and Vezhari river routes also connected to Package E's island and harbour worlds. This Package D account establishes southern participants, cargo and political interests without naming every ancient Sestaran precursor or fixing the full maritime map before Package E.

## Casvilar and the northern approaches, c. 390–379 BR

The existing history of [[The Southern Campaigns of Teren Casvilar]] governs the late republican sequence. Casvilar fought in a belt of northern forest settlements, hillforts and allied districts adjoining later Green March and provincial corridors. He did not conquer Brael, Varena, Tidewood, the Gale-Crown ranges or all Folk land.

Morveth used Atherian support to protect contested crossings. Drenoc assembled dispossessed households and hillfort leaders, while Velcara turned from negotiated supply to resistance when repeated requisitions exhausted winter stores. Kellovar fell in 383 BR after river supply, local rivalry and hunger divided its relief. At Darneth in 380, Casvilar used engineering against protected water and ordered surrendered fighters mutilated.

These campaigns altered the Rauven-facing approaches most directly. Captives went north, garrisons received local food and military creditors acquired claims. Forest mobility and the low revenue of dispersed farms limited occupation. The cold-coast voyage of 385 won gifts and hostages but no durable province.

## The Folk world in 350 BR

At the beginning of Thalion's supremacy, Folk societies occupied several political positions:

| Region | Principal powers | Atherian relationship in 350 BR |
|---|---|---|
| Belathi west | Brael and other hill towns, Dovren landings, Morlach island kindreds | trade, mercenary and harbour agreements; no common submission |
| Hartheni middle country | farm assemblies, retinue chiefs, Ossen route communities | independent interior beside allied, conquered and resistant northern approaches |
| Rauven-facing north | Morveth's clients, damaged Kellovar and Darneth districts, surviving strongholds | garrisons, hostages and unequal treaties after Casvilar |
| Odrani rivers | Varena and rival gords, village assemblies and river princes | selective trade, engineers and service; no Atherian provincial government |
| Kreseni uplands and Vezhari shore | pass holders, Deep-Voiced schools, Suroth and other local centres | contact through eastern and maritime routes; politically independent |

Folk unity existed as related language, recognisable institutions and recurring claims of kinship. It did not supply a common army or sovereign. An empire entering the region had to deal with each crossing, food system, assembly, fort and winter obligation on its own terms.

## Households, law and ordinary achievement

The household included close kin, foster children, apprentices, clients, freed people, accepted guests and slaves in changing combinations. Women and men could control property and political relationships according to local law, but neither sex and no rank possessed equal freedom in every community. A chief's marriage might join markets while a captive concubinage recorded no genuine choice.

All three broad constellations shared the **All-Thing**, an annual open-air gathering of chiefs and free men. It did not govern the entire Folk world. Each river country, forest district or connected group of gords held its own gathering, normally after harvest and before the Grobi war season. The assembly settled disputes which local kindreds could not contain, witnessed marriages and inheritance, heard compensation claims, declared outlawry and decided whether several districts would raise a common levy. A decision bound absent communities only when their recognised men had entrusted a speaker to attend.

Formal voice belonged to free men. Women, slaves, dependants and strangers usually had to place a claim through a recognised male householder, chief or learned judge. A wealthy widow, female landholder or renowned learned woman could sometimes compel a hearing, but this depended on property, kin and reputation rather than an equal right. The exclusion mattered because the assembly decided marriages, captive status and compensation affecting people who could not address it directly.

Hearth-law required a truthful name, food and shelter under dangerous conditions. It did not oblige a household to accept every armed stranger or protect a guest after proven violence. Winter refuge carried stronger protection than ordinary summer hospitality. Burning a refuge, poisoning common water or attacking during an agreed Grobi truce could place a household beyond compensation.

Southern craft produced durable landscapes even when timber vanished. Coppiced woods, charcoal grounds, drained fields, diverted streams, mine scars, rebuilt hall platforms, bridge piles and deep middens record generations of work. Songs and surviving metal objects are only part of that history.

People also enjoyed their skill. Belathi fairs prized riddles which embarrassed powerful hosts without ending hospitality. Hartheni spoon contests rewarded a ladle able to survive a winter cauldron. Odrani strongman tales regularly ended with the hero unable to repair the door he had lifted from its hinges. Vezhari boat races allowed the losing crew to demand a meal from the winners on the ground that defeat had left them too tired to cook.

## Faith and historical memory

Folk religion remained local. Learned houses, household rites, oath places, groves, river offerings and public cult centres coexisted. A power which protected one ford might demand an unjust price. A ruler who received a real response did not thereby acquire moral title to a people or land.

Belathi songs remembered sovereignty, hospitality and difficult foster ties. Hartheni traditions remembered assemblies, compensation and dangerous victory water. Odrani and Kreseni epics celebrated princes, merchants and Deep-Voiced champions while disagreeing about the same wars. Later Enaidfae networks inherited some offices and stories. They were not a timeless priesthood unchanged from the Great Unbinding.
