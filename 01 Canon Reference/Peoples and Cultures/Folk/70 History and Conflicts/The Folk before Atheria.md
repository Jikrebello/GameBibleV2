# The Folk before Atheria — Rivers, Forests and Upland Gords


The ancient Folk were related peoples of the southern forests, coasts, river valleys and mountain approaches. They did not possess one founding ruler, common capital or ancestral kingdom. Their oldest recoverable communities included settled southern populations, human subjects of several Giant states, fugitives, fishing and hunting households, rebels and people displaced from [[Historical Atlas of the Southern Cold World|Nhaldur]]. The Nhalduran refugees included genuine lesser Giants and giant-blooded human prisoners from southern cold-country courts. Later captives, spouses, fosterlings, traders and proto-Atherian settlers added further ancestry.

Modern scholarship reconstructs a **Fäskogarfolk** language-and-custom continuum from related speech, hearth law, winter obligations and material practice.

Three constellations organise the evidence. **Belathi** was the broadest western river and coastal name. **Hartheni** described several middle-forest farm and assembly districts. **Odrani** belonged to an eastern river continuum whose Kreseni upland and Vezhari shore neighbours were closely connected. Each name widened and narrowed over time. A person commonly gave a home river, fort, island, assembly or kindred before any broad people-name.

## Reassembly after the Giants, c. 2300–2050 BR

The Giant collapse reached different southern districts in different forms. Nhalduran refugees arrived with prison languages, metal tools, heat and sound craft. Elsewhere, local Giant estates lost guards and distant markets without suffering a single decisive battle. Human subjects seized stores, killed overseers, negotiated with bodily surviving Giant houses or continued familiar work until another authority appeared.

Recovery depended upon shelter and food. Communities repaired raised granaries, fish weirs, cattle pens, charcoal platforms and timber roads across marsh. A ring wall could protect a settlement while concentrating disease and hunger. Families abandoned low ground after flood, reopened it in drier years and built new halls on the same higher footprints. Later archaeology consequently shows repeated occupations.

Violence accompanied redistribution. Some revolt leaders executed human clients who had served Giant houses. Armed refugees demanded land from older settlements. Giant and giant-blooded prisoners could become warband chiefs because inherited size, strength, endurance or bodily magic made them unusually dangerous after the old guards disappeared. Several took local wives and concubines or founded households through conquest. Communities sold strangers or surrendered rival households to avoid attack. Other villages received displaced children into households, shared seed and opened fisheries. These choices created local reputations which later genealogies often converted into claims of ancient virtue or treachery.

Giant traits appeared irregularly among the descendants: one generation might produce an immense fighter, a child able to endure cold beyond ordinary limits or a practitioner with unusual aptitude for a local art, while the next appeared ordinary. Repeated marriage spread inherited possibilities across the Folk and the related Ättar. Because the strongest southern contributors came from cold-country populations, the mixture reinforced the pale complexions already common in the region.

The first Grobi bands moved north and west from Kharvos and other ruined corridors during the same period. Human communities learnt that Grobi sought food, defensible warmth and captives. They marked migration roads, built beacons and placed common refuge beyond ordinary feud. A clan which refused a winter warning could be punished at assembly, although strong houses sometimes escaped the penalties they imposed on poorer neighbours.

## Proto-Atherian war-settlers, c. 2050–1900 BR

Proto-Atherian leagues entered occupied southern country hungry. Their ancestors had been fed in the Glass for a century, and Folk singers say the settlers still farmed like strangers to the soil. They sought food, timber, ore, routes, harbours and defensible land, and their forts took fields together with the people who knew how to work them: ploughmen, seed-keepers, herders and their oxen. Their portable records and standard measures made stores and military work easier to coordinate. They also turned temporary allotment into permanent claim and described local resistance as an attack upon lawful road construction.

At **Brael Ford**, a war-settlement captain called **Calvos Arren** built a timber fort after a Caerethi coalition granted one season of protected storage. His surveyors then marked fields on both sides of the river, and when the winter levy failed his soldiers took hostages and set their families to plough the fort's new ground. **Ivara of Brael**, a cattle holder and ferry organiser, united three ordinarily rival kindreds by promising that recovered fields would return to the households which had worked them. Her coalition starved the fort of fodder, opened a gate with help from enslaved local workers and destroyed it around 1970 BR.

The victory did not expel every settler. Ivara protected surrendered craftspeople and children who accepted local households, while her war leader **Derron** sold several captured soldiers through the coast. Families of the sold accused Ivara of permitting slavery for alliance. Two generations later, Brael contained Atherian-derived names, measures and marriages inside a Caerethi political community. The episode became the **Gate Measured Twice**, remembered by Folk singers as the defeat of foreign title and by Atherian descendants as the moment their families ceased to belong to a fort.

Other encounters ended differently. Dovren pilots entered mixed harbour agreements. Rauven leaders used settler weapons against Hartheni rivals and then faced garrisons on the captured crossings. Odrani villages hired smiths and scribes while refusing settlement beyond a marked market landing.

Folk memory gave these generations one name: the time **the fed came for the farmers**. Hartheni and Belathi singers turned the settlers' own story against them: *"Heaven fed them a hundred years and sent them to eat our harvest."* Settler descendants answered that the Folk *"were not fed because they were not called"*, and the two sayings have met in land quarrels ever since. Folk and Aurin satirists kept a sharper joke about the settlers' Prophet, **the Guest Who Died at the Door**. In one version his own captains killed him for forbidding them fields that others had sown. In the comic version he starved *"the first winter the bread stopped, like any of us"*, and the punchline runs: *"Heaven's guest ate for a hundred years and starved in a week."* In 1360 Folk claimants in the land quarrels around Grenzburg still date their oldest grievances to the time the fed came for the farmers, and satirists still bring the Guest to a boundary hearing.

## The Withheld Season and the divided halls

Two hard lessons of the early Hearths were learnt in the cold watershed between the Tidewood coast and the Gale-Crown ranges, among halls whose descendants became Folk and Ättar alike. Tellers place both some generations after the first settler forts.

After three bad winters, the rulers of several watershed halls sealed their seed grain, penned their breeding animals and kept their dead in frozen houses until the ground could take them. The keeping saved lives. When the thaw came, the rulers refused to open it, saying that one more month would make the next winter safe, and some opened their neighbours' stores instead of their own. The meltwater rose, but the bodies on the roofs did not rot and the seed in the jars would not sprout. Then, the tellers say, the **High Hunt** came down the old seasonal roads: court riders, winter beasts and moving weather that took whatever had been withheld. It tore corpses from mourners, pregnant ewes and mares from their stalls and sacks from locked lofts, and it took living people who stood in its way. Where a ruler had pointed the riders at a poorer neighbour's barn, the Hunt often took the easier prey. Watershed songs keep one rider who would not. He rode past the poor barn to the ruler's own loft, and a weathered rider's mask in a watershed hall is said to show his face.

Hunger followed, because stores held too long were lost all at once. At one fortified barn a woman cut the first **release mark** into the harness of her only mare and led every animal out into the thaw. The Hunt passed her house but took her eldest son from a neighbour's granary, where he had gone to free prisoners. Folk songs make her a widow; Ättar songs make her the hoarding ruler's sister. Menhir-facing tellers say the Hunt enforced a broken season, later Church polemic called it a demon raid, and farmers insist that the marks work whether or not anyone believes the old account. The custom outlived the argument. Release cuts appear on harness, burial cloth and barn lintels before midwinter stores are opened or spring bodies buried, and watershed assemblies came to expect a chief to break the seal on his store at the thaw before the households that had filled it. [[G-67 - The Marks of Necessary Release|The Marks of Necessary Release]] preserves the telling.

The second lesson divided kin. Two related halls stood a week's march apart. One, caught in a feud, sent the old split antler that summoned every cousin to come armed. The other made ready to answer until its youngest herder found Grobi signs under the snow: split hoofprints around the lambing caves, stolen iron and a child's mitten pinned to a thorn. Its captain kept his spears at home. The summoning hall burned before help came, and the Grobi struck the hall that had stayed home anyway, coming through the lambing caves to kill children in the smoke. Around the ashes a widowed aunt struck the captain with half the antler. He did not defend himself, and he hung the other half above a new hall facing the caves. The cousins' answer became a Folk proverb: *"A people who chooses walls over cousins will one day have walls and no cousins."* Ättar songs say the Grobi planted the summons to empty the watch; Folk songs call that a later excuse. The two branches went on sharing marriage and song more easily than command. [[G-61 - The Hall That Faced the Grobi|The Hall That Faced the Grobi]] preserves the story.

## The western and Atlantic worlds

### Belathi and Caerethi river country

**Belathi** first appears as a family of western speech in later trade lists. It became politically useful when river and hill communities dealt with merchants who wanted one name for several markets. The **Caerethi** were among its strongest inland peoples. They occupied cultivated river corridors, pasture hills and defended heights linked by cattle roads.

**Brael Tor** grew above the ford reclaimed by Ivara's coalition. An inner earthwork held assembly space, principal storehouses and the compounds of learned families. Workshops and ordinary houses spread along the slopes. Surrounding farms, not the enclosed settlement alone, supplied its population. By c. 1450 BR, Brael's market accepted cattle tallies, weighed metal, ring-silver and stamped ingots from foreign smelters without making any one of them the only measure of wealth.

Caerethi politics joined kindred councils, client households, learned judges, war leaders and seasonal assemblies. A successful ruler could raise tribute, host a great feast and command a coalition, but land claims remained entangled with burial, labour and household use. A ruler who attempted to sell a common wood without its users could find the boundary paths blocked and the sale ignored. Once each year, chiefs and free men from the wider river country met at an open-air All-Thing to settle disputes which crossed the boundaries of local assemblies.

### Dovren estuaries and Morlach islands

The **Dovren** lived around western estuaries and lower rivers. Their pilots, salt workers, boat-builders and fish merchants connected inland cattle and metal to the outer coast. Harbour leadership was often shared between a landing speaker, a war keeper and households owning boats. Wealthy pilot families could control access without ruling the farms which fed them.

The **Morlach** name belonged to island and exposed-coast kindreds linked by fosterage and seasonal sailing. Some islands supported cattle, barley and permanent halls. Others were summer fisheries or ritual places. Morlach rulers used foster children to bind sea roads, teach navigation and guarantee peace. A fosterling could be loved as kin and still serve as a hostage whose mistreatment threatened war.

Belathi learned houses preserved genealogy, law, praise, satire, healing and negotiation with local powers, all of it in memory, knotted cords and notched tally-staves. Writing had reached the south chiefly as the captive rolls of Nhaldur's gaolers and the portable records of the settler forts, and assemblies trusted a law that many people had heard recited over one that could be carried off in a satchel. Their authority followed teaching and recognition. Some maintained groves, head deposits or rites in which a ruler's fitness was tested through hospitality and land. Others condemned those practices. Later Enaidfae identities drew selectively from these houses.

### The War of the Three Fosters

The western heroic age included a cattle and succession war known as the **War of the Three Fosters**, which Dovren singers place around 1340 BR. **Maren Sorn**, ruler of a lower Dovren landing, had fostered one daughter among the Caerethi, one son in Morlach and a sister's child with a rival estuary house. When she died, each foster network claimed different boats, cattle and toll obligations.

The Caerethi claimant sought cattle sufficient to maintain Brael's drought stores. The Morlach claimant required harbour access after storms destroyed two island landings. The estuary claimant controlled Maren's household and refused to divide the fleet. Raids escalated after satirists mocked each side's inability to protect a dead woman's guests.

The war destroyed fields and carried people into slavery during the wider failure of Bronze Roads. It ended when boat crews refused a fourth season without repair and divided the remaining hulls according to the work each harbour could perform. The settlement gave foster kin standing in inheritance hearings while confirming that fosterage did not erase the claims of spouses, household workers or enslaved dependants. Songs celebrate single combats; the shipwrights' repair tallies show that shipwrights and food lenders forced the peace.

## The middle forests

### Hartheni districts

The **Hartheni** inhabited woodland farms, marsh edges and river crossings between western hill country and the eastern routes. Their settlements ranged from isolated longhouses to clustered villages and defended assembly places. A longhouse sheltered people, animals, tools and winter stores. Its repeated rebuilding could establish a stronger territorial claim than a ruler's distant grant.

Assemblies heard feud cases, set compensation, witnessed marriages and raised watch levies. Compensation was unequal. The death of a free householder with many kin drew a higher enforceable price than the death of a slave or unattached stranger. Some reforming assemblies allowed a household to claim for accepted guests and freed dependants. Others treated the same people as property. Hartheni districts also sent chiefs and free men to an annual All-Thing, where several assemblies could recognise the same compensation or outlawry and decide whether a common winter watch would be raised.

Personal retinues cut across kinship. A war leader fed and equipped young fighters in return for service. Success attracted followers and captives; failure scattered them. Retinues guarded roads and Grobi watches, enforced a chief's private debts and sometimes overthrew the assemblies which had first hired them.

### Ossen marshes and Rauven approaches

**Ossen** communities occupied reed country, wet woodland and islands of firm ground. They built raised paths, eel traps, bog-iron works and hidden winter stores. Outsiders found the landscape difficult because a route safe in autumn could disappear during thaw. Ossen guides consequently shaped every army entering their country.

The **Rauven** name spread among coalitions nearer the proto-Atherian and later republican approaches. Rauven farms and treaty strongholds dealt with foreign garrisons earlier and more continuously than most Hartheni districts. Some supplied auxiliaries or accepted military land grants. Others formed anti-Atherian coalitions.

### The War of the Three Crossings

Around 1090 BR, the Hartheni war leader **Havel Ossen** built a following by defending three crossings used by ore and salt traffic. When the eastern crossing assembly tried to replace his collectors, Havel seized its hostages and claimed emergency command until the Grobi season ended. Neighbouring districts accused him of manufacturing a winter threat to preserve tolls.

Fighting moved through wet ground which neither side could feed for long. Havel's opponents cut raised paths and lured his retinue towards a false firm bank. His company was surrounded after three days without dry fuel. Many surrendered. The victors killed part of the captured force, broke their weapons and placed arms and bodies in **Ossen Fen** as a public offering.

The deposit became a regional victory place. It also preserved evidence of organised post-battle killing. Later assemblies cited the war when limiting the duration of emergency toll commands. Atherian authors used it as proof that all forest peoples sacrificed prisoners, ignoring both the local political cause and Hartheni criticism of the act.

## The eastern rivers and uplands

### Odrani river settlements

The **Odrani** lived along cold rivers joining interior forests, upland passes and eastern shores. Their early villages combined timber houses, ovens, mixed fields, animals, fishing and woodland work. Beekeeping supplied honey, wax and trade goods. Iron production expanded where ore, charcoal and food could be brought together.

Gords developed at portages, river meetings and defensible market landings. Their timber-and-earth walls enclosed wells, halls, workshops and refuges while fields remained outside. A gord prince depended on surrounding villages for grain and labour. Assemblies could recognise the same leader in war and refuse his judgement over household land. The eastern All-Things met beyond gord walls so that a prince could attend with his following but could not claim the gathering as a court held inside his own fortification.

**Varena** became an influential Odrani market gord after c. 1420 BR. Its river landing received furs, wax, honey, iron and enslaved captives. Foreign silver passed through without replacing weighed metal and obligation. The city's oven quarter was governed by contributing households because bread supply had survived an earlier siege when the prince's granary failed.

### Kreseni passes and Vezhari shores

The **Kreseni** occupied eastern uplands and the river sources below the Gale-Crown approaches. Their communities held mines, pasture valleys, carved passes and resonant chambers inherited from Nhalduran workers. Upland princes sought tribute from convoys, while village assemblies defended pasture and avalanche knowledge as local property.

Kreseni and Vardeni communities shared older southern ancestry, related work vocabulary and recurring marriage across the north-eastern approaches. Their political histories had separated before the strongest Atherian states formed. Vardeni households entered the Aurin and Atherian worlds through their own upland towns; Kreseni remained part of the eastern Folk continuum.

The **Vezhari** occupied cold shores reached by eastern rivers. They fished, built shallow river craft and sea-going boats, and hosted seasonal traders from Odrani, Ättar and foreign ports. One Vezhari centre at **Suroth** gave unusual public authority to a local cult and its keepers.

### The Deep Voice

Eastern workers developed the Deep Voice from the command galleries of Veyrun. Odrani and Kreseni schools aligned breath, meaningful language, stance and the resonance of air, earth and built chambers. Masters served villages, gords and princes as warning callers, engineers, messengers and weapons.

The art reached its military height during the War of Seven Gords, c. 1180–1160 BR. After the later disaster at Dramovar, Sarvek the Listener withdrew to the high Gale-Crown slopes and founded the Quiet Measure, a small order of mountain druids devoted to Zephyraxis. The order joined restraint and close terrain knowledge to prayer, but it remained a fringe tradition. Martial houses continued. Ability came through training, and people from other communities could learn if a school received them.

## Exchange and the southern material world, c. 1400–800 BR

The Breaking of the Bronze Roads weakened distant exchange. Western cattle and salt, middle-forest iron and timber, eastern wax and furs, Ättar fish and hides and Atherian grain moved along shorter roads. Bronze became expensive where tin or copper routes failed. Southern smiths had smelted iron since the reassembly, from Ossen bog ore and Odrani ridge ore, because little salvaged Giant metal ever reached the cold country. Traders now brought back tales from the north of caravans whose bronze had been taken back on the old roads by its dead owners, and Odrani smiths said that iron was the one metal no dead king would come back for. Northern buyers began to ask for southern blooms and bars. Iron tools still spread unevenly, because every bloom needed charcoal, food and skilled work.

Markets gathered translators, judges, cooks, drovers, guards, healers, entertainers and ransom brokers. Slaves worked farms, households, mines, boats and workshops in every constellation, although law and routes to freedom varied. Captive labour connected wealth to war. A splendid torc, ship or gord wall could embody the work of people unable to leave.

Political ambition grew around control of those systems. Brael rulers wanted cattle roads; Dovren leaders wanted harbour dues; Hartheni chiefs wanted crossings and retinues; Odrani princes wanted tribute at river landings; Kreseni houses wanted mines and passes.

## Atherian contact, c. 800–520 BR

Atherian city-states brought larger military markets, coins, wine, glass, worked metal and written agreements. Folk supplied timber, cattle, iron, furs, wax, soldiers and route knowledge. Learned houses that kept law in memory and on tally-staves now met agreements written on boards and skins. Some learnt the letters, but most assemblies still required the spoken law to be recited before they would accept a written copy. Permanent merchant wards formed at some northern towns. Elsewhere, local assemblies allowed seasonal exchange while forbidding survey, fortified warehouses or foreign courts.

Service changed households. An auxiliary might return with wages and an Atherian spouse, a captive and a claim to land. A person sold abroad could later be manumitted into an Atherian city and maintain kin ties southward. Chiefs used foreign support against rivals, while foreign commanders described those rivals as enemies of public order.

Belathi sea roads and Vezhari river routes also connected to the wider island and harbour worlds.

## Casvilar and the northern approaches, c. 390–379 BR

[[The Southern Campaigns of Teren Casvilar]] gives the full late republican sequence. Casvilar fought in a belt of northern forest settlements, hillforts and allied districts adjoining later Green March and provincial corridors. He did not conquer Brael, Varena, Tidewood, the Gale-Crown ranges or all Folk land.

Morveth used Atherian support to protect contested crossings. Drenoc assembled dispossessed households and hillfort leaders, while Velcara turned from negotiated supply to resistance when repeated requisitions exhausted winter stores. Kellovar fell in 383 BR after river supply, local rivalry and hunger divided its relief, but most of its people were gone: they had crossed the ford below the hill on their household tokens, and Drenoc had walked into the water after them to bargain for those who held none. He was not seen again. Morveth's halls paid at that ford afterwards with Atherian silver. At Darneth in 380, Casvilar's engineers brought down the rampart, and he ordered the hands cut from the ford guides who surrendered.

These campaigns altered the Rauven-facing approaches most directly. Captives went north, garrisons received local food and military creditors acquired claims. Forest mobility and the low revenue of dispersed farms limited occupation. The cold-coast voyage of 385 won gifts and hostages but no durable province.

## The Folk world in 350 BR

At the beginning of Thalion's supremacy, Folk societies occupied several political positions:

| Region | Principal powers | Atherian relationship in 350 BR |
|---|---|---|
| Belathi west | Brael and other hill towns, Dovren landings, Morlach island kindreds | trade, mercenary and harbour agreements; no common submission |
| Hartheni middle country | farm assemblies, retinue chiefs, Ossen route communities | independent interior beside allied, conquered and resistant northern approaches |
| Rauven-facing north | Morveth's clients, damaged Kellovar and Darneth districts, surviving strongholds | garrisons, hostages and unequal treaties after Casvilar |
| Odrani rivers | Varena and rival gords, village assemblies and river princes | selective trade, engineers and service; no Atherian provincial government |
| Kreseni uplands and Vezhari shore | pass holders, Deep-Voiced schools, Suroth and other local centres | contact through eastern and maritime routes; politically independent |

Folk unity existed as related language, recognisable institutions and recurring claims of kinship. It did not supply a common army or sovereign. An empire entering the region had to deal with each crossing, food system, assembly, fort and winter obligation on its own terms.

## Households, law and ordinary achievement

The household included close kin, foster children, apprentices, clients, freed people, accepted guests and slaves in changing combinations. Women and men could control property and political relationships according to local law, but neither sex and no rank possessed equal freedom in every community. A chief's marriage might join markets while a captive concubinage recorded no genuine choice.

All three broad constellations shared the **All-Thing**, an annual open-air gathering of chiefs and free men. Each river country, forest district or connected group of gords held its own gathering, normally after harvest and before the Grobi war season. The assembly settled disputes which local kindreds could not contain, witnessed marriages and inheritance, heard compensation claims, declared outlawry and decided whether several districts would raise a common levy. A decision bound absent communities only when their recognised men had entrusted a speaker to attend.

Formal voice belonged to free men. Women, slaves, dependants and strangers usually had to place a claim through a recognised male householder, chief or learned judge. A wealthy widow, female landholder or renowned learned woman could sometimes compel a hearing, but this depended on property, kin and reputation rather than an equal right. The exclusion mattered because the assembly decided marriages, captive status and compensation affecting people who could not address it directly.

Hearth-law required a truthful name, food and shelter under dangerous conditions. It did not oblige a household to accept every armed stranger or protect a guest after proven violence. Winter refuge carried stronger protection than ordinary summer hospitality. Burning a refuge, poisoning common water or attacking during an agreed Grobi truce could place a household beyond compensation.

Folk tellers teach the guest's side of hearth-law through **Wolf and Lantern**, two rogues of the Hearths age who fled their pursuers into the forest and begged shelter from the **Old Mother**, a long-lived witch whose House walked. She kept the pursuers out and did not promise to let her guests leave. They mocked her age, plotted against her treasury and insulted her Daughter, and the House answered each offence with an ordeal: the Borrowed Years, the Treasury That Takes and the **Bride of Teeth**. The last ended at a village revel, where Wolf paid two fingers of his knife hand to buy back a child the Daughter had considered eating. The cycle fixed in Folk custom that a guest who mocks a host forfeits protection and that a witch's bargain follows its words. It also left a kind of household behind it. Several witch lines later claimed descent or apprenticeship through the Daughter, and assemblies argued for generations whether such women were kin to be consulted or a danger to be driven off. Travellers still leave food, jokes or two small carved fingers where a road enters old forest. The full cycle is [[G-06 - The Wolf, the Lantern and the House That Heard Them Laugh|The Wolf, the Lantern and the House That Heard Them Laugh]].

Southern craft produced durable landscapes even when timber vanished. Coppiced woods, charcoal grounds, drained fields, diverted streams, mine scars, rebuilt hall platforms, bridge piles and deep middens record generations of work.

People also enjoyed their skill. Belathi fairs prized riddles which embarrassed powerful hosts without ending hospitality. Hartheni spoon contests rewarded a ladle able to survive a winter cauldron. Odrani strongman tales regularly ended with the hero unable to repair the door he had lifted from its hinges. Vezhari boat races allowed the losing crew to demand a meal from the winners on the ground that defeat had left them too tired to cook.

## Faith and historical memory

Folk religion remained local. Learned houses, household rites, oath places, groves, river offerings and public cult centres coexisted. A power which protected one ford might demand an unjust price.

Belathi songs remembered sovereignty, hospitality and difficult foster ties. Hartheni traditions remembered assemblies, compensation and dangerous victory water. Odrani and Kreseni epics celebrated princes, merchants and Deep-Voiced champions while disagreeing about the same wars. Later Enaidfae networks inherited some offices and stories.
