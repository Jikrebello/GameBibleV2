# Folk Daily Life and the Thirteen Moons

How the Old Blood lives between raids, winter watches, and treaties: the year, the table, hearth-law, and song. Companion to [[The Folk of the Frontier and the Wolfsmere Lineage]]; these customs are strongest among the Green March Kindreds, while other Folk regions vary and the Ättar keep their own reckoning.

## The Thirteen Moons and the Four Fires

The Folk year is **thirteen moons**, each named for what it demands (Hunger Moon, Sap Moon, Salmon Moon, Antler Moon, the Long Dark...), hinged on **four great fire-festivals** at the quarter-days:

- **Bright-Fire** (spring): cattle driven between two bonfires; courting season opens; the moon-count's new tally-sticks are cut. Falls irritatingly near the Church's Greenveil processions, and the borderland's young people attend both for the same reasons young people ever attend anything.
- **High-Fire** (midsummer): the clan-moots; treaties sworn and feuds priced; games, horse-trades, and the seers' weather-court for the year ahead.
- **Last-Fire** (harvest): debts of meat and grain settled; the marriage-fires (see below); the night the Grey-Withy publish, by song, who owes whom.
- **The Quiet Fire** (winter's mouth): the ancestor-night, when hearths burn low, doors stand open one hour for the dead, and — on the Wolfsmere shore — the **Roll of the Drowned** begins: four thousand names, one breath each, three nights. Church writers who call the Quiet Fire "the Folk's Souls-month" are nearer right than either people likes.

**Two clocks, one border:** every market-day, muster, and truce along the frontier is scheduled twice — a Church date and a moon-count — and the mismatch is a standing negotiation. Grenzburg's treaty-fairs fall "third Lord's Day of Harvestmonth *or* the full of the Barley Moon, whichever the river allows," a formula that has prevented and started roughly equal numbers of skirmishes.

## Hearth-Law (hospitality and its edges)

The hearth is a jurisdiction (the same threshold-law that stops vampires — the Folk never forgot why). A guest received at the fire is inviolate three days; the third morning he is either kin, employee, or gone. Salt refused is a declaration; a name given falsely at the fire is the worst act short of kin-slaying, because the hearth *witnessed* it. Fugitives know all this, which is why the Marches' outlaw-trails run door to door in winter, and why the Duke's writ dies at every lintel.

## Food

Forest economy: venison and boar, smoked eel and salmon, pine-bark flour in the Hunger Moon (poverty-food, then defiantly a festival bread), hazelnuts, bilberries, honey and the mead it becomes, hard cheeses aged in caves, and the great winter porridges that take a day and feed a hall. The Folk fast nothing on principle and share everything by rule — the Church's meatless calendar strikes them as a landlord's trick played on God's behalf, and they say so at market.

## Marriage

Made at Last-Fire by **handfasting across the flame**: a year-and-a-day compact, renewable or dissoluble at the next Last-Fire without shame — permanence is *earned*, an arrangement Church canonists have condemned for three centuries and Church peasants have quietly envied for the same three. Bride-price runs in cattle and knives; a seer ties the wrist-cord and names the year's terms. Chapel-Folk marry twice, fire and altar both, and each rite's officiant politely does not ask about the other.

## Death

The Folk do not bury in Church ground and the reason is a wound, not a doctrine: *ground can be taken* (Wolfsmere proved what happens to the dead when it is). The dead are given to **cairn, hollow oak, or river-boat** by clan custom; names go into the Speaker's memory-chain, recited backward at the Quiet Fire ("we walk with the past" — the far-southern peoples would recognize the instinct). A Folk deathbed asks for water, not a lamp: *"the road is wet where we go"* — a line the Church's missioners find leftward-alarming, the seers decline to explain, and the Lakekeepers alone could gloss.

**The keen (sung once, at the cairn, then never again for that name):**
> *Go by the roots and not by the road, / the trees know your name, the stones take your load;*
> *heed no voice from under the mere — / the fire remembers you here, you here,*
> *the fire remembers you here.*

(That third line again. Both peoples keep the same warning in their grave-songs. Neither will discuss it.)

## Songs, Sayings, and the Rude Stuff

**High-Fire round (sung by rival clans at each other, verses improvised):**
> *Moss-Crown counts its acorns, / Ash-Spear counts its scars,*
> *Ghost-Foot counts what you had yesterday, / and Grey-Withy counts the stars —*
> *but come the Fire, come the Fire, / all the counting's done:*
> *one wood, one winter, one dark to share, / and quarrels weigh a ton.*

**Sayings:** *"You may have our lake; you will not have our shepherd."* (refusing to surrender what matters after losing what mattered) — *"Three hearths quarrel over one fire, and none of them asked the wood."* (the standard verdict on Church politics) — *"Bent oak, living oak."* (survival over pride; always said with a wince) — *"Don't sell the drake's quiet."* (don't trade away a protection you don't understand).

**The joke the clans tell about the Church:** A missioner asks a Grey-Withy speaker, "Do your people not fear damnation?" The speaker considers. "We fear the lake. We fear the Long Dark. We fear a bad Salmon Moon." The missioner presses: "But the fires of Hell?" — "Priest," says the speaker, "we would *welcome* a fire that reliable."

## Class and Grievance, Folk-Style

The Folk have no peasants but they have poverty: cattle-poor families client themselves to cattle-rich ones, and a bad seer can starve a clan as surely as a bad harvest. The Chapel-Folk carry the sharpest griefs — taxed by the Duke, tithed by the Church, distrusted by both bloods — and their Bent Oak elders have begun, very quietly, attending Freeflame meetings, where nobody demands they forget their grandmothers. If that current ever becomes a river, the frontier's whole religious map moves.

## In Grenzburg

The Duke's year and the Folk's year grind against each other at every point above; the treaty-fair formula, the double-clock musters, and the Quiet Fire's open doors during Grobi season (an act of insane courage or perfect law, depending who you ask) are all live campaign material. And the Lakekeeper's Roll begins three nights after the walls close for winter — meaning Grenzburg's most dangerous season and the Folk's most sacred one always coincide, which is either terrible luck or the lake's own scheduling.
