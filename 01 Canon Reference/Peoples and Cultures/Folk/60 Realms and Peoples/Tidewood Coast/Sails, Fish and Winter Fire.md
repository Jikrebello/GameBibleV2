# Sails, Fish and Winter Fire

On the Three Beaches, much of the year is preparation for a journey whose weather no one can promise. A family may own part of a boat, repair someone else's nets, cut fuel and send a daughter inland with winter volunteers. Calling it a fishing household describes its chief income, not everything its members know how to do.

## Fish before silver

Fresh fish is a pleasure because so much of the catch must be preserved. A large arrival fills the shore with cutting, washing, carrying, salting and arguments about which racks need mending. Fish spoiled through delay cannot be recovered by a Speaker's promise. Children carry lighter loads and learn to recognize the smell of a batch going wrong.

Drying depends on air as well as salt. Smokehouses need fuel that might otherwise warm a dwelling or heat a workshop. Salt comes through trade and local brine work; cold weather can help concentrate it, but boiling the remaining brine still consumes wood. A household with good nets but no fuel has not solved its winter problem.

Grain arrives through inland exchange and visiting ships. Small plots, animals, shore gathering and forest foods supplement it. No single source feeds every inlet. The ordinary bowl may contain fish, grain, greens or whatever the household has been able to keep. A rich feast adds foods brought far enough to become a subject of conversation.

The first bowl is passed before the cook eats. It expresses hospitality and participation; it is not automatically an offering to the Court of Salt and Teeth. After [[The Summer Without Wrecks]], some descendant households insist that a guest may carry a portion away rather than eat under a host's watch. Children usually understand the rule as permission to visit a friend with supper before learning its darker history.

## Wood that must remain useful

Boat timber is chosen for what a particular part must endure. A naturally curved piece can be more useful than a straight impressive trunk. Workers need time to bring wood down, prepare it and keep it out of the worst conditions. Buyers who count standing trees without asking those questions can order a forest's worth of expensive failures.

[[Rookstrand]] yards mix local practice with methods learned from foreign crews. Repairs travel in both directions. A master may speak contemptuously of an Ättar hull while copying the way its builder protected a working joint. Young workers carry techniques when they marry, leave a quarrel or find an employer who will pay them.

Iron is common at the principal yards but not unlimited. Good tools are maintained, borrowed, stolen and inherited. Smaller inlets still use bone, antler, stone or carefully saved bronze where those materials serve the work. Possessing an imported steel axe does not give an isolated household access to an entire foreign industry's tools.

The [[The Beam War|Beam War]] is remembered by cutters as well as fishers. A family earning money from timber can also depend upon the water damaged by transporting it. Winter discussions become sharp when an overseas order might buy both a new roof and the means to destroy a neighbour's livelihood.

## The sailmakers' hands

Sails absorb years of repairs. Their patches tell where strain has run, where a tear was caught early and where someone used poor material because there was nothing else. [[Edris Sailmaker]]'s example is remembered most often while judging whether an old panel can work again.

The Common Loft at [[Brackenmouth]] supplies usable cloth and fittings for urgent repairs. Donors expect labour as well as thanks. A crew that takes good material and returns spoiled scraps may find little help next time. When the loft is short, sailmakers must decide which damaged boat can safely wait. That decision is not a judgement on the worth of the lives aboard it.

Coastal women are prominent in sail and net work, but neither craft belongs exclusively to one sex. Seafaring households put skill where the work requires it. Owners who mock sewing may discover that the person they insulted is the only worker able to finish their sail before departure.

## A year measured by work

Tidewood uses local forms of the Folk moon reckoning. Its seasonal names emphasize fish runs, hauling, storm preparations and the opening of stored food. The four fire-festivals remain recognizable, although ceremonies differ from those of the Green March. A visitor who assumes the [[01 Canon Reference/Peoples and Cultures/Folk/30 Society and Daily Life/Folk Daily Life and the Thirteen Moons.md|Green March calendar]] is a complete guide will miss some coastal preparations.

Spring opening includes release cuts on storehouse fittings and, in some households, burial cloth. These belong to the [[G-67 - The Marks of Necessary Release|withheld-season inheritance]]. They do not require every household to claim descent from the woman in the story.

Summer gatherings bring races, wrestling, repairs, marriages and the recruitment of crews. The traditional prize for one small-boat race is a meal cooked by the losing crew. It is much disputed whether deliberately catching the dinner during the race counts as abandoning the course. Old participants can explain every ruling and still disagree about their own youthful victory.

At launches, children repeat [[The Boat Too Fine for Water]]. A new vessel wears a plain patch, often temporary, and its master must tolerate advice from people who have never built one. The custom gives pride a public landing: a successful launch is still celebrated, but perfection is not allowed to keep everyone waiting ashore.

## Winter company

Before the hardest weather, boats, preserved food and volunteers leave for the [[Frost-Road and the Winter War|Frost-Road]]. Tidewood's danger is uneven. Some exposed inlets face more storms than Grobi; distant fighting can nevertheless divert food and relatives from them. Frozen estuaries may become approaches for attackers rather than barriers.

Those remaining work near the fire. They mend, carve, tell stories and listen for a returning crew. Some households hold old-rite funerals; Chapel-Folk keep their own prayers and burial practices. The coast has no single song for every death.

A common winter pastime is to begin an account of a voyage with an impossible boast and let listeners dismantle it. The best liar leaves one ordinary true detail that the others miss. The worst insists upon being believed and is asked to mend something useful instead.
