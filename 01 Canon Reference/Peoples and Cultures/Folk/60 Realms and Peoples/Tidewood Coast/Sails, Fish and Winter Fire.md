# Sails, Fish and Winter Fire

Household life on the **Three Beaches** combines fishing, food preservation, boat work, woodland use and seasonal travel. A family may hold a share in a vessel while repairing another crew's nets, gathering fuel and sending a daughter inland with winter volunteers. These overlapping activities allow households to contribute to several kinds of work and to maintain connections beyond their own landing.

Much of the year is spent preparing supplies and equipment for voyages. Weather determines when boats can leave, which foods can be preserved and how long a settlement may have to rely on its stores. Skills in mending, judging materials and recovering useful parts are therefore important throughout coastal life.

## Fish before silver

A large catch fills the shore with cutting, washing, carrying and salting. Fish must be processed promptly to prevent spoilage, and damaged racks can delay the work. Children carry lighter loads and learn to identify a batch beginning to turn. Fresh fish is especially welcome in households whose winter meals depend heavily on preserved food.

Drying requires suitable air conditions as well as salt. Smokehouses consume wood also needed for dwellings and workshops. Salt is obtained through trade and local brine work; freezing weather can help concentrate brine, after which boiling still requires fuel. Fishing equipment, preservation space and a wood supply together determine how much of a catch a household can keep.

Grain arrives through inland exchange and visiting ships. Small plots, animals, shore gathering and forest foods supplement these supplies in different proportions from inlet to inlet. Ordinary meals combine fish, grain and greens according to what has been stored. At richer feasts, foods carried from distant places become a subject of conversation as well as a part of the meal.

The first bowl is passed to another person before the cook eats. This customary sharing expresses hospitality and participation. Following [[The Summer Without Wrecks]], some descendant households also insist that a guest may take food away rather than eat at a compulsory table. Children often first encounter the practice as permission to visit a friend with supper, learning its association with the wrecking inlet later.

## Wood that must remain useful

Boatwrights select timber according to the strain and shape required of each part. A naturally curved piece can be valuable for work that a straight trunk would serve poorly. Felling is followed by hauling, preparation and storage, all of which affect the usefulness of the final timber. Purchases based solely on the number of standing trees can consequently produce unsuitable or damaged material.

[[Rookstrand]] yards combine local methods with techniques learned from visiting crews. Knowledge moves in both directions as workers marry, leave after quarrels or seek better wages. A master may disparage an Ättar hull and still adopt its protected joint, providing younger workers with material for jokes about the supposed antiquity of workshop methods.

Iron is widely used at the principal yards, where tools are maintained, borrowed, inherited and sometimes stolen. Smaller inlets also employ bone, antler, stone and saved bronze for suitable tasks. Imported steel tools remain valuable possessions in households whose other equipment is made locally.

The [[The Beam War|Beam War]] illustrates the close relationship between timber income and other work. A cutting household might earn from an overseas order while depending on water damaged by the transport of its own logs. Such connections continue to complicate discussions of new roofs, better tools and the effects of increased cutting on neighbouring livelihoods.

## The sailmakers' hands

Repeated repairs leave a sail with panels of different ages and strengths. Their arrangement shows where strain has fallen, where a tear was stopped and where poor material had to serve because better cloth was unavailable. [[Edris Sailmaker]] is frequently recalled when workers assess whether a worn panel can be fitted for another voyage.

The Common Loft at [[Brackenmouth]] provides usable cloth and fittings for urgent repairs. Donors expect the receiving crew to contribute labour and return material responsibly. Crews that replace good panels with spoiled scraps may receive less assistance later. When supplies are short, sailmakers judge which damaged vessels can safely wait.

Women are prominent in sail and net work, and men also practise both crafts. Seafaring households rely on whichever members have the necessary skill. Jokes about owners who belittle sewing often concern their eventual dependence on the worker able to finish a sail before departure.

## A year measured by work

Tidewood follows local forms of Folk moon reckoning. Seasonal names emphasise fish runs, hauling, preparation for storms and the opening of stored food. The four fire-festivals remain recognisable, with ceremonies adapted to coastal work. The [[01 Canon Reference/Peoples and Cultures/Folk/30 Society and Daily Life/Folk Daily Life and the Thirteen Moons.md|Green March calendar]] provides a related inland example, while coastal households prepare for the opportunities and hazards of their own shores.

Spring customs include release cuts on storehouse fittings and, in some households, burial cloth. These marks belong to the [[G-67 - The Marks of Necessary Release|withheld-season inheritance]], transmitted from the Tidewood–Gale-Crown watershed through marriage and timber routes. Their local use connects the opening of provisions and the treatment of the dead with the remembered inland catastrophe.

Summer gatherings bring boat races, wrestling, marriages, repairs and the recruitment of crews. The prize in one small-boat race is a meal cooked by the losing crew. Participants dispute whether catching dinner during the race amounts to abandoning the course, and arguments over remembered youthful victories can continue for many years.

At launches, a new vessel wears a plain patch while children repeat [[The Boat Too Fine for Water]]. The master must tolerate advice from spectators, including those who have never built a boat. The custom makes the vessel's first use a shared occasion and allows the crowd to tease its owner before celebrating a successful launch.

## Winter company

Before the hardest weather, boats, preserved food and volunteers leave for the [[Frost-Road and the Winter War|Frost-Road]]. Grobi pressure varies along the coast. Some inlets face greater immediate danger from storms, yet fighting elsewhere draws on their food and sends relatives away. Frozen estuaries can also provide new approaches for attackers.

Households remaining near the shore mend equipment, carve and tell stories around their fires while waiting for returning crews. Old-rite funerals and Chapel-Folk prayers preserve different burial practices within communities that share much daily work.

One winter pastime begins with an impossible boast about a voyage, which listeners attempt to dismantle. A skilled teller conceals an ordinary true detail among the inventions. Someone who insists on being believed may instead be asked to repair a useful object. The pleasure lies in recognising both the seamanship and the familiar exaggerations on which the account depends.
