# The Summer Without Wrecks

The **Summer Without Wrecks** is the name given to the collapse of a prosperous fishing inlet below [[Keldscar and Gannet Head|Gannet Head]] between 710 and 712 AR. Its people had come to depend on an extraordinary catch associated with shipwreck offerings. When ordinary losses stopped supplying the rite, influential households began manufacturing wrecks.

The name is deliberately bitter. The safer summer which should have been welcomed became the reason to lure sailors onto rocks. Later accounts differ about the power behind the offerings, but the false lights, murders and destruction of the inlet are established history.

## Fish that found the nets

The inlet had struggled with poor access and unreliable catches. Offerings at a stone below the cliff were followed by schools of fish entering water they normally passed. Fishers built racks, enlarged houses and bought better boats. Families from other shores married into the settlement. [[Brenna Half-Sail]]'s household was among those that accepted fish and helped provision voyages there.

Seer **Iven Teethstring** called the giver the **Guest with Shark Teeth**. He displayed salvaged cargo beside the feeding stone and described the drowned as the sea's contribution to a shared feast. Households accustomed to offerings of bone, salt or wreckage did not all understand the same thing by that language. Some initially believed they were returning goods already lost beyond rescue.

There were early dissenters. Fishers who shared catches away from the offering place lost access to its nets. A household that refused to expose its recovered cargo at the stone was driven from a drying yard. The abundance was being organized into a dependence before the first false light was set.

## A light moved down the shore

Improved pilotage and a run of favourable passages reduced wrecks. The miraculous catch failed. Households that had expanded their boats and taken advances could not meet their promises with ordinary fishing. Iven argued that the Guest had not withdrawn its gift: the people had stopped supplying its portion.

His supporters moved lights to the wrong approach. A returning vessel struck rocks under the head. Shore parties took its cargo and killed survivors who had seen enough to identify the deception. More losses followed. Some inhabitants helped willingly; some stayed silent because their children ate the catch; others were threatened when they tried to leave.

Brenna recognized rigging from one wrecked vessel. She had repaired it at Brackenmouth and remembered the visiting crew's preparations. Its appearance at the inlet contradicted the claim that the cargo had drifted from an old loss. She carried a warning to the other harbours, knowing that her own family had benefited from the fish.

## Breaking the feeding shore

An armed party reached the inlet in 712 with boats ready to carry captives out. Iven's followers fought them on the steep path and beside the offering place. They drowned a captive sailor at the stone while trying to restore the failing catch. Brenna's younger brother **Teren** died helping pull survivors through the surf.

The rescuers overturned the feeding stone and broke the cargo stages and channels connecting it to the working shore. Witnesses described a mouth opening beneath the spilled goods; others saw only the water behaving impossibly beneath their feet. Iven was killed while resisting at the stone. Several people who had helped set the lights escaped inland.

The fish dispersed. There was no miraculous replacement harvest for the families who had opposed the killings. Ordinary fishing could not support every household that had moved into the inlet during its prosperity. Survivors left, and winter reduced the occupied settlement to scattered structures and a few people unwilling or unable to abandon their homes.

## The people who carried the story away

Some rescued children travelled in boats crewed by people who had just killed their parents. Bereaved households from visiting ships demanded punishment for anyone found at the inlet. Keldscar families who took survivors in were accused of sheltering wreckers. Later testimony distinguished particular acts, but no complete list of the guilty or innocent survived.

Brenna returned to ordinary voyages and helped displaced households obtain food. She could not bring Teren back or free herself of having accepted the inlet's earlier abundance. When a song credited her with calming the sea, she reportedly answered that she had spent most of the landing trying not to drown. The retort belongs to her received life; the landing and her brother's death do not depend on its exact wording.

## Two religious memories

Chapel accounts commonly present the disaster as proof that no offering to the shore powers is safe. Old-rite critics insist that the Guest had usurped a practice quite different from exchanges with the Net-Mother at another pool. Both traditions preserve the killings. Neither has the right to make every child from the inlet a hereditary wrecker.

The public story does not settle the identity of every thing called the Court of Salt and Teeth. Its author-only distinctions are recorded in [[Tidewood Historical Development - Authorial Review]].

The ruined inlet remains below Gannet Head. The custom of refusing a catch that must be eaten at one compulsory table is strongest among its scattered descendants. It is a human refusal that can cost a household food, not a charm guaranteeing protection.
