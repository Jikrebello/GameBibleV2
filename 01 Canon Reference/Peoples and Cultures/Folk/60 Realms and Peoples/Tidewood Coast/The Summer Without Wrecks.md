# The Summer Without Wrecks

The **Summer Without Wrecks** was the collapse of a prosperous fishing settlement beneath [[Keldscar and Gannet Head|Gannet Head]] between 710 and 712 AR. The inlet's extraordinary catches depended on shipwreck offerings to the being known as the **Guest with Shark Teeth**. As improved pilotage reduced losses, influential households began using false lights to lure vessels onto the rocks and obtain bodies and cargo for the feeding place.

An armed landing in 712 ended the wrecking and destroyed the structures used for the offerings. The catch then ceased, and most surviving households dispersed. The name recalls the reversal through which a season of safer passages became a threat to the inlet's livelihood.

## Fish that found the nets

Before the extraordinary catches, the inlet had suffered from poor access and unreliable fishing. Offerings at a stone below the cliff were followed by schools entering water they usually passed. Fishers built racks, enlarged houses and bought better boats. People from other shores married into the settlement. [[Brenna Half-Sail]]'s household accepted fish and helped provision voyages there.

Seer **Iven Teethstring** named the giver the Guest with Shark Teeth. He displayed salvaged cargo beside the feeding stone and described the drowned as the sea's contribution to a shared feast. Households already accustomed to offerings of salt, bone and wreckage understood this explanation differently. Some believed they were returning goods that had been lost beyond rescue.

Control of the catch brought pressure to participate. Fishers who shared food away from the offering place lost access to its nets. A household refusing to expose recovered cargo at the stone was driven from a drying yard. These measures made access to the abundance increasingly dependent on compliance with Iven and his supporters.

## A light moved down the shore

Improved pilotage and favourable passages reduced the supply of wreckage, and the extraordinary catch failed. Households that had enlarged their boats and taken advances could not meet their commitments through ordinary fishing. Iven attributed the failure to their diminished provision of the Guest's portion.

His supporters moved lights to an unsafe approach. A returning ship struck the rocks, and shore parties seized its cargo and killed survivors able to identify the deception. Further losses followed. Some inhabitants assisted willingly, others remained silent while their children ate the catch, and some were threatened when they attempted to leave.

Brenna recognised rigging from a vessel she had repaired at Brackenmouth. She remembered its crew's preparations, making the claim that its cargo had drifted from an old loss implausible. Her warning reached other harbours despite threats and her knowledge that her own family had benefited from the inlet's fish.

## Breaking the feeding shore

An armed party reached the inlet in 712 with boats prepared to evacuate captives. Iven's followers resisted on the steep path and around the offering place. During the fighting they drowned a captive sailor at the stone in an attempt to restore the catch. Brenna's younger brother **Teren** died pulling survivors through the surf.

The attackers overturned the feeding stone and broke the cargo stages and channels linking it to the working shore. Some witnesses described a mouth opening beneath the spilled goods; others recalled impossible movements of the water. Iven was killed while resisting at the stone. Several people involved in setting the lights escaped inland.

The fish dispersed after the feeding place was broken. Ordinary catches were insufficient for the enlarged settlement, including households that had opposed the killings. Most survivors left. By winter, scattered structures and a few people unwilling or unable to depart remained from the prosperous inlet.

## The people who carried the story away

Rescue brought together people with sharply different losses. Some children travelled in boats crewed by those who had just killed their parents. Families bereaved by wrecks demanded punishment for anyone found at the inlet, and Keldscar households taking in survivors were accused of sheltering wreckers. Later testimony distinguished individual acts, but a complete account of participation has not survived.

Brenna resumed fishing and repair voyages and helped displaced households obtain food. Teren's death and her earlier acceptance of the catch remained part of her own relationship with the catastrophe. A received story describes her answering a song that credited her with calming the sea by saying that she had spent most of the landing trying to avoid drowning. The wording belongs to the later telling; the rescue work and her brother's death are preserved in accounts of the event.

## Two religious memories

Chapel accounts commonly present the catastrophe as a warning against all offerings to shore powers. Old-rite critics distinguish the Guest's demands from exchanges with the Net-Mother at a separate tidal pool. Both traditions preserve the murders, while differing over the implications for religious practice elsewhere on the coast.

Among the dispersed descendants, a custom developed of refusing food that must be eaten at a compulsory shared table. Guests may instead take their portion away. This practice recalls the inlet's use of food to enforce participation, and observing it can still cost a household access to a catch it needs.

The ruined inlet remains beneath Gannet Head. Its scattered house platforms, drying posts and broken feeding place connect present visits with a history transmitted through survivors, rescuers and the families of lost sailors.
