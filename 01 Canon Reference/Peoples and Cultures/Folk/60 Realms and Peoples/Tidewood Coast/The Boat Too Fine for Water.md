# The Boat Too Fine for Water

**The Boat Too Fine for Water** is a comic tale performed around [[Rookstrand]]'s yards and at boat launches elsewhere in the Three Beaches. It concerns a craftsman's reluctance to use his finished vessel and accompanies the custom of displaying a plain patch on a new boat. The tale is attested by the late imperial centuries, although its original teller, date of composition and any historical model for the master remain unknown.

## The tale

A boatwright finished a boat so fine that he would not launch it.

The first morning he found a knot in a plank that resembled a squinting eye. He replaced the plank. On the second morning he disliked the colour of the line. On the third he said the water was too dirty. His wife asked whether he meant to build a cleaner sea, and he replied that she understood nothing about quality.

Visitors came to admire the vessel. The master charged them a little fish for the privilege and soon discovered that a boat standing still could feed a household. He polished it every day. His apprentices repaired other people's working craft and waited for him to teach them how his masterpiece sailed.

One autumn his wife went away to visit her sister. The master ate the last of the gifts. Rain kept visitors indoors. He took his old fishing boat out, but a seam opened and he came back wet, furious and carrying less dinner than he had taken with him.

He asked the apprentices to repair it. They said they could not possibly begin until they found wood without a knot, line of the proper colour and water clean enough to test the result. Each then offered to admire his magnificent new boat in exchange for a meal.

That night a storm threatened the beach. The master ordered everyone to save the masterpiece first. His youngest apprentice asked whether it was a boat or a particularly troublesome house. A boat could be launched into the sheltered water and held there. A house required a great deal of carrying.

The master called for the rollers.

The launch broke a polished fitting. A flying splinter tore the sail. There was no time to make either perfect, so the apprentices secured one with an ugly brace and patched the other with the master's finest apron. The boat floated. It took its crew out of the breaking water and brought them back the next morning.

His wife returned to find a muddy vessel, a ruined apron and enough fish for supper.

“At last,” she said, “you have finished it.”

The master agreed. He spent the winter teaching his apprentices how to mend it.

## At the launch

A new Rookstrand boat wears a plain patch or scrap where the crowd can see it. Children ask whether it is a boat or a house, and the owner must answer before hauling begins. This exchange recalls the apprentice's question in the tale and directs the teasing towards the owner's willingness to put the vessel to use.

Crews accustomed to worn equipment particularly enjoy the custom's recognition of a sound repair. The story gives the first damage to new work a place within its useful life. At Brackenmouth's Common Loft, children also perform it with a bench as the boat and an adult playing the sea.

## Other tellings

In an Ättar version, the owner is a boastful young jarl and the least honoured member of his crew supplies the patch. Rookstrand masters prefer the workshop version because the apprentices get the final practical victory. Children often add a crab that has been living under the hull for three years and must be paid to leave.

The workshop, household and crew versions draw on familiar relationships between skilled workers and the people who depend on them. Their changes allow the same story to be told at a master's launch, a communal gathering or a children's performance.
