# Brenna Half-Sail

**Brenna Half-Sail** (671–736 AR) was a coastal repair worker and fisher who helped expose the wreckers beneath Gannet Head and assisted survivors during the destruction of their settlement in 712. Her subsequent voyages and efforts to feed displaced households made her an important figure in the memory of [[The Summer Without Wrecks]].

The name Half-Sail began with an employer's joke about the small quantity of cloth she had salvaged. Brenna used it to make a smaller working sail and returned with cargo the employer had expected to lose. The nickname continued after she could afford better materials.

## A household with reasons not to ask

Brenna worked between [[Brackenmouth]]'s repair yards and Keld family fishing boats. The extraordinary catches below Gannet Head supplied her relatives, and her household benefited from the inlet's growing prosperity. This involvement helps explain her slow acceptance of warnings that its offering place had become dangerous.

Her recognition of rigging from a vessel she had repaired supplied a more immediate reason for enquiry. The visiting crew had prepared to sail past the head, and its equipment was now among the inlet's salvage. Brenna's questions brought threats. Her younger brother **Teren** helped carry the warning beyond households dependent on the catch, making it possible to seek assistance from other harbours.

## The landing

Brenna helped prepare the boats for the armed landing in 712 and carried survivors away from the offering place during the fighting. Teren died in the surf while assisting them. Witnesses differed over whether a wave or an attacker first brought him down.

The party overturned the feeding stone and destroyed the structures used to supply it. With the end of the extraordinary catch, ordinary fishing could no longer sustain all the households that had gathered in the inlet. The rescue and destruction were therefore followed by displacement and severe hardship among survivors.

Brenna's later reputation centred on this landing, although her working life continued for another two decades. She died ashore in 736. Songs that end with the battle compress a much longer period of fishing, repairs and assistance to the displaced.

## The less-sung years

Brenna argued for food for households driven from the inlet, including some whose relatives had contributed to Teren's death. Her defence of their children provoked opposition from neighbours who resented the assistance. Several refused to send crews on her voyages.

Her own household continued old-rite practices while refusing the Guest's compulsory feast. Later chapel tellers represented her as a secret convert, while some old-rite admirers described her as a favourite of the Net-Mother. Neither account has been established. Both interpret a life whose surviving practical concerns included rescue, fishing and provision for families left without a livelihood.

Several descendant households place a small damaged float beside the first shared catch. It is renewed when it rots and represents a boat kept ready to carry someone away. Repeated replacement has made it a household commemoration maintained by each succeeding generation.

One fuller local telling ends with Brenna as an old woman mending cloth while younger people debate the meaning of a storm. The scene gives her later working years a place beside the more commonly sung landing and her brother's death.
