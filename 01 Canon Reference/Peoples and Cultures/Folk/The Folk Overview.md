# The Folk Overview

The Folk are the clan peoples of the southern forests, mountain ranges, cold coasts and Eclessian marches. Their communities range from a few related longhouses to fortified confederacies, river leagues and coastal fleets. Kinship, hearth-law and obligations of winter shelter connect them across considerable differences in wealth, government and religion.

Local belonging is usually expressed first through a kindred or region: Moss-Crown, Grey-Withy, Bent Oak, Tidewood or Gale-Crown. Fäskogarfolk is an archaic scholarly name for the ancestral population from which the Folk and the [[01 Canon Reference/Peoples and Cultures/Ättar/Ättar Overview.md|Ättar]] developed. “Old Blood” remains a proud ceremonial expression among their descendants.

## Origins and historical development

Folk ancestors survived the fall of Giant rule in their own lands. These Orphaned populations preserved and rebuilt communities amid damaged settlements, predatory powers and the loss of older systems. Their histories developed independently of the Glass migration. Later contact with southern Atherian settlers brought conquest, trade, recruitment and intermarriage to particular clans and regions.

Related Ättar societies developed a stronger concentration of hall authority and distinctive responses to Grobi pressure. Connections between the two peoples continued through kinship, voyages and winter service. The [[The Making of the Folk|formation of the Folk]] and [[Historical Spine of the Folk|their historical spine]] describe these relationships and the changing reach of neighbouring empires.

In the present world, imperial borders divide some Folk populations while leaving others well beyond regular outside government. Eclessian pressure is especially strong in the Green March. Deeper forest and mountain communities have histories shaped by their own confederacies, feuds, resources and local powers.

## Regions and settlement

The [[Green March Kindreds]] occupy the northern frontier of the Folk lands. Fortified settlements, mature ironworking and contact with Eclessia support larger political associations. Chapel-Folk communities are prominent here, and disputes over land and conversion accompany the exchange of goods and techniques.

The [[Rootbound Heartwood]] contains old-rite confederacies, sacred groves and extensive customs governing the use of particular places. Enaidfae identity is strongest in these forest societies. Their settlements and productive lands are closely connected with relationships maintained by seers.

The [[Gale-Crown Ranges]] support shepherd clans, mines and hillforts. Passes concentrate travel, allowing their holders to collect tolls and influence exchange. Feuding warrior households and storm courts are important features of the mountain world.

On the [[Tidewood Coast]], fishing, salt, timber and cold-water sailing sustain shore settlements and fortified anchorages. Ättar and Sestaran ships connect its harbours to wider waters. [[History of the Tidewood Coast]] provides a continuous regional account through the communities of Brackenmouth, Rookstrand and Keldscar.

## Households, government and obligation

The household and kindred organise access to land, food and protection. Clan territory is understood to belong to remembered kin, the living community and the powers attached to it. A chief cannot sell such land on personal authority alone. Seasonal use, burial places, water and woodland access all enter disputes over possession.

The common offices are Speaker, War-Keeper and Seer. Larger societies also employ judges, maintain champion retinues, receive tribute and hold public moots. Alliances may elevate a temporary war-king, while authority over the whole Folk world remains beyond any one office.

Hearth-law binds hosts and accepted guests through truthful names, food and sanctuary. Honour is demonstrated by difficult obligations: protecting dependants, paying a feud-price, sharing winter stores and taking a watch. Feuds involve cattle raids, hostages, champions and compensation, with seasonal truces intended to preserve survival. Burning a refuge hall, poisoning a common well or breaking a winter truce places a clan outside ordinary feud-law.

## Work, skill and exchange

Available ore, charcoal, roads and trading partners produce substantial differences in technology. Remote communities make extensive use of stone, bone, antler, hide and inherited bronze, alongside limited bloomery iron. Carpentry, weaving, food preservation, traps, boats and herbal practice may be highly developed in the same settlements.

Larger river communities and fortified confederacies make iron tools, mail, wagons and watermills. Gale-Crown smiths export iron; Tidewood shipwrights build vessels for cold seas; Green March communities adopt Eclessian masonry, crossbows, ploughs and sawmills. [[Folk Economy and Technology]] explains these regional patterns.

Cattle, food, iron, boats, cloth, amber, salt and timber rights are major forms of wealth. Coin circulates near markets and borders. Timber, resin, honey, wax, furs, medicines, livestock and preserved fish travel outward; steel, grain, wine, books, glass and specialised mill parts travel inward. Powerful lineages can control smithies and tolls, while cattle-poor households become clients. Captive service and the sale of people occur under differing local customs.

## Faith and seasonal life

Old-rite communities seek workable relations with the powers associated with forests, water, storms, roads and hunger. Seers conduct offerings and maintain taboos intended to avert unwanted attention. A power that fulfils one bargain may remain dangerous to the household that made it.

Folk accounts group varied beings into local courts according to their conduct and the places where people encounter them. Songs, seasonal practices and inherited warnings preserve useful knowledge, although tellers disagree about the identities behind particular appearances. [[Folk Faith and Appeasement]] and [[Courts of the Folk Lands]] describe these traditions.

The [[Chapel-Folk]] worship the Lord through the Redeemer. They retain their language, songs, kindreds, hearth-law and compatible seasonal customs while renouncing sacrifice and worship offered to the courts. Some missioners demand a more complete abandonment of local practices, while old-rite zealots oppose the converts' refusal to maintain ancestral offerings.

## Winter obligations and the present coast and marches

Grobi migration makes winter defence important far beyond the most exposed settlements. Frontier communities maintain stores, beacons, refuge halls, scouts and fortified crossings. Warriors from safer country take the [[Frost-Road and the Winter War|Frost-Road]], earning standing and followers through service. Coastal households contribute boats, provisions and winter volunteers.

In 1360, these obligations coexist with foreign demand for timber and iron, disputes over communal land, conversion and rivalry among local kindreds. Songs, hospitality, skilled work and seasonal gatherings sustain relationships through those pressures.
