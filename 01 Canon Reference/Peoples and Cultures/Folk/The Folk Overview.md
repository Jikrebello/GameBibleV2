# The Folk Overview

Historical reading: [[Historical Spine of the Folk]], within the [[World Historical Atlas MOC]].

For a continuous regional history, follow [[History of the Tidewood Coast]] through its harbours, boatworkers, rival kindreds and dangerous shore powers.

The **Folk** are the many clan peoples of the southern forests, mountain ranges, cold coasts, and Eclessian marches. They share ancestral roots, hearth-law, winter obligations, and a wary knowledge of the courts beyond ordinary life, but they are not a nation. A person is Moss-Crown, Grey-Withy, Tidewood, Gale-Crown, or Bent Oak before they are Folk.

**Fäskogarfolk** is an archaic scholarly name for the ancestor population from which the Folk and the [[01 Canon Reference/Peoples and Cultures/Ättar/Ättar Overview.md|Ättar]] developed. **Old Blood** is a proud ceremonial expression. Neither implies one government or one religion.

## What Holds Them Together

- **Hearth-law:** a received guest, a truthful name, shared winter food, and sanctuary create enforceable obligations.
- **Clan land:** territory belongs to remembered kin, the living community, and the powers bound to it. A chief cannot sell it alone.
- **Honor:** reputation is proved by keeping difficult obligations, paying feud-price, protecting dependants, and standing the winter watch.
- **The Frost-Road:** people from safer country travel to the Grobi frontier to win honor, followers, and standing through service.
- **The local courts:** seers negotiate with dangerous powers through masks, offerings, taboos, and inherited boundaries. Direct divine attention is feared.

## Political Range

Folk society ranges from a few related longhouses to territorial tribes, fortified hill confederacies, river leagues, coastal fleets, and temporary war-kings. The common offices are **Speaker**, **War-Keeper**, and **Seer**. Larger societies add judges, champion retinues, tributary kindreds, and public moots. No office can lawfully command every Folk clan.

Feuds are common but bounded by cattle raids, champions, hostages, compensation, sanctuary, and seasonal truces. A clan that burns a refuge hall, poisons a common well, or breaks winter truce places itself outside ordinary feud-law.

## Four Regional Worlds

- [[Green March Kindreds]]: northern frontier clans, Chapel-Folk, mature ironwork, fortified settlements, and constant Eclessian pressure.
- [[Rootbound Heartwood]]: deep-forest old-rite confederacies, extensive place-law, sacred groves, and the strongest Enaidfae identity.
- [[Gale-Crown Ranges]]: mountain hillforts, shepherd clans, mines, pass tolls, storm courts, and feud-heavy warrior societies.
- [[Tidewood Coast]]: fishing, timber, salt, cold-water sailing, coastal forts, sea courts, and contact with Ättar and Sestaran ships.

## Faith and Danger

Folk religion is appeasement rather than trust. The powers behind forest, storm, hunger, road, lake, and sea do not love humanity. They may keep a bargain, twist it, or become interested in the petitioner for reasons no mortal would welcome. Wise communities work through local courts and avoid meeting a great power on the road.

The courts are not a clean pantheon. They can contain Menhir courtiers, Faewild creatures, Shades, patrons, place-bound residues, and hidden Idol masks. Folk classifications describe conduct, not reliable cosmology. See [[Folk Faith and Appeasement]] and [[Courts of the Folk Lands]].

## Winter Country

Grobi migration makes winter war a shared institution even where the Grobi are distant. Frontier clans maintain stores, beacons, refuge halls, scouts, and fortified crossings. Interior and coastal warriors take the Frost-Road to join them. Longhouses, ringworks, granaries, evacuation routes, and political reputations are all shaped by the expectation that winter may arrive with an army behind it.

## The Chapel-Folk

The [[Chapel-Folk]] are both Folk and genuinely Redeemer-faithful. They preserve language, kinship, hearth-law, songs, clan offices, and non-idolatrous seasonal customs while renouncing sacrifice and worship offered to the courts. Their existence disproves the claim that conversion requires cultural erasure, which is why both hard missioners and old-rite zealots find them dangerous.
