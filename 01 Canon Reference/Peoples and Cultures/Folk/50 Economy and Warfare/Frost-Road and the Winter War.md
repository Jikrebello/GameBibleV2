# Frost-Road and the Winter War

Grobi migration is not one invasion with a beginning and end. Every hard winter pushes hungry bands, herds, war-chiefs, and Hollow-King pressure north through forest corridors and frozen passes. Folk society plans for this before planning for politics.

## Frontier Duty

Exposed clans owe scouts, beacon watches, refuge space, stored food, repaired crossings, and fighters. Neighboring clans may refuse a feud settlement and still answer a winter beacon. A chief who hoards grain while another hall holds the line loses honor beyond easy repair.

The work is not only battle. Children and elders move stores; smiths repair spearheads; healers manage frostbite; hunters track migration; speakers place refugees; and seers judge whether an unusual route reflects weather, leadership, or a disturbed power.

## Taking the Frost-Road

Young fighters from interior, mountain, and coastal clans travel to the frontier in autumn. They join hosts under a known War-Keeper, swear the winter truce, and receive food and a place at the fire. Service can earn marriage standing, compensation rights, followers, a champion-name, or eligibility for office.

The institution attracts courageous defenders, ambitious second children, exiles, debtors, killers seeking a cleaner reputation, and chiefs building private warbands. Glory is real, but so are missing hands, burned halls, hunger, and veterans who cannot sleep through winter wind.

## Conduct

Winter truce suspends many inter-clan feuds within threatened country. Loot taken from Grobi is divided among the fighter, host, bereaved households, and store fund. Orc teeth are accepted as proof of service in some regions but not as proof that every killing was lawful.

Grobi who surrender remain dangerous captives, not vermin outside moral consideration. Some clans ransom, exchange, employ, or kill them; Chapel-Folk missions sometimes attempt conversion. No policy is universal.

## Strategic Consequence

The winter war prevents easy Folk unification. Frontier hosts create temporary leaders, but those leaders depend upon distant volunteers and stores. It also prevents neighboring powers from dismissing the Folk as scattered tribes: Eclessian marches, Sestaran roads, and southern trade survive partly because Folk strongholds absorb the first pressure.
