# Folk Economy and Technology

There is no single Folk technological level. Access follows ore, charcoal, roads, political scale, trade, and local taboo.

## Regional Gradient

Remote forest communities rely heavily on stone, bone, antler, hide, bronze heirlooms, and limited bloomery iron. This does not prevent sophisticated carpentry, textiles, preservation, herbal practice, traps, boats, and landscape engineering.

River and fortified confederacies produce iron tools, mail, spearheads, swords, wagons, watermills, locks, and substantial timber-earth defenses. Gale-Crown smiths export iron; Tidewood shipwrights build cold-water vessels; Green March communities readily adopt Eclessian masonry, crossbows, ploughs, and sawmills.

## Wealth

Wealth is held in cattle, winter food, iron, boats, horses, cloth, amber, salt, timber rights, tolls, and obligations. Coin circulates around markets and borders but cannot settle every debt. A family with few coins and hundreds of dependants may be richer than a merchant whose silver buys no refuge in winter.

## Exchange

Folk exports include timber, resin, honey, wax, furs, amber, medicines, livestock, salt fish, charcoal, iron, guides, mercenaries, and route knowledge. Imports include fine steel, grain, wine, books, worked glass, crossbows, mill parts, and luxury cloth.

Written concessions are a recurring danger. Foreign contracts often treat communal seasonal rights as unused land. Speakers therefore insist upon walked boundaries, living witnesses, renewal dates, and clauses recognizing water, grove, and burial access.

## Labor and Status

The Folk have no universal serf class, but inequality is real. Cattle-poor households become clients; captives may owe years of service; powerful lineages control smithies and tolls; and seers can monopolize access to productive sacred land. Custom distinguishes a dependent who can eventually leave from a person treated as saleable property. The latter is condemned in many regions and practiced quietly in others.
