# Folk Architecture and Settlement

Folk settlements are built for cold, communal work, feud defence and the possibility of a Grobi winter. Longhouses combine dwelling, workshop, court and refuge beneath one roof, while wells, granaries and livestock yards are arranged to survive siege and fire. The form varies across the forests, ranges and coasts, but each region joins household life to prepared routes of warning and retreat.

## Longhouses

The longhouse is dwelling, workshop, court, feast hall, and emergency refuge. A typical hall stands on stone and packed-earth footings, with a low insulated roof, central hearth, smoke vents, storage lofts, and attached winter stalls. Screens and raised platforms provide household space without pretending that winter survival is private.

Larger settlements cluster halls around a common yard, well, smithy, bakehouse, and assembly fire. Chapel-Folk may add a timber chapel without moving the old meeting tree or changing the orientation of the common hearth.

## Winter Defense

Frontier ringworks combine timber palisades, earth banks, ditches, bent entrances, removable bridges, and cleared ground. Hillforts use several defensive rings and protected livestock yards. Granaries are raised, dispersed, or concealed so one fire cannot destroy the winter. Wells stand behind an inner defense; smokehouses and cache pits lie on separate circuits.

Beacon hills, refuge halls, forest sally paths, and marked evacuation roads connect neighboring settlements. Outer sheds, livestock pens, and ritual structures may be deliberately sacrificed to delay an assault while people withdraw behind the inner bank.

## Regional Forms

- Green March forts incorporate masonry, crossbow platforms, parish chapels, and reused Atherian foundations.
- Heartwood settlements are dispersed around sacred boundaries and use hidden store villages rather than one obvious center.
- Gale-Crown hillforts command passes, mines, and terraced pastures.
- Tidewood settlements face their halls away from winter storms and protect boat sheds within sea-facing earthworks.

## Ruins and Continuity

Folk communities reuse Atherian roads, watchtowers, culverts, and foundations when these do not violate older boundaries. A ruin may be a quarry, refuge, court boundary, ancestral grievance, or all four. Occupation does not imply belief that Atheria created the country.
