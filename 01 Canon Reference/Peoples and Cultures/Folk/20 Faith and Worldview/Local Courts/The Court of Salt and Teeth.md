# The Court of Salt and Teeth

## Folk Account

The **Court of Salt and Teeth** is the collective name used in Tidewood traditions for powers encountered where the winter forest meets the sea. Accounts commonly name the Net-Mother, the White Seal, the Drowned Ferryman and the Guest with Shark Teeth. Fish, wreckage, weather, passage and bodily alteration figure among their gifts and demands.

Coastal tellers often present these appearances as members or faces of a single court. Particular encounters have produced different expectations and obligations, however, and households disagree about which names describe the same being. These disagreements affect offerings, rescues and the treatment of goods recovered from wrecks.

## Signs and Boundaries

Signs associated with the court include teeth washed ashore in ordered rows, unfamiliar knots in returned nets, seals watching funerals and fresh water tasting of salt before a storm. Seers interpret them in relation to the shore and its recent encounters. Their presence can influence decisions about approaching a pool, accepting recovered goods or putting a boat to sea.

## Taboo and Offering

Coastal prohibitions include counting the drowned before landfall, denying shore-right after accepting a wreck's cargo and eating the first catch alone. Offerings include salt, carved bone, returned wreckage, a boat's name and blood mixed into the outgoing tide.

These practices concern relationships formed by receiving something from the shore. Accounts repeatedly describe a gift bringing obligations that extend beyond its immediate use, particularly when a household comes to depend on a catch or an unusual passage.

## Gifts and Failure

The court is credited with filling nets, concealing vessels, calming crossings and lending endurance against cold. Other accounts describe prosperity dependent on shipwrecks, children altered after conception during a bargain, or a borrower called back to sea.

A household's interpretation of an encounter therefore matters to subsequent conduct. Some gifts are remembered as completed exchanges; others become the beginning of repeated demands. The histories of the Three Beaches preserve several examples with different participants and consequences.

## Encounters Remembered on the Three Beaches

The **Net-Mother** is approached at a tidal pool separate from the ruined inlet beneath [[Keldscar and Gannet Head|Gannet Head]]. Fishers clear accumulated matter from a passage and return named things caught in their nets. Schools may then enter the pool, while the same changed water can leave another fishing shore empty. The exchange concerns these local movements of fish and water.

The **Guest with Shark Teeth** supplied the extraordinary catch at Gannet Head. Offerings of wreck cargo and drowned people sustained a prosperity that influential householders sought to preserve through false lights and murder. [[The Summer Without Wrecks]] records the destruction of the feeding place in 712 and the end of its catch. Bereaved families commonly distinguish this Guest from the Net-Mother encountered at the separate pool.

The **Drowned Ferryman** carried living households through a winter passage in 1002 in exchange for [[Edris Sailmaker]]'s stored sailcloth. Household accounts describe valuable material surrendered by a living craftswoman. Later tellings turn the cloth into passengers' shrouds. The loss and the neighbours' efforts to replace it are remembered through Brackenmouth's Common Loft.

The **White Seal** has a less settled place in local accounts. Seals watch the shore and sometimes follow boats, behaviour that seers interpret differently. Some sightings are treated as signs of an encounter; others are attributed to animals following fish. Even tellers who disagree about a particular sighting recognise the danger of following it beyond a familiar landing.
