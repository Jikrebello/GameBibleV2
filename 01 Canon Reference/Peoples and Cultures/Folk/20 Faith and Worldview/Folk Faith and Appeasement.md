# Folk Faith and Appeasement

The Folk do not expect the powers of the world to love them. Forest, moon, storm, hunger, river, road, and grave possess wills or attract beings whose attention can alter mortal life. Worship means maintaining a survivable relationship with that fact.

## The Proper Distance

A direct meeting with a great power is usually a catastrophe. Folk stories praise the person who recognizes the stranger on the road, remembers the correct taboo, gives no unnecessary name, and escapes without receiving a gift. A seer is valuable because a seer creates distance through masks, inherited words, bounded places, and lesser intermediaries.

The same power may save a harvest one year and destroy a family the next without hypocrisy. It is not a parent. It acts according to appetite, season, nature, story, or rules that humans only partly understand.

## Courts, Not a Pantheon

Most communities address a local court rather than a named cosmic sovereign. A court is the set of powers, creatures, residues, masks, and obligations encountered around a place or recurring condition. Its membership may change. Rivals may share one court. A being called generous in one valley may wear a predator's mask across the ridge.

Folk categories are practical:

- What signs announce it?
- What does it refuse?
- What offering ends its attention?
- What promise can it keep?
- What wording must never be used?
- Which season, threshold, or bloodline gives it standing?

These answers matter more to ordinary worship than a being's true cosmological family.

## Offerings

Common offerings include bread, milk, beer, animals, first fruit, tools, weapons, jewelry, songs, hair, blood, vows, silence, and temporary abstinence. An offering is payment, courtesy, distraction, admission, or recognition. It is not proof of affection.

Human sacrifice is lawful in several old-rite confederacies only under narrow inherited forms, usually during famine, court breach, failed kingship, or communal judgement. Other Folk consider it murder disguised as piety. Idol-influenced courts consistently push exceptional sacrifice toward habit.

## Seers

Seers remember precedents, inspect signs, negotiate boundaries, authenticate oaths, and advise when no bargain is better than a favorable one. They are not universally trusted. A corrupt seer can invent a taboo, feed a predatory court, or make personal vengeance sound like sacred necessity.

## Moral Limits

Caprice is not goodness. A bargain kept exactly may still be cruel. The Folk recognize obligations such as hospitality, kin protection, truthful naming at the hearth, and winter mercy that can override a court's demand. Heroes are often those who outwit a power without becoming like it.
