# The Age of Held Proofs

The **Age of Held Proofs** runs from the reading of the *Hundred Grievances* at a Brenholt pithead in 1297 to the present year, 1360 AR. Its name comes from a habit every great power learned in it. In a world where sainthood can be tested, kindling witnessed and contracts examined, a decisive proof can vindicate a church or destroy it, and nobody can be sure which in advance. So the proofs are held: a psalter in a lead vault, a vision no synod will authenticate, an apparition the Sanctum has not ruled on for eight years. Rulers and churches govern territory, and they also govern access to evidence.

The age opened with a drowned chaplain whose book asked to be tested by fire and would not burn, and with a nomad empire whose horses were losing their grace. It closes on an emperor who kneels in silence before a lamp and a court that watches the flame instead of asking him what it means.

## How proof became dangerous

The lesson was a century in the learning.
- **The Refusal of the Lamp (1227).** When Nikandros I of Thalmyria asked the Sanctum for soldiers, the price was that the Great Lamp of Veridun be carried east and tried by the Sanctum's relic court. He refused: *a lamp that is tried is a lamp that is owned.* Thalmyric liturgy still has a line most worshippers take to mean *the East sold us for a doctrine.*
- **Bruckmoor (1243).** The relic courts tested the weeping Lady and destroyed her shrine, rightly; a whole region's piety went with it.
- **The Ossuary Crown (1263).** They tested the crown that spoke with the voices of the plague dead and ruled that *the dead do not testify through things*, and the people who had heard their mothers in it never forgave them.
- **Aldersbruck (1299).** The last note in a drowned chaplain's psalter challenged the Sanctum to prove him wrong with Fire. A synod put the psalter to the brazier and it did not burn. The legate voided the test, sealed the book in lead and charged the Weiss country a second fee for its funerals.
- **Sarnwell (1311).** Eleven image-breakers swore on the Weiss that they had seen the Mother fill a dry well, a relic court believed its enemies, and the spring has passed every test since. Sarnwell showed that a test could also win.
- **The Council of Veridance (1319–1324)** wrote the five tests down, with the reformers' own author sitting in the hall as its witness.
- **The Night Count (1342).** The commissioners of the Korrheim Accord sat up in disputed churchyards to see whether the dead walked, and assigned the parishes by the answer. A whole peace now rested on a test, and on nobody asking for it to be run again.

By 1360 the great powers treat a test the way a mason treats a keystone: they may admire it, but they do not pull it out to see what happens.

## Reform after catastrophe

The [[Freeflame Reformation]] grew out of the [[01 Canon Reference/History/70 Later History and Present Day/The Grey Sweat.md|Grey Sweat]]. After the plague the Sanctum sold sealed letters promising that Sending Houses would sing rites for named dead. Alessior II, an ascetic who believed that the Chancery's seal itself placed a soul in the Church's keeping, let the letters be sold without limit and gave them away by the hundred thousand, and his treasurer Silvio Quarne sold them far faster than they could be sung. When Alessior died in 1296, the night clerks swore they still saw him at his signing desk. Ansgar, the chaplain of the Brenholt Sending House, had counted in the margins of his psalter more than ten letters for every rite he was given time to sing, and had found the unsung dead still walking. In 1297 he drowned with forty-one miners in the Blackwater pit, and at their funeral the tally-keeper Irmel read his margins aloud to the widows in place of the rite. The upper Weiss stopped buying letters. A synod at Aldersbruck in 1299 tried the dead man's book, voided the test when it would not burn, and charged the Weiss country a second fee. In 1306 the miners swore in the river and fought out of their pits for twenty-three years, and no band broke its oath. Doctor Lucian Ferro, the synod's notary, gave the funerals their doctrine, *the Fire keeps no landlord*, in a book the Sanctum had commissioned from him as the first half of a refutation.

The old Church answered before the princes did. The Council of Veridance under Celestin IX (1319–1324) summoned Ferro to finish the refutation and kept him in the hall for five years as its witness. It abolished assurance sales, ordered the Great Arrears sung for every unsung letter, and chartered as the Order of the Lantern the night-singers of Aurelium's burned Saint-Leon quarter, whose priest, Father Ivo, had died in an Inquisitors' prison nine years earlier. The bill for the Arrears then started the war the Council had met to end. Duke Rainald of Ironvale, handed a levy for the Church's debts and a roll showing that his own father had never been sung, burned the Sending letters of his duchy in 1329, and the Ashenmen came up out of the pits to be his army. The [[The Korrheim Accord|Korrheim Accord]] of 1343, sworn on the same river as the Ashenmen's oath, gave every churchyard to the rite that had kept its dead sent and every lord the lamp of his land's yards, and left out the people who kept no yard.

## The steppe after the Black Wind

[[Qarayel and the Black Wind|Qarayel]] died in 1303, undefeated. On the night of his death his stallion carried his body out of the camp unled, the grace-mares followed it into the dark, and the herds came home eleven days later without him. No one has found where he lies. His power had rested on horses descended from a black colt that a captive Velian farrier blessed in 1249; they stood before the Covenant's bound well-wardens as the First Catch horses once had. By 1300 the grace was thinning, Iqashar's wardens held again, and the escort payments stopped. The herds had come home in four bands, to four pastures a week's ride apart. In 1304 his descendants met to divide them and found that they could only name what the horses had done, and the White, Iron, Moon and Ash Hordes took their characters from what had come to each. The Iron Horde's carefully bred lines were the only horses on the frontier that would stand beside the Covenant's own bound things, and from 1304 Iqashar hired them.

## The frontier and the adopted emperor

From about 1310 Iron-mounted companies riding with devil-mantled escorts raided Thalmyric chapel-forts on the Velian frontier by night. At **Chapel-Fort Orinel** in 1318 the seven signal braziers failed one by one during a night assault, and a provincial commander of House Aetherion named Caedros led the dawn counterattack after the last went out. Songs say the bound riders smothered the fires and a great bird of fire rose from the smoke; a Saronikan quartermaster's ledger found a generation later records the fort's oil bought from a contractor who cut it with water. Caedros's **Crimson Pilgrimage** (1319–1322) reopened roads through the Velian districts and returned stolen parish registers, and his siege of **Zehrim** (1324–1325) ended with the garrison spared and the fort's oath-ledgers burned on its altar. The Covenant's Court of Breaches ruled in 1326 that the people those ledgers had bound remained bound in the registers at Iqashar. Thalmyria calls them freed.

In 1327 the summer flux killed both sons of the old emperor Stavren the Patient within a month. In 1328 Stavren adopted the hero of Zehrim, over the objections of the Caleran houses, and died the next year. Caedros IV was crowned at Virelos. He codified frontier amnesties, strengthened provincial audits and has not drawn his sword Ashreaver since. No treaty ended the war: Thalmyric emperors will not put their names into a Covenant instrument, and the Covenant makes no peace without one. The frontier has been an armed peace ever since, raided by Tarkhan-mounted and Velian companies, while Zanthir between the two empires pays tribute to each in alternating seasons.

## The Covenant's witnesses

The Sweat had left the Covenant with a court that could not act without witnesses and a Guard that supplied them. Since 1243 the officer bench of the Ahdanite Guard has witnessed every accession at Iqashar, and since 1252, three years after a lodged prince whose register page had been lost in the plague walked free under the law and raised the Basin, the Cull of Brothers has used the knife and the blinding needle rather than the lodging of a rival's name in the Vault. Marzuq II ruled for nearly half a century with Iron Horde cavalry and Guard counsel. When he died in 1349 the Guard bench witnessed Marzuq III. Two of his brothers were killed and one was blinded. The youngest, a boy in sanctuary at the wells of Al-Qahur, was lodged instead, because Al-Qahur made its acknowledgment of the new Sultan-Caliph wait on it. He is a grown man now in the walled garden there. The page that holds his name lies in the Deep Vault with the names of the dead, in the keeping of the shrine's custodians, and the law of 1249 still says that a prince without a page is a prince at liberty.

## The sea opens

The grass-tolls of the Black Wind had pushed Sestaran trade to the sea, and the minor port of Castara on the Boiling Sea grew with it. In 1328 the Castaran salt-hauler *Naris's Hand*, under the pilot Ottavia Sele, was caught at sea on one of the rare days when the hurricane belt falls flat calm, and crossed to the Shattered Isles. She came home with green crystal, Expanse hardwood and a chart-case of old Viscaran surveys from a wreck, and with a log that disagrees with the calendar. The University of Castara rebuilt the Viscaran storm-keel from the wreck's lines, and by the early 1330s ships crossed in the hurricane season's windows. Castara joined the Sestaran Concord as its seventh seal in 1332. When its surveyors staked wells across Ash Horde pasture for an inland road in 1333, the Ash banners burned the camps, and in 1334 Castara bought the first [[Castaran Long Road|Ash Road Peace]].

## Fiorenzia's fire

Since 1296 the Medori vault had held the Chancery of Sendings' great registers, every letter sold and every name on it, taken in pledge for Alessior II's debts. In 1345 a Sermoran pamphlet printed extracts naming Fiorenzian dead whose Sendings had never been sung and called the family vault **the Bank of the Dead**. The extracts had been sold to the printer by Nicanor Vetti, a Medori factor working off a failed desk in the grey coat of the Debtors' Corps, who was found drowned in the dike he was mending the next spring.

Fiorenzians keep a wax face of each of their dead on the house wall, a custom of the plague years. That winter the faces of the dead named in the pamphlet began to sweat and to speak. Families queued to hear their mothers, and what the faces said named debts, bastards and forged wills in half the houses of the Golden Book. The friar Frate Severin preached that the faces had become doors for the Hungers, that what spoke through them was no Fiorenzian's mother, and that a door is shut by naming and singing the dead behind it, never by fire. The senate chose fire. At the Feast of Souls in 1346, when the city walks its faces through the streets, the senators ordered every speaking face surrendered and burned in the cathedral square. Severin begged them not to light it. They lit it, and witnesses still argue about what they saw standing in the **Pyre of Masks**. The crowd went from the square to the Medori palace for the names.

Lauro Medori, a mask-maker's son who had married into the bank, opened the vault to them. He had the Fiorenzian sheets of the registers carried out and read from the palace steps, and gave them to the friar. For four years Severin, who held no office, led the whole city in the **Long Vigil**, singing every name on the sheets in the churches by day and night, while the painters painted the burned faces back from memory so that each name had one. Crowds drowned mask-makers in the river as door-makers while he preached against it, and in 1348 the appraisers hanged a master painter whose panels showed faces nobody had described to him. When the last sheet was sung in 1349 the citizens made the ducal chair hereditary in the Medori line, over the senate that had burned their dead. Severin preached against the gift. On the eve of that year's Feast of Souls he went alone into the mask-makers' cellar, where the moulds of the burned faces were kept, and was found in the morning sitting upright, wearing a wax face that no family in the city would own and nobody could lift. Vetti's desk-book on how a lender should read a sovereign, printed at Sermora in 1347 as the *Mirror for Sovereigns* and banned everywhere since, is on every ruler's private shelf.

## Vessing and the Closed House

In 1352 three people stood at a grave in the sloe-field outside the churchyard wall at **Vessing**, a hill parish of the southern marches. A Virelian runaway's child had died on the road, and no yard on the Schedule would take it. The mother, the parish sexton who had refused her the churchyard and a reformed carter who had carried the body buried the child at dusk among the sloes, and all three swear that a figure of banked fire stood at the grave. Their testimonies cannot be reconciled, and each tells against the witness's own church. The sexton, a Sanctum man, says the figure told them the Fire dwells where it is welcomed, which is Veridun's teaching. The carter, a Freeflame elder's son, says it told them the Fire is carried by the hands of the Church, which is the Sanctum's. The mother, raised in Virelia's village piety, says the figure spoke no word, wept and pointed over the wall at the churchyard, and reformed preachers read that as *sing for them*. Captured papers of the Weeping Courts claim the apparition as their work. The Sanctum has not ruled.

From 1357 a hearing has examined a prison-house of the Inquisitors of Saint Malveris, where laundresses, guards, clerks, a physician and gravediggers kept evidence of torture, seizure and execution on fabricated evidence. Witnesses say Saint Malveris, or something using the name, appeared in the sealed house, named the victims and refused to give the order a sign that it alone was right. The Inquisitors have answered to the Order of the Lantern since the Council, and the Lantern's first priest died in one of their houses; the hearing could reform them or split them. See [[Saints and Sinners of the Chronicles]].

## The last years

Empress Vasilia of Vlastrava, wife of Caedros IV for forty years, died in 1357. After her fortieth-day memorial the emperor began keeping silences of a day or more before the Great Lamp of Veridun, and courtiers report that the flame stands still while he kneels. In 1358 his elder son Prince Nikandros died in a skirmish with Iron-mounted raiders in the Velian marches. Princess Alexane, Prince Sophios and the adoption of a proven commander, the road by which Caedros himself came to the throne, now divide the court. Lord Primarch Alistair III, elected in 1354, holds the Crown of Embers, the Unburnt Psalter and the Vessing file in one set of vaults. Marzuq III's sons keep rival households, and his lodged brother keeps a garden at Al-Qahur. The Moon Horde's Sorqai is old. In every capital the most dangerous object is a piece of evidence nobody wants opened.

## Stories still happening

Some of the age's tales have no ending yet. Five strangers who escaped a burning lodging-house with an old cook and her neighbour's caged birds refused to sign a patron's charter, and have not yet decided whether to trust one another ([[G-17 - The Company with No Charter|The Company with No Charter]]). In a harbour town an old naval veteran gave away his civic name to hide from a grey carriage that collects the men of a forgotten expedition, and now his wife greets him kindly as a stranger ([[G-23 - The Citizen Who Gave Away a Name|The Citizen Who Gave Away a Name]]). Two families who found two fused neck tags on one dead man are deciding whether to confess an old workhouse fraud while a broker holding the workhouse's debts threatens their children ([[G-49 - The Two Tags on One Dead Man|The Two Tags on One Dead Man]]), and in other towns brokers holding dead paper press claims on the named dead.
