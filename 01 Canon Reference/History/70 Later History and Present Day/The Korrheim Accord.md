# The Korrheim Accord

The **Korrheim Accord** is the confessional peace of 1343 AR that ended open war between the Sanctum and the recognised Freeflame governments after the [[Freeflame Reformation]]. It was drafted at Heldenheim, the council seat of the Korrheim cantons, and sworn with the signatories' hands in the River Weiss below the broken bridge at Aldersbruck, in the reach where the Blackwater drowned of 1290 went into the river. It settled the map of Eclessia's two rites by counting graveyards, and left the theology that caused the war exactly where it was. It has held for seventeen years because nobody who swore it dares break an oath made in that river.

Its governing formula was sworn in the plain speech of the river country: **every roof under its lord's lamp, and every lamp where its dead lie.** A household keeps the public rite of the lord whose land its roof stands on. The lord keeps the rite in which that land's dead are buried. No prince at Aldersbruck chose a confession: each was handed the one his churchyards had already chosen for him. Sanctum canonists, copying the Accord into their law-books in the court Latinate, kept only the first half and rendered it *whose the realm, his the rite*. Lawyers quote that form, and princes like it for what it leaves out.

## The road to Heldenheim

By 1340 Eclessia had been at war over the Reformation for thirty-four years: first the Ashenmen's war out of the mines, then the princes' war that followed Duke Rainald's burning of the Sending House letters in 1329, fought over who should sing the Church's unpaid rites for the dead and who should pay for them. Caldria and Aurelia had fought Freeflame towns and merchants; Ironvale had taken Church estates up and down the Weiss. Towns changed hands and rites with them. In 1338 a forged no-quarter order turned a prisoner exchange between Freeflame and old-Church companies into a massacre, remembered as the Night of Broken Bells.

In 1340 the Council of Heldenheim assigned each Korrheim canton's churches by the vote of its council. The ruling gave the one churchyard of Falkendorf, a reformed canton with an old-Church council, to the old rite, and ordered the graves of forty years moved outside the wall. Falkendorf rose sooner than dig up its dead, and Duke Rainald's last son was killed at the yard gate with the Ironvale pikes sent to help it. The canton companies ordered to put the rising down halted at the churchyard wall, where the women of Falkendorf were sitting on the graves. Their war-priest, Father Gregor, who had buried mixed companies for twenty years, wrote the captains a set of burial articles in place of an assault. A yard stays with the rite that has kept its dead sent. Nobody's dead are moved. Korrheim made the **Falkendorf articles** its own law that winter.

Rainald died in 1341 without an heir and left Ironvale to its guild ministries, who had inherited a war they had not chosen. In the winter of 1341 they banked Ironvale's furnaces and sold iron to neither confession until the princes would talk. Old-Church foundries in Caldria and Brauvalis had lived on Ironvale pig-iron since the Ashenmen gave up the mines, and by the spring the gun-pits stood cold on both sides of the Weiss. Ironvale's own smiths went hungry for it, and the Ministry kept the furnaces banked through the **Iron Fast** anyway.

## The Night Count

The negotiators at Heldenheim could agree that the war should stop and could not agree whose land was whose. Fourteen years of sieges had left towns with two sets of clergy, two registers and a duke who had held them for a season. The cantons offered their own rule, and through the summer and autumn of 1342 the delegates sent out the commissioners of the count: for each disputed parish, a clerk of each rite and a canton notary. They read the burial register and asked who had been sending the yard's dead. Where the books were burned or doubled, they sat up in the churchyard through the night. A yard that lay quiet went to the rite that had been burying in it. A yard that walked was opened to both choirs, and went to whichever sang it still.

The result was the **Schedule of Yards**, the list of every parish burial ground in the signatory lands and the rite that keeps it. It runs to some nine thousand entries. Most took an afternoon. A few hundred took a night, and both sides still tell stories about them: a Brauvalis yard won by three old women with one hymn, a Caldrian yard that quieted only when the two choirs sang together and has been shared ever since. A few dozen places would not lie still for anyone. Those were written down as common ground, and the mixed watches posted on them were the first Accord Wardens.

## The oath in the river

Everyone was tired and nobody trusted anyone's oath. The delegates needed a guarantee stronger than relics, which each side accused the other of faking, and stronger than seals, which the assurance trade had taught Eclessia to despise. They found it in the Weiss. Since the Risings, oaths sworn on the Preacher's Water had held with a consistency nobody could explain. The Sanctum's relic court tested the water in 1342; the result was declared inconclusive and the file sealed, and the old-Church delegates took that as reason enough to fear it. In the spring of 1343 they waded in below the broken bridge beside reformed princes, canton councillors and the Grand Ministers of Ironvale, with the Schedule lying on the bank, and swore. That summer Ironvale lit its furnaces again.

## What it settled

- Old-Church and recognised reformed governments may coexist within Eclessia.
- Every parish yard keeps the rite that was sending its dead when the Schedule was made.
- The lord of the land keeps the lamp of his land's yards, sets its public rite and confirms its clergy. Church property follows the yard.
- Armed conversion across a signatory border is a breach of the peace.
- Any household may carry its dead across a border to a yard of its own rite, and displaced clergy and households have limited passage.
- A yard whose dead go unsent for a year and a day is open to whichever rite will send them.

The "lord" turned out to be a broad word. Ironvale's Grand Ministry signed as keeper of a realm without a prince, and the Korrheim cantons signed one by one, so the formula covers ministries, cantons and chapters as well as dukes.

## What it refused to settle

- Individual freedom of conscience.
- Mixed marriages and children raised across two rites, and where their dead may lie.
- Congregations that keep no yard.
- The status of itinerant preachers, exiles and clandestine parishes.
- Eclessia's reunification mandate towards Thalmyria.
- Whose dead a lord stands under when he inherits land of another lamp: his land's or his house's.

## The Unsworn

The oldest congregations of the Reformation were left out because they had nothing to count. The river people of the upper Weiss, who followed the tally-keeper Irmel and the first Brenholt funeral, give their dead to the Preacher's Water and keep no churchyard. The commissioners found no yard to sit in and wrote no entry, and when the princes waded into the river nobody put a hand in for a people without graves. They are called the Unsworn, and the Accord protects none of them. Other congregations without a yard or a lord have been counted with them since: icon-breakers, commonwealth preachers, and bands who bury where they camp. Princes of both confessions hunt their preachers, and both churches cite them as proof of what the other side becomes. Irmel's answer to the Count is still repeated among them: *the dead are named, not counted.*

## The silence on reunion

The Accord never mentions the reunification mandate, the Sanctum's standing duty to restore one Church and one imperial crown with Thalmyria. Sanctum legates at Heldenheim would not put the mandate into an oath sworn on water that holds oaths. Some feared being bound to a reunion no one could achieve; others feared being bound to give it up. The silence was deliberate, and Veridun's chroniclers have enjoyed it ever since.

## Present-day effects

The war moved to the graveside. Since a yard that goes unsent for a year and a day is forfeit, both churches race to every deathbed on the seam, and a village haunting is a legal event with a deadline. Lords who want a parish changed starve its clergy out and wait for a burial to go unsung. Hauntings are staged to open a yard and concealed to keep one, and Wardens and Lantern investigators are called to tell a walking churchyard from a man in a sheet. Carrying parties cross the borders with their dead on handcarts, and the Wardens escort them.

Every succession is a possible religious crisis, because the Accord's second line can be read two ways. When a lord of one rite inherits land of the other, his lawyers say the lamp follows the dead of his house, and his new parishes say it follows the dead in their yards. Virelia is the hardest case. Its magnates are patrons of their estate yards, so their serfs follow their lords' rite; and its elected crown has no royal graves at all, since each king is buried among his own family, so every royal death reopens the question of whose lamp the crown lands stand under. Border towns keep parallel registers and feast calendars. Nobles use the settlement to acquire land, while sincere clergy use its protections to keep another generation from war. Every few years someone proposes amending the Accord, and nobody will go back into the river to do it.

Grenzburg remains formally within the old-Church settlement, but frontier villages, migrant labourers, Folk converts and Freeflame influences make actual practice less tidy than the ducal register suggests.
