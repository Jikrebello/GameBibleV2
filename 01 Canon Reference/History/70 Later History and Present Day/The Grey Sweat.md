# The Grey Sweat

The **Grey Sweat** was the great mortality of 1232 to 1247 AR, a sweating fever that came in waves for fifteen years and killed about a third of eastern Eclessia and a fifth of its western territories, with heavy losses in the Covenant's Basin, the Sestaran cities and Thalmyria. Eclessians remember it as *the years the ferry queued*: the dead came faster than priests could bless them, rites failed, and in many parishes the dead were seen walking. The Sweat ended suddenly in 1247, in the week remembered as the Quiet Ninth.

It changed the world more through burial than through medicine. Who could send the dead, and what a paid promise to send them was worth, became the question behind Virelia's bound estates, the Sending Houses, the assurance trade and, fifty years later, the [[Freeflame Reformation]].

## The coming of the fever

The Sweat began in eastern Eclessia in the summer of 1232. No surviving authority agrees where it came from. It started with a chill and a grey, sour-smelling sweat that soaked bedding through; most who died did so within two days, many within one. Bodies decomposed quickly in the heat, and gravediggers were among the first to die.

The Church's quarantine doctrine judged a village by its rites. A sealed village with a living priest was clean; a healthy village whose priest had died was not, because its dead could no longer be sent. Soldiers sealed roads accordingly, and suspected carriers were executed at the barriers. The forty licensed funeral ferries on the river crossings were closed, and bodies were burned on the near banks without blessing. [[G-16 - The Forty-First Ferry|The Forty-First Ferry]] remembers a widow, a thief and a physician who ran an illegal night ferry for the dead the licensed boats refused.

## The dead who were not sent

Where priests died and the dead lay unburied or were burned unblessed, people reported the dead returning: a mother at the mill-race, a husband at the ford, whole households at a church door. Parish books from the Sweat years record thin places opening in villages and closing again, and the scale of the hauntings frightened the churches more than the mortality did.

The **Ash-Walkers** were the plague's penitents, processions that went from town to town scourging themselves, smearing ash on their faces and speaking aloud the names of the dead in every parish they entered. Several duchies' records note that hauntings ceased where they passed. When the Sweat ended, their descendants split. Some sought silence and exhaustion in teachings that later fed [[The Apathetics]]. Others went looking for apparitions and relics that would promise them their dead were safe, and the [[The Weeping Courts|Weeping Courts]] found them.

The worst of these was **Bruckmoor**. For forty years a weeping Lady had drawn pilgrims there, and in the Sweat's last decade whole parishes walked to her with offerings and the names of their dead. In 1243 the Inquisition of Saint Malveris proved the shrine an Idol-feeding operation that had eaten that grief, and the site was burned and salted. Bruckmoor is the founding trauma of the modern relic courts; its fuller story is told in [[The Hunger Wars]].

## Beyond Eclessia

The fever followed the trade roads and sea lanes.
- **Thalmyria.** It reached the ports by Sestaran shipping around 1234. Thalmyric parish priests are married men who live among their flocks, and many died with them, but where no priest survived, villagers sang the funeral hymns over their own dead. Thalmyria came out of the Sweat with fewer haunted villages than Eclessia and a hardened conviction that the Flame answers the singer, not the office. Eclessian relic courts call the Thalmyric records unverifiable.
- **The Covenant.** The fever reached Covenant lands about 1236 by the Velian fairs, the Zanthiri ports and Mazrakan. It fell lightly on the dry country and heavily on the Basin and the Iqashar barracks. Covenant chroniclers call the Sweat **the Emptying of the Benches**. In Covenant law the death of every bound person needs a witnessed release in the Living Register, and the witnesses on the court benches died faster than the debtors. Unreleased names piled up, heirs inherited the debts of the dead, and frontier garrisons whose oaths had lost their witnesses marched home. Thalmyria's Nikandros I walked into forts on the Qarath frontier whose gates stood open. Suhrab's last book of the *Ledger of Ledgers* (1243) let a sealed register stand as witness where none survived.
- **Sestara.** In Fiorenzia the plague emptied a third of the patrician houses entered in the Golden Book, and in 1238 the senate sold seats to keep a quorum. The Medori bought one.
- **The steppe.** The scattered Tarkhan camps suffered least. Wind-speakers say the steppe's dead go with the wind.

## The Quiet Ninth

The Sweat did not fade. In 1247 it stopped everywhere in the same week, with no final wave: the last recorded case in every surviving register falls within nine days of the Feast of Souls. In that week the Ash-Walker processions disbanded in the middle of their marches, everywhere at once and without instruction. Asked why, the penitents gave versions of the same answer: *it was finished being needed.* The Church has claimed the penance was accepted, and has never repeated the claim in formal doctrine. Covenant registers record the date and give no cause. Every power in the world would like the credit, and none has dared take it.

## Land, labour and the bond of the clean village

The survivors inherited empty land and a shortage of hands. Across western Eclessia, tenants demanded wages, fixed rents, inheritance rights and the freedom to leave, and lords who needed both workers and a priest for their villages gave way. Towns grew, guilds strengthened, and abandoned holdings were consolidated or let on easy terms.

In Virelia the sabre-right nobility held the patronage of most parishes, and they used the quarantine rule. A village with no living priest was unclean and could be sealed. Its survivors were moved onto the demesne estates under bond, in exchange for a priest, a consecrated cemetery and burial in rite. Virelians call this the **bond of the clean village**, and it hardened into the second serfdom that made Virelia a grain exporter. Its runaways have been walking towards river towns, the southern marches and Grenzburg's seasonal camps ever since, and many go where Freeflame elders will bury anyone.

The plague-gap registers never closed. Titles, marriages, inheritances and clerical appointments from the missing years are still disputed in 1360.

## The Twin Cardinals

The Sweat reached the Sanctum's own conclave. In 1240 the fourteen cardinals who had lived to vote elected **Aimery of Corvel**, who sickened that evening and was dead before dawn, before he had sung a single rite. Under the quarantine rule a Sanctum whose head had died unsent was unclean, and the electors would have been sealed in with the fever. His twin brother **Aubert**, also a cardinal, buried him in the conclave garden without a rite, put on his ring and came out to the loggia in his place. For twenty months Aubert reigned as his brother. His one great act, the **Ferry Rule** of 1241, allowed any baptised person who knew the names of the dead to sing the funeral where no priest lived, and thousands of parishes buried their dead by it.

In the spring of 1242 the sacristans began to see a cardinal in red at the garden wall at night, with the Primarch's face, while the Primarch sat at supper. One of them said the name *Aimery* aloud and sang, and the figure went. The garden was dug and Aubert confessed. The Church split at once over what he had done. One college held that his bishops, his cardinals and his Ferry Rule stood; the other held that everything he had sealed was void; and parishes that had buried their dead under the Ferry Rule did not know whether their dead had been sent. This was the **Schism of the Twin Cardinals**. It ended late in 1242, when both colleges, sitting together, elected **Lord Primarch Gelasin II**. Gelasin confirmed the Ferry Rule for the plague years, forbade it afterwards, kept Aubert's bishops and refused to rule on Aubert, who died in a Brauvalis abbey in 1251. Aurelian canonists call him the Usurper of the Ring and keep his name off the roll of Primarchs. Brauvalis abbeys, and later the Freeflame, call him the Ferryman Primarch. The doubt he left never quite closed, and it sent many families to buy letters for dead who had already been sung.

## The Ossuary Crown

In 1251 salvagers who would not say where they had been brought a crown of fused bone, wire and white orchard wood to an abbey fair in Brauvalis. It spoke in thousands of voices, naming the plague dead and their last wishes. Families paid to hear their mothers. Claimants carried it into the plague-gap title suits, where its voices testified to wills no notary had drawn up and to debts nobody living remembered. A widow learned that her husband had kept a second household; an abbey learned that its dead abbot had promised a mill to a village.

The Sanctum seized the crown in 1254. A Brauvalis abbey, a Virelian magnate and a voice claiming to be the crown's lawful keeper all sued for it. In 1263 the relic court gave the **Ruling of the Hollow Mouth**: *the dead do not testify through things.* Few were comforted, because the voices knew things only the dead could have known. In 1360 the crown sits in a Sanctum reliquary under three unresolved claims, and scholars of the Egg White argue about what was speaking. If the dead could not speak for themselves, someone had to speak for them, and the ruling sent families in their thousands to the Sending Houses.

## The Sending

After the Sweat, the Sanctum answered the fear of the unsent dead and the doubt the Twin Cardinals had left. In 1252 Gelasin II reorganised the old assurance letters into the **Chancery of Sendings**. Chapters called Sending Houses were endowed to sing named rites for named souls, including, for families who wanted certainty, every rite once sung under the Ferry Rule; families bought sealed letters promising those rites; and the Medori counting-house carried the payments across a dozen jurisdictions. Where the rites were sung, parish records report that the dead stopped walking. The trade spread because it worked there.

### Gerlind of Lindenfurt

**Saint Gerlind** (c. 1228–1279) is the plague's great saint. She was a miller's daughter of Lindenfurt, a ford-and-mill town on a tributary of the upper Weiss. In 1236 the Sweat killed the parish priest, her mother and her two brothers; the ferries were closed and the bodies burned on the bank. Her mother was seen at the mill-race for nine nights. On the tenth, eight years old, Gerlind gathered the neighbours, had each of them say aloud the names of their dead, and sang the one funeral hymn she knew. Her mother was not seen again.

She worked the ford as a ferrywoman, and from about 1248 she walked to parishes whose priests had died and whose dead still walked. Her rule was short: every dead person named aloud by the people who knew them, then the hymn. She took bread and refused coin. Her one remembered saying is *the dead are named, not bought.* She crossed into Virelia's sealed villages and was flogged at a magnate's gate in 1257 for burying bond-folk without his leave.

The Ferry Rule had ended with the plague years, and she sang under no licence. In 1266 a Sanctum relic court tried her for unlicensed rites and for undercutting the Sending Houses. The court's own observers swore they had watched a thin place close while she sang. She was acquitted on condition that she sing only with a priest present, and she obeyed by bringing whatever priest she could find. Several Sending House chaplains sang with her for nothing and lost their places for it. She died of a winter fever at Lindenfurt in 1279 and was buried beside the mill-race.

Her death made her valuable. Lord Primarch Alessior II, who had sung villages quiet himself as a young bishop, canonised her in 1284 with unusual speed, and the Chancery of Sendings printed her face on its letters; the Gerlind seal became the best-selling letter of the assurance trade. Her grave was enclosed in a Sending House chapel that charged pilgrims, and her bones were carried to the Sanctum in 1290. In 1285 her followers in Aurelium's Saint-Leon quarter, who sang the dead out for nothing, were burned from their homes after an apparition of Gerlind herself was seen condemning them; Lantern investigators later proved it staged. The Sanctum honours her as the obedient saint who sang with a priest present. The Freeflame quote her saying against every sold Sending. Lindenfurt people still go to the mill-race to say names.

## Legacy

Eclessian families still speak of events as before or after the Sweat. Wage labour in the west, bound estates in Virelia, the relic courts' grilles and seals, the plague-gap title suits, and runaways moving south all descend from it. So does the assurance trade that the Sweat first discredited and then made indispensable. The failure of paid promises during the dying, and the ones that were sold after it and never sung, gave Ansgar of Brenholt his audience half a century later.
