# High Noon and the Qarath Disaster

High Noon and the Qarath Disaster runs from 1104, when the Crown of Embers went into the Sanctum's vault at Aurelium, to roughly 1290. For most of its first century Thalmyria looked stronger than at any time since the Fall: its emptied frontier resettled by soldier-farmers, a war against the Covenant won, a full treasury and an emperor called the Wall of the World. The strength broke in a drought summer at Qarath's Well in 1214, where an army that drank from a sworn well was routed and its emperor taken, and again in the Crown Hall at Veridun, where his own court blinded him. Civil war followed until 1225, and the Grey Sweat of 1232–1247 struck a world already wounded. What survived handed its unpaid debts forward into [[The Age of Held Proofs]].

The age's wonders were as public as its wars. Farmers' lamps kindled on the walls of frontier towns; a widow's lamp burned at an emperor's funeral while his heirs' stayed dark; a raptor of white fire hung over a census road; escaped slaves crossed a broken passage under the Korrheim mountains; and Sermora saw the man it had hanged standing above its harbour wall.

## After the ash

The Two Winters of 1101–1103, two winters with no summer between under ash-grey skies, ended in the finest harvest in a century, which farmers called the Apology. They left the map rearranged. In the east, the famine had decided which lordships submitted to which, made Virelia's crown elective and put the Crown of Embers into the Sanctum's custody ([[The Great Split — Two Crowns, One Fire]]). On Thalmyria's northern frontier whole valleys had emptied. Settlers had died or gone north for bread, and so had many of the Southern Houses, the families descended from the occupation who held frontier land under Covenant deeds from the surrender terms of 957–1052. Terraces went to thorn and the aqueduct branches silted. A generation of large families followed the Apology, and by 1118 the empire had more young men than land to settle them on.

## The Wall of the World, 1118–1167

**Caedros III** was crowned at Virelos in 1118, a descendant of Theodosia raised in the frontier monasteries. He resettled the empty frontier as **military districts**. Each family received land on condition of service: in war it owed a lance, and every year it owed a lamp lit from the Rekindling relay and hung by the door. District towns grew around their granaries and chapels, and their men drilled on the threshing floors.

Some of that land was land the Southern Houses still held by deed. In 1126 the Covenant's Court of Breaches at Iqashar ruled the old surrender instruments breached, and in 1131 the Covenant came to enforce the ruling as it had enforced its claims in the north centuries before. It sent no ordinary army. Its great **mercy towers**, engines that walked before the columns proclaiming terms in the local tongue and predicting where people would flee, offered the district families their property in exchange for surrender. Two of them came south. The district families held their land only so long as they defended it, so surrender meant losing it under their own law, and they would not bargain. The towers kept predicting a breaking point that never came and turned to terror: marked stones left on threshing floors, false orders called through the dusk in familiar voices, mills bombarded at harvest. District women sang the towers' proclamations back at them from the terraces. The terror hardened the districts.

And the district lamps kindled. Thalmyric chroniclers of the **War of the Southern Deeds** (1131–1139) record farmhouse lamps taking the Flame as their families held doorways and granary lofts, and Covenant records describe fire that came off the walls of villages where no warlock was present. At **Kolonna** in 1134 Caedros himself dismounted to carry a district family's children out of a burning granary, and the lamp at his saddle took the Flame before the whole town. Eclessian examiners who came to see the district lamps afterwards found them unremarkable, since they burned only in defence. The Covenant lost a tower, lost its commanding prince to dysentery in the siege camps in 1135, and lost a flank in 1137 when two of its jurists read one order two ways under their provincial codes. In 1139 it ransomed custodians whom Thalmyria had captured with registers holding the names of the Covenant's living soldiers, and the ransom refilled the Thalmyric treasury.

What followed was High Noon. Roads were relaid and aqueduct branches reopened. The district towns filled; monasteries rebuilt in stone; Aversian ore came down the fortress roads and Sestaran credit came up the rivers. Caedros never married. He lived as a soldier-ascetic in a plain room at Veridun, and after Kolonna he believed the Fire watched the throne.

## The bill under the mountains

Prosperity had victims close at hand.

**The Three Knocks.** In one high Aversian mine, miners had long kept a compact with the small grey people seen at the edge of the lamplight: three knocks from the dark meant a load-bearing seam was moving; miners left one share of ore and never cut the pale rib that crossed the deepest shaft. The ore of High Noon's arms and ploughs came out of mountains like that one, and Shield-House officials turned the custom into production law. They stopped leaving the share, cut the pale rib to make quota, declared dead workers "court-defaulters" whose families owed the mine replacement labour, blamed missed quotas on "the Knockers" and sent strikers and prisoners into the worst workings as payment. To end the stoppages an overseer arranged a false middle knock meaning the passage was safe, and paid an interpreter to confirm it. When the crews went down, the weight the rib had carried came down on them, and gas filled the refuge chambers the overseer had ordered sealed against strikers. Prisoners, guards and free miners died together. When the surviving interpreters accused the overseer, he executed them for breaking the bargain. Miners never answer the middle of three knocks. [[G-68 - The Three Knocks under the Mountain|The Three Knocks under the Mountain]]

**The Saint of Ashen Wings.** In the silver mines of the Heldenberg, worked by convicts, Two Winters bread-rioters and the children of Folk captives, a slave courier led a revolt through the prison tunnels around 1116 and freed several valleys. When the counts' army came back up the valley in the winter of 1123, he sent condemned overseers through a broken passage in the mountain called the Cold Way. Those who came back had been changed by it, and dangerous, but through them the rebels learned the passage, came out behind the counts' army in the snow and broke it. In 1124 the free valleys swore the **Oath of the Shafts**, the first of Korrheim's cantons. The liberator killed some of the changed survivors with his own hand and hid what the passage had done. Around 1137 he confessed before the canton assembly, laid down his office and went into exile; he spent his last years pulling people out of similar experiments and died in 1146 closing a breach. His cult calls him the Saint of Ashen Wings. Korrheim eats white bread at its liberation feast, and the next morning shares black bread made with the feast's ashes while the experimental victims are named. [[G-37 - The Feast Followed by Ashen Names|The Feast Followed by Ashen Names]]

**The Saint above the Harbour Wall.** In the second of the Two Winters, famine ships from the Eclessian coast came to Sermora and the city closed its harbour chain. A foreign-born chain-tender lowered it. Some ships forced the entry; harbour workers and refugees died in the crush, and Sermora hanged the chain-tender as a traitor. In 1166 a rival's fleet and hired Ättar keels besieged the city with a leased storm-engine. Defenders and the attackers' own chronicles describe a terrifying figure above the harbour wall; the engine broke and the siege failed. Afterwards Sermoran soldiers murdered foreign residents blamed for the breach, and the apparition's rebuke is remembered alongside the rescue. At the city's feast the harbour chain is lowered for one foreign or refugee vessel before it is raised for the procession, and older songs still call the chain-tender a traitor.

**The Great Southern Purge.** In the south of Eclessia, marcher lords and the Knights of Saint Aveline spent 1158–1163 destroying Folk seers and shrine settlements under a relic-court ruling that the shrines held unexamined fire. They cut the boundary groves. In the midwinter of 1163, riders whose breath made no smoke went through the marcher camps on three nights, taking an Aveline chapter and the Folk households nearest the cut groves alike, and the Purge ended. ## The Unanswered Funeral, 1167

Caedros III died childless in 1167 and left his succession to the Fire: at his bier in Veridun's cathedral, beneath the Great Lamp, the claimant whose lamp kindled would rule. The Synod objected and was overruled by a dead man.

Seven claimants, nephews and generals, stood at the bier holding lamps. None kindled. One flared, and an old warlock in the choir smelled script-fire under the wick; "Shell-fire smells of hot coin," he said, and the saying stuck. The other six had the counterfeiter blinded on the cathedral steps, the first imperial blinding in Thalmyric memory. In the nave, among the district honour guard, **Despina of Kolonna**, a soldier's widow who had walked to Veridun to pray for her three sons on the frontier, was holding a lamp of her own. It took the Flame. The court suppressed the report. The district soldiers carried it home.

In the **War of the Seven Lamps** (1167–1169) the district armies decided the succession and acclaimed their own commander, **Andrion**, whom the court called the Widow's Emperor and who took the name as a badge. Despina went home to Kolonna and refused every summons to court. The Widow's Lamp is kept in Kolonna's church, and district families still carry their door-lamps to it on the anniversary.

## The Threshing Floor, 1171–1173

Andrion (1169–1198) was the districts' emperor. He extended the district levy and had district households counted for it. In Elenara, the river province of vineyards and monasteries, the prince saw in those years a chance at a royal crown of his own and ordered a census of his own. It was announced as grain relief before a drought, and the count did move grain; its real purpose was to seize dependants for road labour, list refugees for taxation and choose hostages from doubtful households. He was warned that it went beyond the law and went ahead, because a complete register would prove him a king. Clerks and soldiers broke down doors, dragged families to the counting stations and killed those who resisted. Mothers hid children under grain, and the tally-marks led the patrols to every barn.

A mortality followed along the census roads. Count-houses and barracks fell sick within hours of each sealed schedule's arrival: fever, then failing breath. Clerks, soldiers, nobles, labourers and children died. Witnesses saw a dark, immense raptor outline filled with white fire above the leading edge of the road. The prince first blamed foreigners. Then he confessed what the census had been for, released the hostages, burned the schedule and came barefoot to the threshing floor of a household at **Kremna** that his census had already bereaved. The mortality stopped there.

Court histories call it plague. Devotional Lives name a commissioned angel. Harvest songs say the soldiers finally refused their orders after seeing their own children sick. Elenara stayed a duchy, and is one in 1360. Thalmyric and later Eclessian law codes require a census to state its purpose and forbid its use for seizure without a new public act; jurists call the limit "the floor beyond the count". After communal threshing in the river country, one circle of grain is left uncounted and handed out by hand. [[G-48 - The Threshing Floor beyond the Count|The Threshing Floor beyond the Count]]

## The east under an empty crown

In Eclessia the Crown of Embers stayed in its vault. Kings who petitioned the Sanctum to bring it out received one answer: reunion first, which in practice meant Veridun's submission to examination. The last king of Aldwin's line died in 1166, and the Primarchs took the Aurelian estates into the same trust as the crown and ruled Aurelia directly.

Around 1180, after a local famine, children across Brauvalis, Caldria and Veridancia left their homes on one autumn morning and walked west toward Caleran, calm, carrying bread. All were turned back at the watershed, by a wall of birds or a preaching stranger or nothing they could name, the accounts disagree. All came home except forty, and of the forty there are no baptismal records, no household rolls and no graves, while their parents remained certain to their deaths that someone was missing. The Church ruled the **Walking of the Small** a fever-dream of grief after the famine. The ruling has never been read aloud without someone objecting. Around 1203 a weeping Lady began to be reported at Bruckmoor, and the parents of the Walking were among her first pilgrims.

In 1183 the Sanctum defined the **Unkindled Conception**, teaching that the Mother was conceived "without ash", a vessel made pure by grace before she bore the Fire; in 1204 it defined her bodily **Rising**. Eclessian preachers tied the first definition to the grief of the Walking. Veridun rejected the Sanctum's authority to define either doctrine, and Thalmyric polemicists said the Sanctum had defined a doctrine to change the subject.

## The standard and the keys

The company remembered through the **Ransomed Standard** formed in the 1180s and 1190s to recover bodies and pledged prisoners from ground neither army wanted to enter, and served the High-Noon frontier until its fortunes broke with Qarath. It contained professionals, exiles and hostages. Failed ransom meant mutilation and execution. Reprisal killed noncombatants, and members of the company took part in the murder of surrendered rivals. Later accounts disagree about who commanded. Its later bearer kept the torn hostage ties hanging where a victory streamer should be.

Around 1201 a Velian amir's coalition besieged a district town while its council secretly hid stores and arranged its own escape. The **Woman Who Returned the Keys**, a widow who kept the town's warehouse inventories, entered the besieging camp by deception, killed and beheaded its commander herself and returned with the head and with proof of the coalition's diverted pay, which broke it apart. She later surrendered the town's keys rather than turn a rescue into a hereditary crown.

## Qarath's Well, 1214

**Suhrab the Lawbinder**'s reign, 1190–1243, brought the Covenant to its apex. Raised as a jurist after renouncing the throne, he took it when a court ruled his renunciation void, and spent his reign harmonising every provincial code into the *Ledger of Ledgers*. In 1212 his Court of Breaches revived the ruling of 1126 with a new offer to the district families on the old Southern House lands: confirmation of their farms under Covenant deed, with no levy owed. After two generations of Andrion's levies, some families accepted and registered their deeds at the Court's frontier bench beside **Qarath's Well** in the salt country.

**Markellos**, Andrion's grandson, had come to the throne in 1209 at twenty-three, a soldier raised in the districts. In the drought summer of 1214 he marched to Qarath's Well to stop his own people accepting. The outer wells along the road had been fouled with carcasses by Tarkhan companies in Covenant pay, and by the time the army reached Qarath its water carts were dry. The well was a sworn well, with a bound warden in its depths and a well-peace cut into its curb in three scripts, Atherian among them: whoever drank as a guest was under the well's peace until the next sunset and might not lift a weapon against the host. The officers who could read the Atherian took it for a dedication. The army drank at dusk.

At dawn, when the drinkers took up their spears against the Covenant line, the water in them turned. Men vomited water; their arms hung like wet rope; some drowned standing in the dust. The Kedravan monastic contingents had refused to drink, because the refuge monasteries' rule since the occupation forbade drinking sworn water, and their commander, **Nikandros**, of a cadet Aetherion branch in Kedrava, formed them up and withdrew in order with every wounded man they could carry. The Tarkhan companies, on horses descended from the fallen empire's own studs that do not shy from bound things, rode through what was left. Markellos stayed with the men who had drunk and was taken at sunset on the second day.

Suhrab received his captive with elaborate contract-courtesy and ransomed him for the military districts settled on the Southern House lands, enforcing the ruling of 1126 eighty-eight years late. The Ransomed Standard recovered the dead from the salt flats under a truce. Thalmyric preachers still argue whether the Fire left the army that drank or stayed with the emperor who stayed; Covenant jurists say the terms were posted in the drinkers' own tongue.

## The wound made at home, 1214–1225

Markellos came home without his districts. His uncle and regent **Sergian** had him blinded in the Crown Hall at Veridun, beneath the Great Lamp and the Virelian Lamp. Sergian's jurists argued that an emperor made unfit for office voided every instrument he had sworn, a doctrine the Covenant had once used itself when a sultan-caliph's oaths died with him. Within the season the Covenant's Court of Breaches answered that the ransom had been sworn to the Throne, which does not die, and cited the deposit by which the Covenant had once kept the Eternal Ember for Veridun to redeem. Covenant garrisons occupied the districts in 1215 regardless. Markellos died of the wound-fever within the month. The blinding bought nothing but shame.

Civil war ran until 1225. Sergian's regency, the dispossessed district lords, the Kedravan monasteries behind Nikandros, the Saronikan admiralty and the duke of Elenara fought over a throne with an empty treasury. In 1217 a Veridun crowd killed Sergian in the same hall. That year Caldrian marcher captains crossed the watershed and seized three rite-changing valleys while Thalmyria could not defend them.

Two stories from the civil wars are told everywhere. In Vlastrava, around 1220, a mediator arranged a surrender that saved King Stanimir's household. Once they were safe, the mediator offered him his enemy's defenceless children and urged him to avenge a murdered kinsman. Stanimir had promised protection freely, and refused; his own brother called the refusal disloyal and killed the mediator, and the children were returned under guard. Vlastravan Lives call the mediator a devil's agent, and Vlastrava's word became the one men trusted. In a lesser city around the same time, a poor wall-worker saw that an emergency pumping order would burst the cisterns under a crowded district, and stopped it with a vent sequence nobody had taught her; the officials took the credit and withheld her wages, and engineers still cut her notch into the stone before a pressure test.

**Nikandros I**, 1225–1263, was crowned at Virelos with the monasteries and Vlastrava behind him. He had brought half an army home from Qarath and was not known to kill surrendered households; later tellers made him merciful in everything, which he was not. He stabilized the remainder and asked the west for soldiers. In 1227 the Sanctum named its price: the Great Lamp of Veridun, tried by the Sanctum's relic court. Nikandros refused, saying that "a lamp that is tried is a lamp that is owned", and fought on alone. A decade later the plague did what no ally would. Covenant garrisons whose oaths had lost their witnesses marched home between 1238 and 1246, and Nikandros walked through their open gates and won back about half the lost districts. His restoration created the line later claimed by Caedros IV, but it could not restore all territory or heal every provincial loss. Victorious chronicles and roadside graves tell very different versions of this recovery.

## The Sweat and the Black Wind

The Grey Sweat came in waves from 1232 to 1247 and killed faster than priests could bury. [[The Grey Sweat]] tells how the dead went unsent and walked, how laity sang their own dead out in Thalmyria, how the Covenant's court benches emptied and how the fever stopped everywhere in the same week. [[G-16 - The Forty-First Ferry|The Forty-First Ferry]] remembers the illegal funeral crews who worked where official provision failed. On the steppe a captive farrier's blessing on a dying black colt renewed the old horse-grace, and from 1261 Qarayel's Black Wind burned fairs and made every settled power pay for the road ([[Qarayel and the Black Wind]]). The grass-toll pushed Sestaran trade onto the sea, and the Medori grew rich on the estates and Sendings of the plague dead ([[Historical Spine of the Sestaran Concord]]).

The era left altered fields, mountain wounds, abandoned wards, rival feasts and the unpaid costs of victory. It led into [[The Age of Held Proofs]].
