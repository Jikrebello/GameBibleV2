# High Noon and the Qarath Disaster

High Noon and the Qarath Disaster runs from 1104, when the Crown of Embers went into the Sanctum's vault at Aurelium, to roughly 1290. For most of its first century Thalmyria looked stronger than at any time since the Fall: its emptied frontier resettled by soldier-farmers, a war against the Covenant won, roads and aqueducts rebuilt on the enemy's ransom money, and an emperor called the Wall of the World. The strength broke in a drought summer at Qarath's Well in 1214, where an army that drank from a sworn well was routed and the well itself named what the drinkers owed, and again at Veridun, where the court took the farms of every man who had drunk under the seal of an emperor too sick to hold a pen. Civil war followed until 1225, and the Grey Sweat of 1232–1247 struck a world already wounded. What survived handed its unpaid debts forward into [[The Age of Held Proofs]].

The age's wonders were as public as its wars. Farmers' lamps kindled on the walls of frontier towns; a widow's lamp burned at an emperor's funeral while his son's flared with bought fire; an army sickened on a well whose terms nobody but one monk would read, and new letters stood wet on its curb at sunset; a raptor of white fire hung over a census road; escaped slaves crossed a broken passage under the Korrheim mountains; and Sermora saw the man it had hanged standing above its harbour wall.

## After the ash

The Two Winters of 1101–1103, two winters with no summer between under ash-grey skies, ended in the finest harvest in a century, which farmers called the Apology. They left the map rearranged. In the east, the famine had decided which lordships submitted to which, made Virelia's crown elective and put the Crown of Embers into the Sanctum's custody ([[The Great Split — Two Crowns, One Fire]]). On Thalmyria's northern frontier whole valleys had emptied. Settlers had died or walked back into the interior for bread. Many of the Southern Houses, the families descended from the occupation who held frontier land under Covenant deeds from the surrender terms of 957–1052, had died too or crossed north into Covenant country, taking their deeds with them. Terraces went to thorn and the aqueduct branches silted. A generation of large families followed the Apology, and by 1120 the empire had more young men than land to settle them on.

## The Wall of the World, 1121–1159

**Caedros III** was crowned at Virelos in 1121, a descendant of Korinna raised in the frontier monasteries, who had left the monastery school at sixteen for the frontier garrisons. He was a big, loud man who danced at district weddings, stood godfather to more than four hundred district children and knew their parents by name. As a garrison captain he had married **Chrysanthe**, a Saronikan admiral's daughter, and her father's ships now carried the settlers' grain; she had given him a son, **Orestan**, and three daughters. He resettled the empty frontier as **military districts**. Each family received land on condition of service: in war it owed a lance, and every year it owed a lamp lit from the Rekindling relay and hung by the door. District towns grew around their granaries and chapels, and their men drilled on the threshing floors.

Some of that land was land the Southern Houses still held by deed. In 1126 the Covenant's Court of Breaches at Iqashar ruled the old surrender instruments breached, and in 1131 the Covenant came to enforce the ruling as it had enforced its claims in the north centuries before. It sent no ordinary army. Its great **mercy towers**, engines that walked before the columns proclaiming terms in the local tongue and predicting where people would flee, offered the district families their property in exchange for surrender. Two of them came south. The district families held their land only so long as they defended it, so surrender meant losing it under their own law, and they would not bargain. The towers kept predicting a breaking point that never came and turned to terror: marked stones left on threshing floors, false orders called through the dusk in familiar voices, mills bombarded at harvest. District women sang the towers' proclamations back at them from the terraces. The terror hardened the districts.

And the district lamps kindled. Thalmyric chroniclers of the **War of the Southern Deeds** (1131–1139) record farmhouse lamps taking the Flame as their families held doorways and granary lofts, and Covenant records describe fire that came off the walls of villages where no warlock was present. At **Kolonna** in 1134 Caedros himself dismounted to carry a district family's children out of a burning granary, and the lamp at his saddle took the Flame before the whole town. Eclessian examiners who came to see the district lamps afterwards found them unremarkable, since they burned only in defence. The Covenant lost a tower, lost its commanding prince to dysentery in the siege camps in 1135, and lost a flank in 1137 when two of its jurists read one order two ways under their provincial codes. That flank's levies threw down their arms at the ford of **Vresna**, and the emperor's son Orestan, who commanded the district horse, had some four hundred of them cut down where they knelt. The district riders, who had lived six years under the towers' terror, cheered him. His father did not. In 1139 the Covenant ransomed custodians whom Thalmyria had captured with registers holding the names of the Covenant's living soldiers, and the ransom refilled the Thalmyric treasury.

That autumn, at Kolonna, before the district families he had fought beside, Caedros declared that his heir would be whichever claimant's lamp the Fire kindled at his bier. Everyone understood that the **Kolonna Declaration** barred Orestan without naming him. The Synod protested that the Fire is not a court of succession. Empress Chrysanthe argued for her son until she died in 1155. The districts cheered again.

What followed was High Noon. Roads were relaid and aqueduct branches reopened. The district towns filled; monasteries rebuilt in stone; Aversian ore came down the fortress roads and Sestaran credit came up the rivers. Caedros spent the Covenant's ransom on all of it, gave a plough-team to each of his godchildren on their marriage, and then borrowed from Sestaran houses to finish the aqueduct at Kolonna. The treasury that had been full in 1139 was in debt by the 1150s. In his last years the emperor kept the lamp that had kindled at Kolonna in his bedchamber and sat up with it at night. It did not kindle again.

## The bill under the mountains

Prosperity had victims close at hand.

**The Three Knocks.** In one high Aversian mine, miners had long kept a compact with the small grey people seen at the edge of the lamplight: three knocks from the dark meant a load-bearing seam was moving; miners left one share of ore and never cut the pale rib that crossed the deepest shaft. The ore of High Noon's arms and ploughs came out of mountains like that one, and Shield-House officials turned the custom into production law. They stopped leaving the share, cut the pale rib to make quota, declared dead workers "court-defaulters" whose families owed the mine replacement labour, blamed missed quotas on "the Knockers" and sent strikers and prisoners into the worst workings as payment. To end the stoppages an overseer arranged a false middle knock meaning the passage was safe, and paid an interpreter to confirm it. When the crews went down, the weight the rib had carried came down on them, and gas filled the refuge chambers the overseer had ordered sealed against strikers. Prisoners, guards and free miners died together. When the surviving interpreters accused the overseer, he executed them for breaking the bargain. Miners never answer the middle of three knocks. [[G-68 - The Three Knocks under the Mountain|The Three Knocks under the Mountain]]

**The Saint of Ashen Wings.** In the silver mines of the Heldenberg, worked by convicts, Two Winters bread-rioters and the children of Folk captives, a slave courier led a revolt through the prison tunnels around 1116 and freed several valleys. When the counts' army came back up the valley in the winter of 1123, he sent condemned overseers through a broken passage in the mountain called the Cold Way. Those who came back had been changed by it, and dangerous, but through them the rebels learned the passage, came out behind the counts' army in the snow and broke it. In 1124 the free valleys swore the **Oath of the Shafts**, the first of Korrheim's cantons. The liberator killed some of the changed survivors with his own hand and hid what the passage had done. Around 1137 he confessed before the canton assembly, laid down his office and went into exile; he spent his last years pulling people out of similar experiments and died in 1146 closing a breach. His cult calls him the Saint of Ashen Wings. Korrheim eats white bread at its liberation feast, and the next morning shares black bread made with the feast's ashes while the experimental victims are named. [[G-37 - The Feast Followed by Ashen Names|The Feast Followed by Ashen Names]]

**The Saint above the Harbour Wall.** In the second of the Two Winters, famine ships from the Eclessian coast came to Sermora and the city closed its harbour chain. A foreign-born chain-tender lowered it. Some ships forced the entry; harbour workers and refugees died in the crush, and Sermora hanged the chain-tender as a traitor. In 1166 a rival's fleet and hired Ättar keels besieged the city with a leased storm-engine. Defenders and the attackers' own chronicles describe a terrifying figure above the harbour wall; the engine broke and the siege failed. Afterwards Sermoran soldiers murdered foreign residents blamed for the breach, and the apparition's rebuke is remembered alongside the rescue. At the city's feast the harbour chain is lowered for one foreign or refugee vessel before it is raised for the procession, and older songs still call the chain-tender a traitor.

**The Great Southern Purge.** In the south of Eclessia, marcher lords and the Knights of Saint Aveline spent 1158–1163 destroying Folk seers and shrine settlements under a relic-court ruling that the shrines held unexamined fire. They cut the boundary groves. In the midwinter of 1163, riders whose breath made no smoke went through the marcher camps on three nights, taking an Aveline chapter and the Folk households nearest the cut groves alike, and the Purge ended. ## The Unanswered Funeral, 1159

Caedros III died in 1159, four years after his empress, and the Kolonna Declaration came due: at his bier in Veridun's cathedral, beneath the Great Lamp, the claimant whose lamp kindled would rule. The Synod objected and was overruled by a dead man.

Seven claimants stood at the bier holding lamps: Orestan, two sons-in-law who commanded field armies, three nephews and the captain of the palace guard. None kindled. Orestan's flared, and an old warlock in the choir smelled script-fire under the wick; "Shell-fire smells of hot coin," he said, and the guard walked him out of the cathedral. The court wanted an emperor before morning, and it had a lamp that had flared in the hand of the dead emperor's son. It acclaimed Orestan. In the nave, among the district honour guard, **Despina of Kolonna**, a soldier's widow who had walked to Veridun to pray for her three sons on the frontier, was holding a lamp of her own. It took the Flame. The court suppressed that report as it had silenced the warlock. The district soldiers carried both stories home.

The districts would not have the emperor they called Hot-Coin. In the **War of the Seven Lamps** (1159–1162) two of the other claimants fought for themselves, Orestan's hired companies held Veridun and the river road, and the district armies carried the widow's lamp as their warrant. In 1162 they broke Orestan's companies under the walls of Kolonna and acclaimed their own commander, **Andrion**, whom the court called the Widow's Emperor and who took the name as a badge. They brought Orestan to Kolonna in a farm cart and asked Despina what should be done with him. She said he should keep a grave. Andrion sent him to the ford of Vresna, where the four hundred lay in a pit with no marker, to build a chapel over them with his own hands and keep it. He kept it for twenty years. Thalmyric preachers still argue whether the Declaration saved the empire from the butcher of Vresna or drove a barred son to buy script-fire, and whether the ford was his penance or his prison.

Despina refused every summons to court. The Widow's Lamp is kept in Kolonna's church, and district families still carry their door-lamps to it on the anniversary.

## The Threshing Floor, 1171–1173

Andrion (1162–1198) was the districts' emperor. He inherited an empty treasury and Caedros's Sestaran debts, extended the district levy to pay them, and had district households counted for it. In Elenara, the river province of vineyards and monasteries, the prince saw in those years a chance at a royal crown of his own and ordered a census of his own. It was announced as grain relief before a drought, and the count did move grain; its real purpose was to seize dependants for road labour, list refugees for taxation and choose hostages from doubtful households. He was warned that it went beyond the law and went ahead, because a complete register would prove him a king. Clerks and soldiers broke down doors, dragged families to the counting stations and killed those who resisted. Mothers hid children under grain, and the tally-marks led the patrols to every barn.

A mortality followed along the census roads. Count-houses and barracks fell sick within hours of each sealed schedule's arrival: fever, then failing breath. Clerks, soldiers, nobles, labourers and children died. Witnesses saw a dark, immense raptor outline filled with white fire above the leading edge of the road. The prince first blamed foreigners. Then he confessed what the census had been for, released the hostages, burned the schedule and came barefoot to the threshing floor of a household at **Kremna** that his census had already bereaved. The mortality stopped there.

Court histories call it plague. Devotional Lives name a commissioned angel. Harvest songs say the soldiers finally refused their orders after seeing their own children sick. Elenara stayed a duchy, and is one in 1360. Thalmyric and later Eclessian law codes require a census to state its purpose and forbid its use for seizure without a new public act; jurists call the limit "the floor beyond the count". After communal threshing in the river country, one circle of grain is left uncounted and handed out by hand. [[G-48 - The Threshing Floor beyond the Count|The Threshing Floor beyond the Count]]

## The east under an empty crown

In Eclessia the Crown of Embers stayed in its vault. Kings who petitioned the Sanctum to bring it out received one answer: reunion first, which in practice meant Veridun's submission to examination. The last king of Aldwin's line died in 1166, and the Primarchs took the Aurelian estates into the same trust as the crown and ruled Aurelia directly.

Around 1180, after a local famine, children across Brauvalis, Caldria and Veridancia left their homes on one autumn morning and walked west toward Caleran, calm, carrying bread. All were turned back at the watershed, by a wall of birds or a preaching stranger or nothing they could name, the accounts disagree. All came home except forty, and of the forty there are no baptismal records, no household rolls and no graves, while their parents remained certain to their deaths that someone was missing. The Church ruled the **Walking of the Small** a fever-dream of grief after the famine. The ruling has never been read aloud without someone objecting. Around 1203 a weeping Lady began to be reported at Bruckmoor, and the parents of the Walking were among her first pilgrims.

In 1183 the Sanctum defined the **Unkindled Conception**, teaching that the Mother was conceived "without ash", a vessel made pure by grace before she bore the Fire; in 1204 it defined her bodily **Rising**. Eclessian preachers tied the first definition to the grief of the Walking. Veridun rejected the Sanctum's authority to define either doctrine, and Thalmyric polemicists said the Sanctum had defined a doctrine to change the subject.

## The standard and the keys

The company remembered through the **Ransomed Standard** formed in the 1180s and 1190s to recover bodies and pledged prisoners from ground neither army wanted to enter, and served the High-Noon frontier until its fortunes broke with Qarath. It contained professionals, exiles and hostages. Failed ransom meant mutilation and execution. Reprisal killed noncombatants, and members of the company took part in the murder of surrendered rivals. Later accounts disagree about who commanded. Its later bearer kept the torn hostage ties hanging where a victory streamer should be.

Around 1201 a Velian amir's coalition besieged a district town. Its council hid the town's stores, sold the amir a share of them, and sold him the gate keys as well, for its own safe passage out. The **Woman Who Returned the Keys** was a widow who kept the town's warehouse inventories, and she saw grain she had tallied into the hidden stores being eaten in the enemy camp. She walked out to the siege lines with her tally-boards as the council's clerk, come to count the surrender stores, and at the amir's pay-table she read the tallies aloud: what the council had sold him, and what he had told his allied captains he had paid for it. The captains found two months of their own pay in the difference and cut him down at the table. The coalition broke up within the week, fighting over his baggage, and the town lost more people in that week's fighting than in the siege. She came home with the keys, which had been in his strongbox. The town hanged two councillors from the warehouse hoist and offered her the keys for life. She handed them back rather than turn a rescue into a hereditary crown.

## Qarath's Well, 1214

**Suhrab the Debtor**'s reign, 1190–1243, brought the Covenant to its apex. Raised as a jurist after renouncing the throne, he took it when a court ruled his renunciation void, and spent his reign writing the *Ledger of Ledgers*, a book of the occasions on which Covenant law had killed the Covenant's own people, each closed with the clause owed. In 1212 his Court of Breaches closed its book of the Southern Deeds by reviving the ruling of 1126 with a new offer to the district families on the old Southern House lands: confirmation of their farms under Covenant deed, with no levy owed. After two generations of Andrion's levies, some families accepted and registered their deeds at the Court's frontier bench beside **Qarath's Well** in the salt country.

**Markellos**, Andrion's grandson, had come to the throne in 1209 at twenty-three, a soldier raised in the districts. In the drought summer of 1214 he marched to Qarath's Well to stop his own people accepting. The outer wells along the road had been fouled with carcasses by Tarkhan companies in Covenant pay, and by the time the army reached Qarath its water carts were dry. The well was a sworn well, with a bound warden in its depths and terms cut into its curb in three scripts, Atherian among them: *Drink, guest, and be at peace with the house of this well until the sun sets again. The guest who lifts his hand against the house before then owes the house the price of the water, and the house names the price.* Most of the officers who could read the Atherian took it for a dedication. **Nikandros**, of a cadet Aetherion branch in Kedrava, who commanded the Kedravan monastic contingents and had learned his letters in a refuge monastery, read it aloud to the emperor's council as a bargain and was overruled. The army drank at dusk. Markellos, who had heard him, drank last, from the same bucket as his men. The Kedravans did not drink at all, because the refuge monasteries' rule since the occupation forbade drinking sworn water.

At dawn, when the drinkers took up their spears against the Covenant line, the water in them turned. Men vomited water; their arms hung like wet rope; some drowned standing in the dust. Nikandros formed up the Kedravans and withdrew in order with every wounded man they could carry. The Tarkhan companies, on horses descended from the fallen empire's own studs that do not shy from bound things, rode through what was left. Markellos stayed with the men who had drunk.

At sunset on the second day the well's peace ran out. No Covenant officer came for the emperor's sword. Under the terms on the curb the drinkers were guests who had broken the house's peace, and the house would name what they owed. The custodians of the frontier bench sat by the well-house and waited. At dusk new letters stood wet in the stone below the old ones, in the same three scripts: *A well for every cup.* The custodians copied them down.

Suhrab was at Iqashar and never saw the field. He had wanted the military districts settled on the Southern House lands, the ruling of 1126 enforced eighty-eight years late, and his Court had drawn the deeds of cession before the army marched: a bounded gain he could seal and call a peace. He is said to have asked the Court of Breaches whether the price could be lowered to that, and to have been told that the Sultan-Caliph is not the house. Iqashari tradition says he entered Qarath in his own *Ledger*; Thalmyrics do not believe it.

Every man who had drunk owed the house the well of his own holding, and until he gave it the water stayed turned in him. The army walked home with its spears tied across its backs, because the men could not close their hands. On the Southern House lands the debt was collected farm by farm. A custodian came with the family that still held the old bronze water-token for that ground, a deed-stone went up at the field corner, the holder said the words, and his arms came back to him; afterwards his household drew its own water as the token-holder's tenants. A drinker from the interior, where no custodian could reach, stayed as weak as a child unless he walked back to the frontier bench and signed away a well his family had dug. The Ransomed Standard recovered the dead from the salt flats under a truce. Thalmyric preachers still argue whether the Fire left the army that drank or stayed with the emperor who drank last; Covenant jurists say the terms were posted in the drinkers' own tongue.

## The wound made at home, 1214–1225

Markellos had drunk under his standard, and the Court of Breaches held that the Throne's cup was owed the Throne's well: the head of the aqueduct Caedros III had built at Kolonna, which watered the districts. Markellos would not give it. He came home to Veridun in a litter with the water still in him, unable to grip a pen.

His uncle **Sergian**, the chancellor who kept the empty treasury and the seals, made use of that. Sergian's jurists argued that a holder who owed his well to the enemy held his farm for the enemy, and that a district holding, which is held for defence, was forfeit the moment its holder drank. In the winter of 1214 the court declared the **Forfeiture of the Drinkers**: every district holder who had drunk at Qarath lost his farm, and the farms went to the hired companies Sergian needed and had no money to pay. The seal on the decree was the emperor's. The hand that pressed it was Sergian's.

When Markellos learned what had been sealed in his name, he had himself carried out of the palace and up the mountain road to Kedra, where the monks who had brought his wounded home took him in. He never sat in the Crown Hall again. Sergian kept the seals and governed in the name of an emperor who would not come down, and his envoys told Iqashar that the cup had been the man's and the man was gone. Within the season the Court of Breaches answered that the emperor had drunk under his standard, so the Throne had drunk; the Throne does not die, and it had not left the palace when the man did. It cited the Sublime Threshold and the deposit by which the Covenant had once kept the Eternal Ember for Veridun to redeem. In 1215 Covenant garrisons took the aqueduct head at Kolonna, and with it the Southern Deeds districts below. A thing taken by force is not a thing given, so the debt stood, and Markellos stayed sick. Since that answer no Thalmyric emperor will drink as a Covenant guest or put his name to a Covenant instrument, because whatever he swallows or swears is the Throne's.

In the rest of the frontier Sergian's companies turned the drinkers' families off their farms, burned barns with the harvest still in them and beat men who were too weak from the water-sickness to lift a spade off their own threshing floors. The Kedravan houses were offered forfeited farms and refused them: *we did not drink, and we will not eat.* The forfeiture bought nothing but shame.

The war that followed is called the **War of the Seal**, because for ten years the emperor was in a monastery cell and the empire was whoever held his seal. Sergian's companies, the dispossessed drinkers, the Kedravan monasteries behind Nikandros, the Saronikan admiralty and the duke of Elenara fought over a throne with an empty treasury. In 1217 drinkers' families who had walked to Veridun with their door-lamps killed Sergian in the Crown Hall. That year Caldrian marcher captains crossed the watershed and seized three rite-changing valleys while Thalmyria could not defend them.

Two stories from the war are told everywhere. In Vlastrava, around 1220, a mediator arranged a surrender that saved King Stanimir's household. Once they were safe, the mediator offered him his enemy's defenceless children and urged him to avenge a murdered kinsman. Stanimir had promised protection freely, and refused; his own brother called the refusal disloyal and killed the mediator, and the children were returned under guard. Vlastravan Lives call the mediator a devil's agent, and Vlastrava's word became the one men trusted. In a lesser city around the same time, a poor wall-worker saw that an emergency pumping order would burst the cisterns under a crowded district, and stopped it with a vent sequence nobody had taught her; the officials took the credit and withheld her wages, and engineers still cut her notch into the stone before a pressure test.

**Nikandros I**, 1225–1263, was crowned at Virelos by the gift of the emperor he had warned. Markellos, thirty-nine and still sick, came down from Kedra with the drinkers behind him, poured a cup of water out on the floor before the altar without tasting it, and put his claim into Nikandros's hands: "I drank. He did not." The price was Nikandros's oath to give the drinkers back their farms. Drinkers' songs say the cup was poured freely; Sergianist chronicles say the Kedravan abbot held his wrist. Both may be true, since Markellos had lifted nothing heavier than a spoon in eleven years. Every emperor crowned at Virelos since has poured out a cup of water untasted before he takes the crown. The monasteries and Vlastrava stood behind the new emperor, and he was not known to kill surrendered households. He kept half his oath. His soldiers turned Sergian's companies off the forfeited farms inside the empire, burning their huts and killing the men who resisted, but he could not reach the districts the Covenant held. Later tellers made him merciful in everything, which he was not. Markellos took vows at Kedra and died there in 1231, with the water still in him.

Nikandros asked nobody for help. In 1227 help was offered. A legation from Aurelium came to Veridun with the largest offer the Sanctum has ever made: it would bring the Crown of Embers out of its vault and set it on Nikandros as the one head of a reunited empire, send Eclessian lances against the Covenant and order the Caldrian captains out of the three valleys. Its price was that the Great Lamp of Veridun be carried east and tried by the Sanctum's relic court. Thalmyrics say the legates had brought a cord to measure his head. Nikandros refused, saying that "a lamp that is tried is a lamp that is owned", as Makarios had refused with four nails two centuries before, and fought on alone. A decade later the plague did what no ally would. Covenant garrisons whose oaths had lost their witnesses marched home between 1238 and 1246, and Nikandros walked through their open gates and won back about half the lost districts and the aqueduct head. His restoration created the line later claimed by Caedros IV, but it could not restore all territory or heal every provincial loss. Victorious chronicles and roadside graves tell very different versions of this recovery.

## The Sweat and the Black Wind

The Grey Sweat came in waves from 1232 to 1247 and killed faster than priests could bury. [[The Grey Sweat]] tells how the dead went unsent and walked, how Thalmyric villagers sang their own dead out, how the Covenant's court benches emptied and how the fever stopped everywhere in the same week. [[G-16 - The Forty-First Ferry|The Forty-First Ferry]] remembers the night ferry that carried the dead the licensed boats refused. After the fever the Sanctum sold sealed letters promising that Sending Houses would sing rites for named dead, and a miller's daughter, Saint Gerlind, walked the haunted parishes singing them out for bread.

On the steppe a captive Velian farrier sang over a dying black colt in 1249 and renewed the old horse-grace. Qarayel, whose herds came from that colt, was acclaimed in 1261, and from 1265 his Black Wind burned the Velian fairs and made Iqashar and the Sestaran roads pay ([[Qarayel and the Black Wind]]). Nikandros's son Evandros the Sower, busy resettling the half-empty districts with Kedravan mountain families, sent envoys in 1279 to buy the farrier for the imperial studs; two years later the farrier was found in the horse lines with his throat cut, and Qarayel sent the envoys' heads to Veridun in a feed-bag. The grass-toll pushed Sestaran trade onto the sea, and the Medori grew rich on the estates of the plague dead and the payments of the living for their Sendings. In 1274 their credit bought a Primarch ([[Historical Spine of the Sestaran Concord]]).

The era left resettled terraces, mountain wounds, abandoned wards, rival feasts and a trade in promises to the dead that was already selling more rites than it sang. It led into [[The Age of Held Proofs]].
