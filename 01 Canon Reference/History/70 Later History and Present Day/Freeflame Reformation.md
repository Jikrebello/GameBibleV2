# Freeflame Reformation

The **Freeflame Reformation** is Eclessia's continuing religious fracture over who may send the dead, who may kindle the White Flame, and whether the Church's routing assurances ever had the power claimed for them. It began at a flooded pithead in Brenholt in 1297, when the friar Ansgar of Brenholt sang the *Hundred Grievances* over forty-one drowned miners instead of letting their widows buy letters, and it produced a funeral strike, a war fought out of the mines, a princes' war, a reforming council in the old Church and, in 1343, the [[The Korrheim Accord|Korrheim Accord]] that froze the map. The Freeflame, or Order of the Open Flame, govern Ironvale and much of the Markish north in 1360.

The quarrel was sharper than a dispute about money. In this world prayer for the dead does something, and so does its absence. Both sides of the Reformation had seen the dead walk.

## The assurance trade at its height

After the [[01 Canon Reference/History/70 Later History and Present Day/The Grey Sweat.md|Grey Sweat]], the Sanctum's Chancery of Sendings sold sealed letters promising that a Sending House would sing named rites for a named soul. Where the rites were sung, the trade kept its promise, and families who had watched their dead return after the plague paid gladly. The trouble was arithmetic. By the 1280s the Chancery was selling letters far faster than any House could sing them, and a Primarch had told the world that this did not matter.

**Lord Primarch Alessior II** (r. 1281–1296) was a Malvora of Sestara whom the Sweat had orphaned at twelve. The chapter that sang for his family raised him, and as a bishop he was known for walking into haunted villages and singing them quiet. He slept on a board and ate bread. In 1281 Bartolo Medori's credit bought the conclave's votes for him. Alessior learned the price when the bills came, after his election, and he paid them. In 1283 his **Bull of the Sufficient Seal** taught that a letter sealed by the Chancery placed the named soul in the Church's keeping from the day the wax was pressed, and that the rite would follow in the Church's time. He believed it, and on that doctrine the letters could be sold without limit. He rebuilt the Sanctum's fallen dome and meant to paint beneath it, in gold, the name of every soul for whom a letter had been sold; the painters had covered about a ninth of it when he died. At night he signed letters with his own hand in the Chancery's copy-room, thousands at a sitting. The engineer of the trade was his Cardinal-Treasurer.

### Silvio Quarne, the Bridge-Seller

**Silvio Quarne** (c. 1238–1299) was a Fiorenzian notary's son left alive by the last wave of the Sweat with a ledger and a gift for figures. He entered the Malvora household as a clerk, joined the Chancery about 1265 and made it efficient. He introduced the printed letter; the schedule of rates by age and estate, with its cheaper rate for a child; the blank letter sold with the name left open; and the household bond that covered a family for three generations. He became Cardinal-Treasurer in 1282. In 1284 he put the newly canonised Saint Gerlind's face on the letters, and the Gerlind seal outsold every other. In 1285 an apparition of Gerlind condemned the singers of Aurelium's Saint-Leon quarter, who sang the dead out for nothing, and a riot burned the quarter. Lantern investigators later traced the apparition to his office.

His own audits told him by 1288 that the Houses sang perhaps one rite in ten of those sold. He sold the unsung letters too. Brokers in Vorthara paid well for bundles of what the trade came to call **dead paper**, and the money went to the Medori and the dome. When the Freeflame later found dead paper in brokers' houses, it bore his office's countersign. Quarne fled Aurelium in 1297 with his private ledgers and died two years later in a rented room in the Black Spire wards of Vorthara. The ledgers were gone when the room was opened. Sestaran historians call him a gifted administrator blamed for a Primarch's credulity. The Freeflame named him after Ansgar's First Grievance: the **Bridge-Seller**. Vortharan tellers say his ledgers still add up at night.

### The Primarch under the dome

Alessior died of a winter fever in the copy-room early in 1296 and was buried beneath his dome with a sealed letter in his hands. Within the month the dome-painters and sacristans were saying that he walked at night under the gold names, reading them. The Chancery's own choir sang him out by name, and the Sanctum has denied the whole matter ever since. By summer the story had run down every road in Eclessia: the Primarch's seal had not sent the Primarch. He died owing the Medori more than the Primarchal State could pay, for his election and his dome, and Bartolo Medori took the Chancery's master registers as surety.

## Ansgar's Count

**Ansgar of Brenholt** (1264–1305) was a marches friar and a lecturer on the doctrine of the Vestibule, a miner's son from the iron country of the upper Weiss. When he was seven, Saint Gerlind had come to Brenholt and sung his father's name after a collapse at the mine-head. In the summer of 1296, when the story of the dome reached the Weiss, he set the letters sold in Brenholt parish against the rites its Sending House had actually sung, and found more than ten letters for every rite. He then counted the dead still seen at the mine-heads and the ford and found them among the unsung names. His friars sang those names themselves. The walking stopped, and several of them swore that a woman's voice joined the hymn.

## The Blackwater funerals

In the late spring of 1297 the Blackwater pit at Brenholt flooded on the night shift and drowned forty-one miners, eleven of them boys. In the morning the Sending House's chaplain set up a trestle at the pithead with the printed table of rates, and the widows queued. Ansgar overturned the trestle. He had each family say aloud the names of its dead, and then, where the funeral rite should have been, he sang the *[[The Hundred Grievances — Selections|Hundred Grievances]]* as a litany, and the crowd answered each one with Gerlind's saying: *named, not bought*. A pit-tally girl chalked them on the board where the shifts were counted. *"No seal of wax weighs anything at the Weighing. You have sold the poor a bridge that is not yours."* The First Grievance answered Alessior's bull; the hundred together named the dead Primarch eleven times; the Hundredth ended with a challenge: *prove me wrong with Fire, fathers, and not with fees.*

The Grievances went down the Weiss as a song before anyone printed them. Bargemen sang them at the locks and carters at the inns. Across the upper Weiss country families began to bury their dead the Brenholt way, with the names said aloud by the people who had known them, a hymn and no letter. The Sending Houses called these **unbought funerals** and the courts called them unlicensed rites. By 1300 the Houses of the upper Weiss had no buyers, and their chaplains had been stoned out of three towns. The Ironvale printing of the Grievances, made about 1299 from the tally-board copy, carried them further than the song.

## Aldersbruck

In 1305, at the demand of the dukes of the Weiss and the starving Houses, a synod met at Aldersbruck, the bridge-town below Brenholt, under the legate Tiburce Vaudrey, and took up the Hundredth Grievance. By old relic-court custom the fire is put to the book and not to the man. Ansgar's psalter, its margins full of the names of the Brenholt dead he had sung out, was laid on a brazier in the bridge chapel before the synod, Duke Ortwin of Ironvale and as much of Brenholt as could crowd onto the bridge. It did not burn.

The legate declared the trial void for a draught and damp vellum, sealed the psalter in lead, and ordered Ansgar held in the duke's toll-tower on the bridge while he wrote to the Sanctum for instructions. The duke wanted the prisoner out of his duchy, and the legate would not move him without orders. The thaw came while they argued. On the night the Weiss took the bridge, the guards ran from the rising water with the keys, and the duke's fourteen-year-old son **Rainald**, sent that evening to bring the prisoner off, was turned back at the bridgehead and watched the tower go into the river. Ansgar's body was never found.

The synod's junior notary, **Lucian Ferro**, a twenty-six-year-old jurist from the Latinate south, wrote all of it down: the brazier, the unburnt book, the void ruling, the tower and the keys. His record had been copied before anyone thought to stop it.

The Sanctum vaulted the Unburnt Psalter in lead and has denied it exists ever since. The reach of the Weiss below the broken bridge became the **Preacher's Water**. Oaths sworn on it hold strangely well. Freeflame congregations say the legate and the duke killed Ansgar by keeping him on the bridge. Old-Church preachers say the river gave the verdict the brazier would not. The Weiss bargemen say the river took back a man who had named its drowned. In the hill parishes above Brenholt people still say he climbed out downstream, and that the nameless friar who sometimes sings at a mine-head funeral is him.

## The Ashenmen Risings, 1306–1329

In the spring of 1306 miners and farmers from Brenholt and the upper Weiss waded into the river at the broken bridge, smeared their faces with the grey silt the iron-washings leave in it, and swore to fight until the last false seal was burned. Their enemies called them the Ashenmen, after the plague penitents; they kept the name for the grey on their faces.

They fought out of the ground. The Ashenmen held the pits and drifts of the upper Weiss, which they knew and their enemies did not. By night they came up to raid Sending Houses and church estates, carried the letter-chests down the shafts and burned the seals in the deep galleries. At Brenholt they hanged the Sending House's chaplains from the pithead winding gear. They smashed images of the Mother across the iron country, a guilt the movement's second generation still handles badly: reformed congregations keep her feast under other names. Old-Church armies burned the villages above the pits and could not dig out the men below them. In 1314 Caldrian engineers turned the Weiss into the Brenholt drifts, and about three hundred people drowned in the dark, women and children among them, who had been sheltering there. The Ashenmen came up from other shafts within the month.

The oath held. In twenty-three years no Ashenmen band is recorded breaking its word, deserting or selling out a neighbour. They were impossible to bargain with, because their oath named an end no prince would grant, and they starved the iron country: from 1306 the mines of the upper Weiss sent almost nothing to the forges. Bond-labour, tithe, Sending fees and old grudges kept the bands full.

In 1311, in a valley the Ashenmen had stripped of images, the Mother appeared to a crippled washerwoman at **Sarnwell**, and the spring that rose there has passed every test the relic courts have devised. The old Church read it as a rebuke to the image-breakers. Reformed preachers called it bait. It is the Sanctum's proudest modern proof.

The Risings ended by the letter of their oath. In 1329 Rainald, Duke of Ironvale since 1309 and by then converted, seized the chests of every Sending House in his duchy and burned their sealed letters in Brenholt market square. The Ashenmen of Ironvale held their oath fulfilled, came up out of the pits and took service under his banner, and the iron came up with them.

## Ferro and the Open Flame

Ferro spent his life in the south. After Aldersbruck he became a canonist at the university of Veridance, and about 1312 the Sanctum, which needed the Brenholt teaching answered in words the Weiss country would read, commissioned him to set it out in full and then refute it. About 1316 he delivered the first half, *The Order of the Open Flame*. It states the case with a fairness nobody in the Sanctum had wanted. The Lord's Flame kindles wherever real sanctity gathers, *the Fire keeps no landlord*, and so no hierarchy can mint it or sell it. Congregations are governed by tested elders. They read scripture aloud, cast hymns together, refuse the assurance trade and keep relic devotion to a minimum, and the congregation sings the Sending for anyone who dies among them, whatever the dead could pay, on the precedents of Gerlind and of the Ferry Rule, which in the plague years had let any baptised person sing the funeral where no priest lived. The reformers adopted the book whole as their order.

The refutation never came. Ferro said for thirty-six years that he was still writing it. The Sanctum suspended his licence to teach and never tried him, because a trial would have meant reading his book aloud. In 1330 Rainald, who had read Ferro's record of Aldersbruck with his own father's name in it, invited him to Ironvale. Ferro refused: *I wrote their case. I did not promise to plead it.* The reformed towns built on his book became famously orderly, literate and merciless without him. Freeflame elders call him theirs. Lantern teachers call him the Church's most honest servant. Veridun's chroniclers say he proves that an Eclessian lawyer cannot refuse a brief.

## The Reform Wars and the end of a ducal house

From 1329 the war belonged to princes. Rainald armed the heirs of the Risings with the iron they had brought up, confiscated Church estates and fought old-Church neighbours along the Weiss. Caldria's campaigns of 1310–1324 and Aurelia's trade war of 1310–1313 against Freeflame-linked merchants had already shown how quickly doctrine became a land war. Princes on both sides took Church property they had wanted for generations. In 1340 the Freeflame-majority canton of Falkendorf resisted an old-Church settlement and brought Korrheim to the edge of civil war; Rainald's last son died in the fighting. Rainald died in 1341 without an heir. His house had spent its blood, its credit and its standing with the Church, and Ironvale's guild ministries chartered the Grand Ministry to govern in its place.

## The old Church reforms

Lord Primarch **Celestin IX** (r. 1330–1354) convened the **Council of Veridance** in 1337, in the university town where Ferro lived, and its first act was to summon him to deliver the refutation he had owed for twenty years. Ferro, fifty-eight years old, came to the hall and told the fathers that he could not refute the parts of his book that were true, and offered to show them which parts those were. The Council kept him. For fifteen years he sat by the stove in the guild-hall of Veridance as its witness, never voting, and the Council's decrees are a line-by-line answer to *The Order of the Open Flame* with its author in the room.

It abolished assurance sales and repudiated Alessior's Sufficient Seal. Without the seal, every letter sold and never sung was a debt to a named soul, and the Council ordered every one of them performed: **the Great Arrears**. To sing them it chartered, in 1338, a brotherhood the Sanctum had spent fifty years suppressing. Its founder, **Father Ivo of Saint-Leon**, had been carried out of the burning Saint-Leon quarter in 1285 as a child of two. He grew up among the quarter's scattered singers, who went on sending the dead for nothing, at night and by lantern-light because by day it was forbidden, and he came to Veridance as a grown man to study under Ferro. As the **Order of the Lantern**, his singers walked haunted parishes singing names out of the old registers. The registers had been in the Medori vault since 1296, held as surety for Alessior's debts, and the Council had to buy copies sheet by sheet. The Lantern's first great case proved the Saint-Leon apparition staged. Freeflame congregations call the order a stolen choir.

The Council also rebuilt seminary education, teaching the reformers' books accurately so that clergy could answer them, and in its last year codified the **five tests** of sainthood, drafted around Sarnwell. Veridun noticed that none of the five can be applied to a saint who left no remains, which rules out Caedros I. Ferro died in the spring of 1352, and the Council closed that autumn, in the year of Vessing. The reform kept the old Church standing and did not restore its credit with the people who had bought the letters.

## What it left

Freeflame reform found a real fraud against the dead and made villages sing their own Sendings again. Its congregations taught farm children to read the Grievances aloud and disciplined corrupt clergy in front of them. It also hanged chaplains from winding gear, let Rainald and his neighbours strip Church estates they had wanted for generations, broke the Mother's images and the people who kept them, and raised children who called every grandmother's custom a corruption. Old-Church writers point out that the Freeflame's most famous object is a river, its most famous saint is a woman they forbid anyone to pray to, and its order was written by the Sanctum's own lawyer. Freeflame writers answer that the Sanctum's most famous object is a book it says it does not have.

## The frozen settlement

The [[The Korrheim Accord|Korrheim Accord]] of 1343, sworn on the Preacher's Water, ended open war by letting each recognised ruler fix the confession of his territory. Radical congregations who would not swear on the river were left outside it. Conscience, mixed families and border parishes were left unresolved.

## Present-day fault lines

- Ironvale is the strongest Freeflame polity, governed by guild ministers.
- Veridancia trains both old-Church reformers and some of their most effective opponents.
- Virelia's elective crown could change the confessional balance with one succession.
- Grenzburg is formally old Church but exposed to migrants, pamphlets and reform-minded labourers.
- Dead paper still circulates, and brokers still press claims on the named dead.
- The Hollow Flame exploits every abuse while offering severance rather than reform.
