# Freeflame Reformation

The **Freeflame Reformation** is Eclessia's continuing religious fracture over who may send the dead, who may kindle the White Flame, and whether the Church's routing assurances ever had the power claimed for them. It began at a flooded pithead in Brenholt in 1290, when the widows of forty-one drowned miners turned away from the Sending House's trestle and buried their dead by reading aloud from the psalter of the chaplain who had drowned with them. It produced a funeral strike, a war fought out of the mines, a council that tried to pay the Church's debts to the dead, a princes' war over who should sing them, and in 1343 the [[The Korrheim Accord|Korrheim Accord]], which drew the map by counting graveyards. The Freeflame, or Order of the Open Flame, govern Ironvale and much of the Markish north in 1360.

The quarrel was sharper than a dispute about money. In this world prayer for the dead does something, and so does its absence. Both sides of the Reformation had seen the dead walk.

## The assurance trade at its height

After the [[01 Canon Reference/History/70 Later History and Present Day/The Grey Sweat.md|Grey Sweat]], the Sanctum's Chancery of Sendings sold sealed letters promising that a Sending House would sing named rites for a named soul. Where the rites were sung, the trade kept its promise, and families who had watched their dead return after the plague paid gladly. The trouble was arithmetic. By the 1280s the Chancery was selling letters far faster than any House could sing them, and a Primarch had told the world that this did not matter.

**Lord Primarch Alessior II** (r. 1274–1289) was a Malvora of Sestara whom the Sweat had orphaned at twelve. The chapter that sang for his family raised him, and as a bishop he was known for walking into haunted villages and singing them quiet. He slept on a board and ate bread. In 1274 Bartolo Medori's credit bought the conclave's votes for him. Alessior learned the price when the bills came, after his election, and he paid them. In 1282 his new treasurer told him that the Houses were years behind their letters and falling further. Alessior could not believe that the Church had been allowed to promise what it could not perform, and in 1283 his **Bull of the Sufficient Seal** taught that a letter sealed by the Chancery placed the named soul in the Church's keeping from the day the wax was pressed, and that the rite would follow in the Church's time. On that doctrine the letters could be sold without limit, and he also gave them away. On the great feasts the Chancery sealed letters for the poor at no charge, and in 1287 he had one sealed for every name in Aurelia's plague rolls, the dead of the Sweat who had been burned unblessed on the river banks: some four hundred thousand letters, with no rite behind any of them. At night he signed letters with his own hand in the Chancery's copy-room, thousands at a sitting. Parishes that had sung for their own poor since the plague stopped singing, because the seal sufficed. The engineer of the trade was his Cardinal-Treasurer.

### Silvio Quarne, the Bridge-Seller

**Silvio Quarne** (c. 1238–1299) was a Fiorenzian notary's son left alive by the last wave of the Sweat with a ledger and a gift for figures. He entered the Malvora household as a clerk, joined the Chancery about 1265 and made it efficient. He introduced the printed letter; the schedule of rates by age and estate, with its cheaper rate for a child; the blank letter sold with the name left open; and the household bond that covered a family for three generations. He became Cardinal-Treasurer in 1282. In 1284 he put the newly canonised Saint Gerlind's face on the letters, and the Gerlind seal outsold every other. In 1285 an apparition of Gerlind condemned the singers of Aurelium's Saint-Leon quarter, who sang the dead out for nothing, and a riot burned the quarter. Lantern investigators later traced the apparition to his office.

His own audits told him by 1288 that the Houses sang perhaps one rite in ten of those sold. He sold the unsung letters too. Brokers in Vorthara paid well for bundles of what the trade came to call **dead paper**, and the money went to the Medori, who were still owed for the election. When the Freeflame later found dead paper in brokers' houses, it bore his office's countersign. When the Houses of the upper Weiss stopped sending money in 1297, Quarne fled Aurelium with his private ledgers and died two years later in a rented room in the Black Spire wards of Vorthara. The ledgers were gone when the room was opened. Sestaran historians call him a gifted administrator blamed for a Primarch's credulity. The Freeflame named him from the first note in a drowned chaplain's psalter: the **Bridge-Seller**. Vortharan tellers say his ledgers still add up at night.

### The Primarch at his desk

Alessior died of a winter fever in the copy-room early in 1289 and was buried in the Sanctum's crypt with a sealed letter in his hands. Within the month the night clerks were saying that he still sat at the signing desk after the lamps were out, and that in the morning there were letters on it, sealed and signed in his hand, with the name left blank. The Chancery's own choir sang him out by name, and the Sanctum has denied the whole matter ever since. By summer the story had run down every road in Eclessia: the Primarch's seal had not sent the Primarch. He had died in the first winter of the War of the Crimson Banner, which Caldria and the Drusenwald march fought until 1295 over a frontier relic that the Church courts claimed, and two years after the free sealing of the plague rolls. He owed the Medori more than the Primarchal State could then pay, and Bartolo Medori took the Chancery's master registers as surety.

## The singers for nothing

The practice that broke the trade was older than the trade's worst years. Saint Gerlind had sung the plague dead out for bread, and when a relic court tried her in 1266 it let her go on condition that she sing only with a priest present. Two lines of singers came down from that ruling. In the cities, lay choirs went on sending the poor for nothing and took a priest with them when they could find one. The largest was in Aurelium's Saint-Leon quarter, and after the quarter was burned in 1285 its survivors sang at night and carried lanterns because by day it was forbidden. In the country, a few Sending House chaplains did the same work alone after their paid rites were finished, and lost their places when they were caught. The Reformation came out of the second line and the Order of the Lantern out of the first, and each has called the other a thief of Gerlind ever since.

## Ansgar's Count

**Ansgar of Brenholt** (1260–1290) was a marches friar who had lectured on the doctrine of the Vestibule before he came home as chaplain of the Brenholt Sending House, in the iron country of the upper Weiss. His father was the House's letter-seller. When Ansgar was seven, Saint Gerlind had come to Brenholt and sung at the mine-head after a collapse, with the House's old chaplain beside her as her acquittal required, and the boy had held the old man's book.

From about 1284 he kept two tallies in the margins of his psalter: the letters his father's trestle sold, and the rites the House gave him time to sing. They ran at better than ten to one. He then counted the dead still seen at the mine-heads and the ford, and found them among the names he had never been given time for. He sang those names himself, at night and for nothing, and wrote each one in the margin when it was done. The walking stopped where he sang, and several who sang with him swore that a woman's voice joined the hymn. Beside the names he wrote what he could not say from the House's pulpit, a line or two at a time, addressed to superiors he never sent them to. *No seal of wax weighs anything at the Weighing. You have sold the poor a bridge that is not yours.* When the story of the dead Primarch at his desk reached the Weiss in the summer of 1289, the notes grew longer.

## The Blackwater funerals

In the late spring of 1290 the Weiss came down in a thaw-flood, took the bridge at Aldersbruck and broke into the Blackwater pit at Brenholt on the night shift. Forty-one miners drowned, eleven of them boys. Men were alive behind the water in the upper gallery, and the pithead could hear them knocking. Ansgar went down the ladderway with the rescue party to sing for them, since nobody could reach them with anything else, and at the pithead he gave his psalter to the tally-keeper, a young woman named **Irmel**, and told her to keep his count. The second inrush took the ladderway. Nobody who had gone down came up, and the Blackwater's drowned went out by the adit into the Weiss.

In the morning the House's letter-seller set up his trestle at the pithead with the printed table of rates, as the rule required, and the widows queued. He was Ansgar's father. Irmel stood at the board where the shifts were counted and read the forty-one names off it, and then the chaplain's, and then she opened the psalter. She read the names in the margins, the dead he had sung for nothing, and the notes beside them, and the queue turned from the trestle to listen. The crowd answered each note with Gerlind's saying: *named, not bought*. She chalked the notes on the shift board as she read. A hundred fitted, and the Weiss country has called them the *[[The Brenholt Margins — Selections|Brenholt Margins]]* ever since. They name the dead Primarch eleven times, and the last of them is a challenge he never delivered: *prove me wrong with Fire, fathers, and not with fees.* The letter-seller folded his table before noon. The parish book records his burial two winters later, unbought.

The Grievances went down the Weiss as a song before anyone printed them. Bargemen sang them at the locks and carters at the inns. Across the upper Weiss country families began to bury their dead the Brenholt way, with the names said aloud by the people who had known them, a hymn and no letter. Women led most of these funerals, because the first one had been a widows' funeral and because the men were underground. The Sending Houses called them **unbought funerals** and the courts called them unlicensed rites. By 1298 the Houses of the upper Weiss had no buyers, and their chaplains had been stoned out of three towns. The Ironvale printing of the Grievances, made about 1299 from the tally-board copy, carried them further than the song. The movement had a board, a song and a dead man's book, and for more than twenty years it had no doctrine and nobody to speak for it.

## Aldersbruck

For nine years no legate came to the upper Weiss. Until 1295 the Sanctum's legates were on the Caldrian frontier, where the relic court was pressing its claim in the War of the Crimson Banner, and the Chancery, whose master registers lay in the Medori vault, could not tell from its own books which Houses had stopped selling. In 1299, at the demand of the dukes of the Weiss and the starving Houses, a synod met at Aldersbruck, the bridge-town below Brenholt, under the legate Tiburce Vaudrey. It could not try a drowned man, so it tried his book. Irmel was made to surrender the psalter, and the synod took up the last note in its margins. By old relic-court custom the fire is put to the book and not to the man. The psalter was laid on a brazier in the chapel on the town pier of the broken bridge, before the synod, Duke Ortwin of Ironvale and as much of Brenholt as could crowd the bank. It did not burn.

The legate declared the trial void for a draught and damp vellum and sealed the psalter in lead. He then ruled on the funerals. Every unbought funeral in the upper Weiss was an unlicensed rite, and each was to be sung again by a Sending House at the family's cost. The Weiss country called this the **second fee**. Irmel was flogged at the bridgehead for keeping a dead priest's book from his superiors.

The synod's junior notary, **Lucian Ferro**, a twenty-year-old law clerk from the Latinate south, wrote all of it down: the brazier, the unburnt book, the void ruling, the fee and the flogging. His record had been copied before anyone thought to stop it.

The Sanctum vaulted the Unburnt Psalter in lead and has denied it exists ever since. The reach of the Weiss below the broken bridge, where the Blackwater adit empties its grey water, became the **Preacher's Water**. Oaths sworn on it hold strangely well. Ansgar was never tried and never condemned, and each party has its own account of him. Freeflame congregations say the House sent him down alone and sold letters over him in the morning. Some old-Church teachers say he died the Sanctum's own priest at his post, and that the Church has spent sixty years prosecuting a book and never its author. The Weiss bargemen say the river took back a man who had named its drowned. In the hill parishes above Brenholt people still say he came out by the adit, and that the nameless friar who sometimes sings at a mine-head funeral is him.

## The Ashenmen Risings, 1306–1329

For five winters the Houses' bailiffs distrained for the second fee: a cow, a loom, a set of picks, for a rite over dead who already lay still. Few families paid. In the autumn of 1304 the Chancery, short of coin with its Houses empty, sold the upper Weiss's unpaid fees to brokers in Vorthara, and the brokers' hired pikes spent the next year collecting them.

In the spring of 1306 miners and farmers from Brenholt and the upper Weiss waded into the river at the broken bridge, smeared their faces with the grey silt the iron-washings leave in it, and swore to fight until the last false seal was burned. Their enemies called them the Ashenmen, after the plague's ash-bearers; they kept the name for the grey on their faces.

They fought out of the ground. The Ashenmen held the pits and drifts of the upper Weiss, which they knew and their enemies did not. By night they came up to raid Sending Houses and church estates, carried the letter-chests down the shafts and burned the seals in the deep galleries. At Brenholt they hanged the brokers' collectors and the Sending House's chaplains from the pithead winding gear. They smashed images of the Mother across the iron country, a guilt the movement's second generation still handles badly: reformed congregations keep her feast under other names. Old-Church armies burned the villages above the pits and could not dig out the men below them. In 1314 Caldrian engineers turned the Weiss into the Brenholt drifts, and about three hundred people drowned in the dark, women and children among them, who had been sheltering there. The Ashenmen came up from other shafts within the month.

The oath held. In twenty-three years no Ashenmen band is recorded breaking its word, deserting or selling out a neighbour. They were impossible to bargain with, because their oath named an end no prince would grant, and they starved the iron country: from 1306 the mines of the upper Weiss sent almost nothing to the forges. Bond-labour, tithe, the second fee and old grudges kept the bands full.

### Sarnwell

In the Sarn valley an Ashenmen band of eleven had broken every image they found and thrown the village's painted limewood Mother down the washing-well, which went dry that summer. In 1311 the eleven came up at dusk to water at it and found a woman standing at the kerb with the pieces of the image in her arms. By their account she said nothing. She set the pieces on the kerb one at a time, and the well filled and ran over the washing-stones. The first person healed at **Sarnwell** was a crippled washerwoman of the village, and the registry of cures that the shrine has kept since is the old Church's proudest modern archive.

The eleven swore to what they had seen with their hands in the Weiss. In 1313 a relic court took their depositions through a grille, under a safe-conduct both sides kept. The Adversary's Counsel argued that heretics in arms could not be witnesses. The court answered that it had never been offered better ones: eleven enemies of every image in the valley, under an oath that no Ashenman had yet broken, swearing against their own cause. The spring has passed every test devised since. The old Church reads Sarnwell as a rebuke to the image-breakers, and reformed preachers have called it bait. The eleven went back down the pit and fought for sixteen more years. They broke no more images.

## Ferro and the Open Flame

Ferro spent his life in the south. After Aldersbruck he became a canonist at the university of Veridance, and about 1309 the Sanctum, which needed the Brenholt funerals answered in words the Weiss country would read, commissioned him to set out their teaching in full and then refute it. Nobody had yet written that teaching down. About 1313 he delivered the first half, *The Order of the Open Flame*, and it states the case with a fairness nobody in the Sanctum had wanted. The Lord's Flame kindles wherever real sanctity gathers, *the Fire keeps no landlord*, and so no hierarchy can mint it or sell it. Congregations are governed by tested elders. They read scripture aloud, cast hymns together, refuse the assurance trade and keep relic devotion to a minimum, and the congregation sings the Sending for anyone who dies among them, whatever the dead could pay, on the precedents of Gerlind and of the Ferry Rule, which in the plague years had let any baptised person sing the funeral where no priest lived. The towns of the unbought funerals read the book and found that it said what they had been doing. They adopted it whole as their order, and so the Reformation's doctrine was written by its prosecutor more than twenty years after its first funeral.

The refutation never came. Ferro said for twenty-six years that he was still writing it. The Sanctum suspended his licence to teach in 1314 and never tried him, because a trial would have meant reading his book aloud.

## The Council of Veridance, 1319–1324

By 1317 the old Church could see its difficulty from any steeple. In the Weiss country, where families had sung their own dead for twenty-seven years, the yards lay quiet. In the loyal south, where the seal had sufficed since 1283, the dead were walking again. **Lord Primarch Celestin IX** (r. 1317–1354), elected at thirty-eight after three older cardinals had refused the chair, convened the **Council of Veridance** in 1319, in the university town where Ferro lived. Its first act was to summon him to deliver the refutation he had owed for six years. Ferro, forty years old, came to the hall and told the fathers that he could not refute the parts of his book that were true, and offered to show them which parts those were. The Council kept him. For five years he sat by the stove in the guild-hall of Veridance as its witness, never voting, and the Council's decrees are a line-by-line answer to *The Order of the Open Flame* with its author in the room.

It abolished assurance sales and repudiated Alessior's Sufficient Seal. Without the seal, every letter sold or given and never sung was a debt to a named soul, and in 1320 the Council ordered every one of them performed: **the Great Arrears**. The registers that said which names were owed had been in the Medori vault since 1289, held as surety for Alessior's debts, and the Council had to buy copies sheet by sheet.

It also needed singers, and the only people in Aurelia who had been paying the Church's debt were the night-singers of Saint-Leon, whom the Sanctum had hunted for forty years. Their priest, **Father Anselin of Saint-Leon**, had been carried out of the burning quarter in 1285 as a child of two and had studied under Ferro at Veridance. The Inquisitors of Saint Malveris took him at a night funeral in 1314, and he died in one of their prison-houses the next winter without a trial. The Council gave his brotherhood a licence to sing the Arrears in 1320, and for four years its singers walked haunted parishes at night singing names out of the old registers. In 1324, at its last session, the Council chartered them as the **Order of the Lantern**, set rectors from among Ferro's students over them, and placed the Malverine prisons under the new order's review. The Lantern's first great case proved the Saint-Leon apparition staged. Freeflame congregations call the order a stolen choir. The Order has never been told which house Anselin died in.

The Council rebuilt seminary education, teaching the reformers' books accurately so that clergy could answer them, and in its last year codified the **five tests** of sainthood, drafted around Sarnwell. Veridun noticed that none of the five can be applied to a saint who left no remains, which rules out Caedros I. The Council closed in 1324. It had answered almost every charge in the psalter's margins, and it had done so thirty-four years after they were read out.

## The Arrears-levy and the Burning of the Seals

The reform carried a bill. The sheets had to be bought from the Medori and the singers fed, and in 1326 Celestin laid the **Arrears-levy** on every diocese. Two years later he sent Lantern singers up the Weiss to sing again, for certainty, the dead of nearly forty years of unbought funerals. The iron country had heard that offer before, at Aldersbruck.

**Rainald**, Duke of Ironvale since 1309, was an old-Church prince who had fought the Ashenmen in his own mines for twenty years while his forges stood cold. The Council's auditors brought him the levy for his duchy and, with it, the Arrears roll for his own house. His father Ortwin had bought a household bond for three generations, and no rite had ever been sung for it. In 1329 Rainald seized the chests of every Sending House in Ironvale and burned their sealed letters in Brenholt market square, saying that Ironvale's dead were sung and Ironvale owed nothing. The Ashenmen of Ironvale held their oath fulfilled, came up out of the pits and took service under his banner, and the iron came up with them.

In 1330 Rainald invited Ferro to Ironvale. Ferro refused: *I wrote their case. I did not promise to plead it.* The reformed towns built on his book became famously orderly, literate and merciless without him. Freeflame elders call him theirs. Lantern teachers call him the Church's most honest servant. Veridun's chroniclers say he proves that an Eclessian lawyer cannot refuse a brief. He died at Veridance in 1339, and the university buried him in its own yard, which the commissioners of the Accord later found they could assign to neither rite.

## The princes' war and the end of a ducal house

From 1329 the war belonged to princes, and it was fought over who should sing the Arrears and who should pay for them. Rainald armed the heirs of the Risings with the iron they had brought up, kept the levy and took the Sending Houses' endowments to sing Ironvale's dead by Ferro's book. His neighbours along the Weiss did the same or marched against him for doing it. Caldria's campaigns of 1310–1324 and Aurelia's trade war of 1310–1313 against Freeflame-linked merchants had already shown how quickly doctrine became a land war. Princes on both sides took Church property they had wanted for generations. Towns changed hands and rites with them, and families buried their dead under whichever lamp held the churchyard that month. In 1338 a forged no-quarter order turned a prisoner exchange between Freeflame and old-Church companies into a massacre.

In 1340 the Council of Heldenheim tried to keep Korrheim out of the war by assigning each canton's churches by the vote of its council. Falkendorf's council was old-Church by one vote and its people were reformed, and the ruling gave the canton's one churchyard to the old rite and ordered forty years of unbought graves moved outside the wall. Falkendorf rose sooner than dig up its dead. Ironvale sent pikes, and Rainald's last son was killed at the yard gate. The canton companies sent to put the rising down halted at the wall, where the women of Falkendorf were sitting on the graves. Their captains were Korrheimers with kin on both sides, and their war-priest Father Gregor wrote them a set of burial articles in place of an assault: a yard stays with the rite that has kept its dead sent, and nobody's dead are moved. The Accord was built on those articles three years later.

Rainald died in 1341 without an heir. His house had spent its blood, its credit and its standing with the Church, and Ironvale's guild ministries chartered the Grand Ministry to govern in its place.

## What it left

The unbought funerals found a real fraud against the dead and made villages sing their own Sendings again. Reformed congregations taught farm children to read the Grievances aloud and disciplined corrupt clergy in front of them. The movement also hanged chaplains from winding gear, let Rainald and his neighbours strip Church estates they had wanted for generations, broke the Mother's images and the people who kept them, and raised children who called every grandmother's custom a corruption. Old-Church writers point out that the Freeflame's most famous object is a river, its most famous saint is a woman they forbid anyone to pray to, and its order was written by the Sanctum's own lawyer. Freeflame writers answer that the Sanctum's most famous object is a book it says it does not have, and that its reforming order is a choir it burned out and then hired.

The woman who read the first funeral lived to see the settlement and stood outside it. Irmel kept the Brenholt board through the war and never took Ferro's book for her rule. Her people gave their dead to the Weiss below the broken bridge, to lie with the forty-one, and kept no yard. When the princes waded into the river in 1343 she watched from the bank, and the saying remembered from her is *the dead are named, not counted.* She died the next year and was put into the Preacher's Water. Reformed elders honour her and do not let her followers preach.

## The frozen settlement

The [[The Korrheim Accord|Korrheim Accord]] of 1343, sworn on the Preacher's Water, ended open war by giving every parish yard to the rite that had kept its dead sent and every lord the lamp of his land's yards. Congregations that kept no yard were left outside it. Conscience, mixed families and border parishes were left unresolved.

## Present-day fault lines

- Ironvale is the strongest Freeflame polity, governed by guild ministers.
- Veridancia trains both old-Church reformers and some of their most effective opponents.
- Virelia's elected crown has no royal graves of its own, and every election reopens the question of whose lamp the crown lands stand under.
- Grenzburg is formally old Church but exposed to migrants, pamphlets and reform-minded labourers.
- The Great Arrears are still being sung, from registers the Medori hold.
- Dead paper still circulates, and brokers still press claims on the named dead.
- The Hollow Flame exploits every abuse while offering severance rather than reform.
