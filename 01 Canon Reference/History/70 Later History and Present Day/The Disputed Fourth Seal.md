# The Disputed Fourth Seal

The Disputed Fourth Seal is the succession quarrel that has divided the Covenant since the death of its prophet. Mujalib gave his authority away in his lifetime under three sealed instruments and died in 596 AR without publicly naming anybody to stand over their holders. Two instruments then appeared, each claiming to be his fourth and last Seal. One named Uthar ibn Qays, captain of the guard of the wells; the other named Husan ibn Raiq, the Prophet's grandson. Uthar became the first caliph. Seven years later Husan's household came under safe conduct to the station well of Bir Qassim, where the well's bound keeper would not draw water for them, and the deaths there turned a quarrel over a document into two faiths.

## The Three Seals and the fourth

Mujalib never ruled under a title. In 563 he closed three instruments with his own witness-mark and gave each to a holder. The **Seal of Water**, with the oasis, its channels and the kept wells, went to the guard of the wells under Uthar ibn Qays. The **Seal of Names**, with the roll and its granaries, went to the college of scribes at Al-Qahur. The **Seal of the Roads**, with the musters, the camel roads and the station wells, went to the marshal Qatada Sayf-al-Ahd. Covenant tradition calls these first holders the Rightly-Sealed, because the Prophet's own hand sealed them. For thirty-three years the three governed side by side, quarrelled over grain and precedence and took the Atherian north, while the old man in the house by the first channel sealed nothing that set one of them above the others.

He died in the winter of 596. In the spring two instruments were produced:

- The scribal college at Al-Qahur produced the **Fourth Seal**, naming Uthar ibn Qays holder over the three and bearing the college's attestation.
- Ruqaiya bint Mujalib's household produced the **Counter-Instrument**, naming her son Husan ibn Raiq and bearing what they held to be Mujalib's true final witness-mark.

The college condemned the Counter-Instrument. Ruqaiya's party condemned the Fourth Seal. Uthar held the wells and had the college's roll behind him, and he took a title made for the occasion: caliph, the holder over the holders. Qatada, the third of the Rightly-Sealed, would acknowledge neither instrument, and in 598 he halted his army in the north until the two could be tried. Uthar took the caliphate; Husan took the desert.

## Bir Qassim, 603 AR

For six years Husan kept to the dry country south of the Basin, among well-houses whose shares his grandfather had sealed in the years of the kept wells. In the summer of 603 Qatada offered him a safe conduct to come north and plead before arbitrators at Iqashar, the marshal's city, and the guard and the college set their seals to it beside the marshal's. The instrument named fifty-eight travellers, Husan, his mother Ruqaiya and their household, and bound them to the North Well Road by Qafra, Bir Nadim and Bir Qassim and no other way. The camel-men and drovers the household hired were not named in it. A company of Qatada's road-guards, veterans of the northern war, rode as escort.

A flash flood took the causeway at Bir Nadim, and the escort led the travellers six miles round by a drover's track before it came back to the named road above Bir Qassim. Every station well on that road was kept by one of the bound offices of the 4,444, which lowered and raised the bucket for those who travelled under the Covenant's protection, a skin for each traveller and three for each camel. At Bir Qassim the keeper drew for the escort and for the unnamed drovers. For the fifty-eight it would not draw: their bucket came up out of the dark wet and empty, and the rope ran back through the hands of whoever held it.

The escort's captain sent one rider to the travelling court at Qafra to ask whether the protection still stood, and another north to the marshal. The household waited in the well-yard in the heat. The soldiers shared their skins, and the drovers gave the children their camels' measure and let the camels go dry. On the fourth day the court's answer came, with a bailiff to see it carried out. Its presiding jurist, **Jarud ibn Sulayt**, ruled the protection void because the route was an essential term. From that hour the travellers were rebels under arms, to be disarmed and held, and water given to them was aid to rebels. The bailiff stopped the soldiers' skins. The drovers went on passing theirs across the yard at night. By the sixth evening twelve of the household had died of thirst, the oldest and the youngest among them.

That night Husan stood at the well-mouth with the instrument and read it aloud, clause by clause. After each clause his people let down the bucket, and after each clause it came up dry.

At dawn the captain paraded the escort to take the travellers' arms, and the escort would not do it. They had served notices on forty towns of the north, one of their sergeants told the bailiff, and had never been asked to serve one on a well. The captain, who had sent to the court in the first place, went with his men. Nine of the household could no longer sit a camel, and Ruqaiya, who was about sixty-five, was one of them. By the Sealed Blood's account she ordered her son to go, saying that the blood had to outlive the well. Fifteen of the drovers stayed with the nine and with what water they had. The escort put the other thirty-seven on the drovers' camels and took them west along the drover's track towards the strait.

Qatada was already on the Basin road, riding south to meet the travellers he had invited, when two letters reached him within a day of each other: his captain's account of the well, and the caliph's order to arrest the escort and deliver the rebels. He read the order, answered nothing and rode after his men. He caught them in the dry hills, brought them to the strait and saw Husan into a Qazmari boat.

A column of the guard of the wells came down the road to Bir Qassim four days after the escort had gone. Ruqaiya and three others were already dead. The column's commander carried Jarud's ruling and applied it. His men killed the five of the household who were still alive, as rebels, and fourteen of the drovers, as people who had given water to rebels and whom no instrument named. A camel-boy hid among the animals and carried the count west. The commander had the graves dug and paid for five stones, a fact the Mourning remembers with contempt rather than gratitude. The mourning houses later paid for thirty more, and they spent a generation going from village to village to learn the drovers' names, which nobody had written down.

Within a year the well was dry for everyone. Mourners say it went dry on the morning of the killing; Witnessed-Seal registers record that the college at Al-Qahur recalled the keeper from Bir Qassim to stop pilgrims drawing from it, and that the unkept shaft silted. It has given no water since.

## The marshal and the veiled heir

Qatada rode back from the strait to answer the college's summons and died on the road in the spring of 604. Witnessed-Seal registers give a fever at Qafra. The Sealed Blood says he would drink at no kept well after Bir Qassim, and that the road north has no other kind. The Seal of the Roads passed into the college's keeping, and no marshal held it again until Qahir ibn Ruzbeh, nearly three hundred years later.

Husan lived twenty-eight years more at Qazmarid and was never again seen unveiled outside his household. The Sealed Blood has never agreed whether he veiled himself by vow or for shame at having left his mother beside a dry well. He died there in 631. The guides who have led the Sealed Blood since go veiled before strangers and are called the Veiled Wasiyin.

The annual **Mourning of the Wells** repeats Husan's reading. It recites the safe-conduct clause by clause and answers each with: *And yet the well was dry.* Then the thirty-five names are read, Ruqaiya's first and the drovers' last. On that day Sealed-Blood households set a filled skin outside the door for whoever passes and water their animals last, a custom they call the drovers' measure. Witnessed-Seal registers count five dead at Bir Qassim, the rebels killed under a lawful ruling. The mourning houses count thirty-five. They say the thirst killed as surely as the swords, and that the drovers died for the one righteous act done at that well.

## The Seal on the Frontier

In 597 the Covenant's armies were halfway through the conquest of the Atherian north, and from that year every breach-court that voided a town's charter did so in the caliph's name. The towns had an answer. Defenders shouted it from their walls and lawyers wrote it into their pleadings: *your court sits under a seal nobody may open.* Sealed-Blood jurists refused to sit in Uthar's name. Qatada's halt of 598 rested on the same argument, and with it the field advance stopped. Before the march town of Tamsar in 611 a Sealed-Blood jurist climbed onto a wagon and told the Covenant army that its court sat under a seal nobody dared open. Neither army gave battle, and the peace of 612 followed.

The Velian cities were served under Uthar's seal by the commanders of the Banu Mizan, and their pre-conquest charters still wait in fireproof chests. If the Fourth Seal were ever proved forged, every judgement made in Uthar's name would be open to challenge, the Velian ones among them. Qahirid jurists count that among the reasons the Seal stays shut.

## The Divide

[[The Witnessed Seal]] teaches that authority continues through public attestation and the community's jurists. [[The Sealed Blood]] teaches that succession continued through Ruqaiya, Husan and the Veiled Wasiyin. Bir Qassim gave each side its lesson. Witnessed-Seal schools learned that an instrument can be valid and wicked, and their penitential jurists teach it as **lawful and wrong**; their formalists add that the escort mutinied and the marshal deserted. The Sealed Blood learned that the Covenant's courts and its bound offices will obey an instrument over the Prophet's own blood, and that the people who gave water were on no roll. Sealed-Blood villages still draw their water by hand, let no bound office keep their wells and serve the stranger first.

Both instruments reportedly survive. The Fourth Seal rests in the Vault of Names at Al-Qahur, in the Deep Vault, which since 1059 has been a separate house from the Living Register at Iqashar; Qazmarid claims the Counter-Instrument lies beneath the Caliph-Wasi's throne. Opening either for forensic examination would void the succession it might prove. The dispute is therefore decidable in principle and undecidable in practice.

## Present Day

Iqashar and Al-Qahur are Witnessed-Seal. Qazmarid is Sealed-Blood. Zanthir is Witnessed-majority with protected Sealed-Blood communities. The three Seals still have their heirs: the caliph at Al-Qahur keeps the wells, the Unnamed Custodian keeps the names, and the Sultan-Caliph at Iqashar holds the roads and claims the fourth. Every Qahirid succession reopens the wound, and the Cull of Brothers that awaits Marzuq III's death will reopen it again.
