# The Empty Seat Facing Outward

**The Empty Seat Facing Outward** remembers the crisis of movement and authority in the principal outward camp after scouts found the lands beyond the Glass already held by Giant-blooded powers, fortified nations and frightening kingdoms. Its empty chair belongs to later storytelling; the historical dispute concerned how the Prophet's last commissioned scout-captain should guide a people whose leaders had no right to inherit the Prophet's revelatory office.

## Historical setting

**c. 2165–2130 BR.** outward threshold of the Glass. This tradition belongs to [[Age of Scattering]]; its cultural bearers are Ark-derived and neighbouring Orphaned communities.

It is told among Jirahar, Ahdari and southern Ark-descended peoples remembering the Prophet's death. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## Historical basis

Before his death, the Prophet personally commissioned an experienced scout-captain to reconnoitre the inhabited country beyond the Glass and guide the first safe movement into it. The mandate concerned routes and protection. It granted neither revelation, universal government nor a divine title. Other commanders, judges, provisioners and household leaders accepted the danger reported by the scouts while disputing the timing and method of departure.

The captain refused both permanent encampment and a single war of conquest. He led repeated circuits: scouting, escorting groups outward and returning for those still waiting. This mobile discipline became an institutional and devotional ancestor of later Jirahar road companies, portable courts and Keepers, although he was not the first leader of a finished Jirahar people. A rival commander instead used the same routes and the fear of delay to justify the grain requisitions remembered in [[G-71 - The Five Staffs and the Empty Granary|The Five Staffs and the Empty Granary]].

The southern and Near-Glass populations separated gradually through these choices. The chair, the formal attempt to fill it and the three-day council are later ways of compressing a real dispute which extended across several seasons.

## The tale

When the Prophet died, the people waited beside his empty seat for someone to rise and speak as he had spoken. The commissioned scout held his route staff. The provisioner held the keys. The judge held the disputes. The archivist held the remembered words. The household elders held the obedience of families. Each office was real. None contained the commission that had died with the man.

For three days factions tried to place one holder in the seat. Soldiers threatened the judges. Families hid stores from the provisioners. A singer recited the Prophet's last rebuke—that no child of the camp should pretend to own the Voice—and mourners overturned the chair before anybody could sit.

The outward scouts then described walled nations, Giant-blooded champions and kingdoms stronger than any single camp. Some leaders urged immediate conquest. Others would not leave the shelter they understood. The scout-captain repeated the narrower words of his commission: he would find roads, guard those who travelled and return for those prepared to follow.

The crisis did not end. The captain's company kept moving between road and camp, while southern war leaders argued that the people must seize gates and farmland before other powers did. Near-Glass households wanted to remain close to the refuge and carry its practices onward. Families split slowly. Offices travelled together for a generation and then learned to function differently in different landscapes.

The empty seat therefore marks no single council that divided three nations. It marks the loss from which many later disagreements grew.

In one remembered winter, a soldier sat there and declared that the Prophet had named him privately. His own mother pulled him up by the ear and made him repeat the true commission he held: guarding the west ditch until dawn. The laughter ended the claim but not the hunger for a successor. He guarded the ditch.

## Why it is remembered

The story protects the Prophet from being converted into a dynasty. It also admits the terror of having to act when no successor can remove responsibility from ordinary leaders.

## How tellers differ

Jirahar seats face the remembered road. Ahdari seats face defended water. Some Thalmyric memorial seats face south toward conquest. Each orientation claims fidelity without proving possession.

## What remains

An empty place appears in several unrelated assemblies whenever someone claims authority no office can inherit.
