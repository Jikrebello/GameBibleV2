# The Threshing Floor beyond the Count

## Historical setting

**c. 1171–1173 AR; High Noon.** Elenara, a tributary river principality of the successor-imperial world, in the years between the golden age of Caedros III and Qarath; the threshing floor is at Kremna. This tradition belongs to [[High Noon and the Qarath Disaster]]; its cultural bearers are Thalmyric, Eclessian and Sestaran tellers.

It is told in High-Noon harvest districts where a useful census became a route for soldiers and death.

## The tale

A river prince ordered every household counted so grain could be moved before drought. The count worked. Storehouses opened, roads were repaired and distant villages received seed. Then the prince secretly marked on the same tally the households whose sons he wanted for road labour, the refugees he meant to tax and the doubtful families from which he would take hostages. A complete register, his counsellors told him, would prove his principality fit to be a kingdom.

Soldiers followed the counters. They seized sons, broke doors and killed resisters. Families hid children beneath grain, but the marks led patrols to every barn. Then death came down the census roads. Within hours of each sealed schedule reaching a counting-house, the clerks, the soldiers in the nearest barracks and the households around them fell sick with the same fever and the same failing breath. People along the courier roads looked up and saw something above the leading edge of the dying: a dark raptor-shape as wide as a storm, filled with white fire, moving at the pace of a courier's horse. It struck officers and peasants, nobles and children without distinction.

At the edge of one village, an old woman whose sons the census had already taken continued threshing after everyone else fled. The prince had by then confessed the census's true purpose in the market square of his own capital, freed the surviving hostages and burned the seizure schedule, and he came to her floor barefoot. The mortality reached her floor and stopped. Some say the blazing shape stood over the falling grain and folded its wings. Others say the troops had already refused orders after seeing their own children sick. The old woman simply swept a circle clean and poured the household counters into the chaff.

The prince ended the seizures, though his chancery later blamed the clerks for misusing his command, and his bid for a crown died with the census. Survivors forced every later count to spill grain outside the total: a visible portion belonging to people whom no number could make available for seizure.

The old woman refused a court reward and asked instead for the marked doors to be repaired. Some had no survivors to reopen them. Their unclaimed grain was given away without adding the households to another list. The empty thresholds remained, making the cost visible after the food was gone. Nobody counted those doors again.

## Why it is remembered

The tale turns a mundane good—knowing how many mouths need food—into dread as the same knowledge guides violence. The humble stopping place matters as much as the wonder because a worker makes the limit visible.

## How tellers differ

Court histories call the deaths a fever and the prince the saviour who ended it. Devotional versions name an angel of judgement sent down the couriers' roads, which stopped at the floor when its errand was done. Victims' families insist the prince confessed only after mass death, and harvest songs say exhausted soldiers saw the woman and disobeyed. Physicians separate the fever from the later Grey Sweat by its season and symptoms. None acquits the prince's second use of the count.

## What remains

After communal threshing, one circle of grain is left uncounted and distributed by hand. Law codes across the successor world require a census to state its purpose and forbid its later use for hostages or labour seizure without a new public act. Jurists call the limit "the floor beyond the count."
