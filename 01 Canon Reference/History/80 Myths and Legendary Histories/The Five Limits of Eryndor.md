# The Five Limits of Eryndor

**The Five Limits of Eryndor** is the collective name for several families of stories about the Far-Road King. They recount the spring under the sea at Terys, the lions that followed his army, the gilded faces that spoke with his voice, the wood that closed behind him and the water that would not travel. A refusal ends the water story: the woman who kept the spring would not leave it for a king. Delkosi and Qahuri tellers add a sixth limit, the one he tried to cross by foreign art: his own death.

The stories developed in Ortheian, Qahuri, Arqeshite, Vashari, Delkosi, Jirahar and later Atherian traditions. They share unusual incidents known to have occurred during the eastern campaign, but their explanations and sequence differ. Together they turn a successful conqueror into a man repeatedly shown the boundaries of command.

## The spring under the sea

The island sanctuary of Terys drinks from a spring that rises under the sea, drawn up through a bronze bell. In the Ortheian royal cycle Eryndor goes down in the bell himself. On the sea floor he finds the spring's keeper waiting in the shape of a woman with water for hair, and he is her guest for one night. He comes up with a ring and the place of the spring, and Terys falls. Qahuri versions make the keeper refuse him and the spring turn salt at his touch.

Terysian laments leave the king on the shore. They name the divers forced down in the besiegers' bell, the ones who drowned when something struck it, and the mainland diver who sold the spring for his family's freedom. The historical siege found and blocked the spring, forced captured divers to work a bell and ended with the massacre and enslavement of the island's people. The spring has run salt ever since. What struck the bell remains unknown.

## The lions who followed

In the Ruzhan cliffs the king's grooms take eggs from the nests of Sky-Lions to raise mounts for him. The pride follows the army east and back, stooping on the pack trains at every pass. The upland royal cycle ends the story in the air: the grown lions carry Eryndor up on a harnessed frame, and he sees the World Egg entire and receives its horizon as his inheritance. Qahuri versions say that Father Sun permitted him one view so that he would understand how little of the earth he possessed.

Vashari herders end the story on a ledge, where the eggs are given back and the lions go home. The oldest accounts describe the theft, the pursuit and the drovers and handlers the lions killed. The flight appears only in court poetry written for later Ortheian kings.

## The faces that answered

Kassur III flees his fallen capital with his gilded images, and one night in the reed country the images speak with the voice of the king pursuing him. Their bearers carry them across the lines, and the river king is left without a face. In the Arqeshite telling the images go over because Kassur's own people had stopped bowing to him; in the Ortheian, because heaven had chosen. The Vashari version, told at Kavresh where Kassur died, follows the old king instead, into an orchard house where he would not look at his reflection.

Later tellers add a second scene in Eryndor's hall at Arqesh. The king hears his own voice from Kassur's face. A companion who mocks it in the same upland accent dies under the king's hand, and the Ortheian officers win the right to stand. Arqeshite laments end the scene with the images, which outlive both kings in the dark.

## The wood that closed

A grove of Root-Minds answers through a village interpreter and asks a price for passage: the terraces along the valley road must go back to the wood. Eryndor will not give up ground he has taken. He fells the speaking trees and marches through, and the wood closes behind him. Roots cross the cut, cart spokes come into leaf, and the road home becomes a different valley each morning. He cuts his way out and leaves prisoners, villages and garrisons on the far side.

Arqeshite laments make the falling trees recite the names of destroyed cities and deported families. Vashari accounts place a queen and a company of ascetics beside the grove, where they rebuke a king who expects the land to obey rank. Imperial romances enlarge the closed wood into a forest around the edge of the world. Local tradition remembers one valley, one closed road and villages left beyond the trees, and its tellers still dispute whether the villages were abandoned or spared. The oldest versions agree that the grove asked for land and the king refused it.

## The water that would not travel

On the road back a Vashari guide brings the wounded king to a seep in a lightless gorge. The water mends his flesh while he stays. The woman who keeps the spring tells him that it mends only those who stay beside it. He stays a season, and Parena comes up from the river to be with him. Then the couriers find him, and he goes, and the wound opens on the road. He offers the keeper gold to come west with a jar of the water. She refuses, and the jar goes stale in a day.

Jirahar households tell this story before feeding the last person to arrive at a journey's end. Their version gives the king one line and the keeper the rest. A Jirahar guide goes down the gorge to find where the water rises and never comes back, and the keeper outlives everyone in the story. Road-house versions make her refusal the only answer in the cycle given entirely by an ordinary human being. It succeeds where sea, sky, face and wood merely reveal a limit.

Some royal performances send Eryndor home from the gorge because the spirit of Valeron calls from the west. Olyssaran exiles answer that Valeron was killed at a pay table by his own soldiers and had nothing to call his son home to.

## The voice in the tomb

The newest family of stories follows the king past his death. Wounded and afraid, Eryndor sends for the embalmers of Qemaret, to whose dead mistress he had pledged his body for a loan of grain ten years before, and submits to their discipline: undyed linen, fasting, draughts of gold and salts. The rite is meant to take years; he has months. He dies aboard ship at Nemeru, waiting for a wind home, and the masters close his name into the tomb in a ship's hold. At Delkos the corpse speaks behind a curtain, calls itself the living king, disowns his daughter and gives away the world a second time. Years later the same masters stand before the courts and say that the rite failed, and the tomb is walled up with the voice inside.

Delkosi versions end with the king still reigning behind his curtain, as the veterans believed. Qahuri tellers make it a tale against their own art: the voice is a perfect likeness that knows every secret of the camp and is nobody. Arqeshite laments give the last word to Kassur's gilded images, which had learned the voice first. Ortheian court singers leave the episode out and say the body was stolen. Road-house tellers call it the only limit Eryndor never admitted reaching.

## How the traditions differ

Ortheian singers celebrate a daring king and omit Olyssar. Qahuri priests present mortality as the limit of every foreign title and the loan of 695 as a debt the dead collected. Arqeshite laments make conquest answer to the dead. Vashari storytellers centre rulers, ascetics and keepers who cannot be impressed. Jirahar travellers give the water to an ordinary woman. Later Atherian writers turn the spring and the closed wood into proofs of engineering genius, while dissident copies restore the forced divers and the villages left behind.

Later Redeemer-faith retellings sometimes place a saint beside the water or convert Eryndor before death. These are devotional adaptations. The pre-Redeemer king was never numbered among the saints.

## What remains

The historical campaign contained a blocked undersea spring at Terys, a stolen clutch of Sky-Lion eggs and the pride that followed, the images that went over to Eryndor in 688 BR, a grove that closed a road behind the army and a seep in a gorge that healed only those who stayed. No surviving account describes all five incidents reliably. The sixth story rests on better witness: Eryndor died aboard ship at Nemeru in 684, his embalmed body was carried to Delkos, a voice in his tomb claimed to be the king until the Qemaret masters testified against it around 657 BR, and the tomb was walled up soon afterwards. Its site is lost.
