# The Five Limits of Eryndor

**The Five Limits of Eryndor** is the collective name for several families of stories about the Far-Road King. They recount the grain a dead woman lent him, the lions that followed his army, the gilded faces that learned to mock him, the wood that closed behind him and the water that would not travel. A refusal ends the water story: the woman who kept the spring would not leave it for a king. Delkosi and Qahuri tellers add a sixth limit, the one he tried to cross by foreign art: his own death.

The stories developed in Ortheian, Qahuri, Arqeshite, Vashari, Delkosi, Jirahar and later Atherian traditions. They share unusual incidents known to have occurred during the eastern campaign, but their explanations and sequence differ. Together they turn a successful conqueror into a man repeatedly shown the boundaries of command.

## The grain that was lent

A king who has emptied every granary on the coast comes into the Sacred Basin with a hungry army, and the three crowns shut their stores. The only doors that open belong to a woman four centuries dead. In the Qemaret telling her stewards walk Eryndor through store courts where grain is measured out by lamplight, and a voice from the inner house asks what he will leave with her. He offers silver, cities and a share of Arqesh. She asks for him: the right of her masters to make his body ready when he dies. He is thirty, and he laughs, and the grain goes up the river.

Ortheian court singers make the grain a gift from a queen who admired him, and leave out the pledge. Qahuri tellers keep the laugh and count the years from it. The historical army ate Khaseret's grain in 695 BR, garrisoned her store courts as the loan required and marched on Arqesh. Road-house tellers call it the only limit the king ever accepted, and say he accepted it because he took it for a joke.

## The lions who followed

In the Ruzhan cliffs the king's grooms take eggs from the nests of Sky-Lions to raise mounts for him. The pride follows the army east and back, stooping on the pack trains at every pass. The upland royal cycle ends the story in the air: the grown lions carry Eryndor up on a harnessed frame, and he sees the World Egg entire and receives its horizon as his inheritance. Qahuri versions say that Father Sun permitted him one view so that he would understand how little of the earth he possessed.

Vashari herders end the story on a ledge, where the eggs are given back and the lions go home. The oldest accounts describe the theft, the pursuit and the drovers and handlers the lions killed. The flight appears only in court poetry written for later Ortheian kings.

## The faces that answered

Kassur III flees his fallen capital with his gilded images, and one night in the reed country the images speak with the voice of the king pursuing him. Their bearers carry them across the lines, and the river king is left without a face. In the Arqeshite telling the images go over because Kassur's own people had stopped bowing to him; in the Ortheian, because heaven had chosen. The Vashari version, told at Kavresh where Kassur died, follows the old king instead, into an orchard house where he would not look at his reflection.

The second scene is set in Eryndor's hall at Arqesh. The faces that speak with the king's voice order one of his own officers to bow, and the officer dies for refusing. A companion mocks them in the same upland accent, and their bearers strangle him while the king looks on. After that the faces speak with the dead man's voice and call the king by his camp name before every petitioner, and the conqueror of Arqesh rides east to get out of earshot. Ortheian singers leave the companion out. Arqeshite laments end the scene with the images, which outlive both kings in the dark.

## The wood that closed

A grove of Root-Minds answers through a village interpreter and asks a price for passage: the terraces along the valley road must go back to the wood. Eryndor will not give up ground he has taken. He fells the speaking trees and marches through, and the wood closes behind him. Roots cross the cut, cart spokes come into leaf, and the road home becomes a different valley each morning. He cuts his way out and leaves prisoners, villages and garrisons on the far side. The garrisons go on sending him their muster rolls.

Arqeshite laments make the falling trees recite the names of destroyed cities and deported families. Vashari accounts place a queen and a company of ascetics beside the grove, where they rebuke a king who expects the land to obey rank. Imperial romances enlarge the closed wood into a forest around the edge of the world. Local tradition remembers one valley, one closed road and villages left beyond the trees, and its tellers still dispute whether the villages were abandoned or spared. The oldest versions agree that the grove asked for land and the king refused it.

## The water that would not travel

On the road back a Vashari guide brings the wounded king to a seep in a lightless gorge. The water mends his flesh while he stays. The woman who keeps the spring tells him that it mends only those who stay beside it. He stays four years, and Parena comes up from the river with their daughter to be with him. Then the couriers find him, and he goes, and the wound opens on the road. He offers the keeper gold to come west with a jar of the water. She refuses, and the jar goes stale in a day.

Jirahar households tell this story before feeding the last person to arrive at a journey's end. Their version gives the king one line and the keeper the rest. A Jirahar guide goes down the gorge to find where the water rises and never comes back, and the keeper outlives everyone in the story. Road-house versions make her refusal the only answer in the cycle given entirely by an ordinary human being. It succeeds where the lender, the sky, the faces and the wood merely reveal a limit.

Some royal performances send Eryndor home from the gorge because the spirit of Valeron calls from the west. Marenthan exiles answer that Valeron was killed at a pay table by his own soldiers and had nothing to call his son home to.

## The voice in the tomb

The newest family of stories follows the king past his death. Wounded and afraid, Eryndor sends for the embalmers of Qemaret, to whose dead mistress he had pledged his body for a loan of grain more than twenty years before, and submits to their discipline: undyed linen, fasting, draughts of gold and salts. The rite is meant to take years; he has months. He dies aboard ship at Nemeru, waiting for a wind home, and the masters close his name into the tomb in a ship's hold. The grandchildren of the Belyra deportees, who crew the ship, cut its cables and carry him to Delkos. There the corpse speaks behind a curtain, calls itself the living king, disowns his daughter and gives away the world a second time. Years later the same masters stand before the courts and say that the rite failed, and the tomb is walled up with the voice inside.

Delkosi versions end with the king still reigning behind his curtain, as the veterans believed. Qahuri tellers make it a tale against their own art: the voice is a perfect likeness that knows every secret of the camp and is nobody. Arqeshite laments give the last word to Kassur's gilded images, which had learned the voice first. Ortheian court singers leave the episode out and say the body was stolen. Road-house tellers call it the only limit Eryndor never admitted reaching.

## How the traditions differ

Ortheian singers celebrate a daring king and omit Marenthe. Qahuri priests present mortality as the limit of every foreign title and the loan of 695 as a debt the dead collected. Arqeshite laments make conquest answer to the dead. Vashari storytellers centre rulers, ascetics and keepers who cannot be impressed. Jirahar travellers give the water to an ordinary woman. Later Atherian writers turn the loan and the closed wood into proofs of a king's daring, while dissident copies restore the pledge and the villages left behind.

Later Redeemer-faith retellings sometimes place a saint beside the water or convert Eryndor before death. These are devotional adaptations. The pre-Redeemer king was never numbered among the saints.

## What remains

The historical campaign contained a loan of grain from a dead financier's house against the borrower's body, a stolen clutch of Sky-Lion eggs and the pride that followed, the images that went over to Eryndor in 688 BR and afterwards mocked him in a dead companion's voice, a grove that closed a road behind the army and a seep in a gorge that healed only those who stayed. No surviving account describes all five incidents reliably. The sixth story rests on better witness: Eryndor died aboard ship at Nemeru in 673, his embalmed body was carried to Delkos, a voice in his tomb claimed to be the king until the Qemaret masters testified against it around 657 BR, and the tomb was walled up soon afterwards. Three island harbours now claim its site.
