# The Five Limits of Eryndor

**The Five Limits of Eryndor** is the collective name for several families of stories about the Far-Road King. They recount his descent beneath the sea, ascent into the upper air, conversation with speaking trees, search for life-giving water and construction of a gate against a surviving horror. A final episode tells how his own soldiers refused to carry him farther east.

The stories developed in Ortheian, Qahuri, Arqeshite, Vashari, Jirahar and later Atherian traditions. They share unusual incidents known to have occurred during the eastern campaign, but their explanations and sequence differ. Together they turn a successful conqueror into a man repeatedly shown the boundaries of command.

## Beneath the sea

At Terys, Eryndor enters an enclosed bell to inspect the foundations of the harbour defences. A creature or power of the deep strikes the bell, attendants drown and the king returns terrified by the weight of water above him. Ortheian singers make him ruler of an undersea court for one night. Terysian laments name the divers and haulers who died while the king obtained a single observation.

The historical siege employed an observation bell and ended with the massacre and enslavement of the harbour's inhabitants. The identity of the deep attacker remains unknown.

## Above the world

Farther east, handlers harness two trained Sky-Lions to a suspended frame. Eryndor rises to see an enemy position beyond the ridges. The creatures panic or tire, and the descent kills attendants and injures the handlers who bring him down.

The upland royal cycle says that the king saw the World Egg entire and received its horizon as an inheritance. Qahuri versions say that Father Sun permitted him one view so that he would understand how little of the earth he possessed. The oldest accounts describe a dangerous reconnaissance flight using living Sky-Lions. Later court poets turned the creatures into messengers of divine adoption.

## The speaking grove

A grove of Root-Minds answers questions through an interpreter. Eryndor asks whether he will conquer the remaining roads and return to Ortheia. The answer gives a place or season of death. He threatens the interpreter, cuts one tree or orders the grove burned; the sentence continues from another trunk.

Arqeshite laments make the trees recite the names of destroyed cities and deported families. Vashari accounts place a queen and a company of ascetics beside the grove, where they rebuke a king who expects knowledge to obey rank. The oldest versions agree that the grove answered one question concerning the king's death. They provide no reliable account of later events or of any authority granted to him by the answer.

## The water in the dark

Eryndor sends a small party along a lightless road in search of water said to restore exhausted flesh. A cook, porter, drover or guide washes a dried fish in a spring and sees it move. In some versions the discoverer drinks and survives beyond an ordinary human life; in others the person disappears into the Egg White. Eryndor cannot retrace the route because he abandoned the slowest travellers or dismissed the guide who knew the turns.

Jirahar households tell this story before feeding the last person to arrive at a journey's end. Their version gives no praise to the expedition. Its meaning lies in the king losing what an overlooked servant found. A restorative seep and a missing traveller belong to the historical expedition; the traveller's identity and fate remain unknown.

## The gate against the enclosed host

During a retreat through an exposed pass, engineers close the road with masonry, metalwork and an existing local boundary. The barrier contains a predatory host long enough for the army to withdraw. Prisoners, villages and soldiers beyond the completed gate are left to die or survive without help.

Later imperial romances enlarge this work into a wall around every monster at the edge of the world. Local tradition remembers one pass, one retreat and villages abandoned beyond the barrier. Its tellers still dispute which side of the gate received protection.

## The refusal

At the end of the cycle, Eryndor demands one more march. Veterans have lost companions, animals and dependants; eastern recruits have already travelled beyond the lands they promised to defend. The soldiers ground their spears and refuse. The king threatens, pleads and offers treasure. Nothing moves them.

Some royal performances send Eryndor home because the spirit of Valeron calls from the west. Road-house versions make the soldiers' refusal the only answer in the cycle given entirely by ordinary human beings. It succeeds where sea, sky, grove, water and gate merely reveal a limit.

## How the traditions differ

Ortheian singers celebrate a daring king and omit Olyssar. Qahuri priests present mortality as the limit of every foreign title. Arqeshite laments make conquest answer to the dead. Vashari storytellers centre rulers and ascetics who cannot be impressed. Jirahar travellers give the water to an unnamed worker. Later Atherian writers turn the bell and gate into proofs of engineering genius, while dissident copies restore the forced labour and dead workers.

Later Christian retellings sometimes place a saint beside the water or convert Eryndor before death. These are devotional adaptations. The pre-Redeemer king was never numbered among the saints.

## What remains

The historical campaign contained an undersea bell, a Sky-Lion observation frame, a speaking grove, a local sealed pass and a failed search for restorative water. No surviving account describes all five incidents reliably. Eryndor remained a human being with a complete human soul. His body, royal office, portraits, legendary persona and the Crowned Countenance that fed upon his ceremonies possessed separate histories after his death.
