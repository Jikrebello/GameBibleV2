# The Three Deaths of Every Great House

> **Stories told.** This is local lore of an uncontacted country. Its tellers and material customs exist there in 1360, but no transmission to the known world is established.

## Historical setting

**post-Giant local generations; not securely synchronized to AR.** far-distant cypress commonwealth and its adjoining Egg White courts. This tradition belongs to [[Distant Lands and Otherworld Histories]]; its cultural bearers are the uncontacted Orphaned peoples of the Last Houses.

It is told in a far-distant Orphaned mortuary commonwealth where great Houses rose from the care of bodies after a civilisational collapse. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## The tale

When cities failed, mortuary workers knew how to wash the dead, separate sickness from hunger and identify families by scars, teeth and clothing. They fed survivors from funeral stores and returned enough names that scattered households could find one another. People gave them land and authority because they had kept the dead from becoming refuse.

The worker families became great Houses. They beautified their founders, hid prices charged to the desperate and treated control of burial as proof of fitness to rule. At the funeral of a beloved matriarch, the House recited her first death: the public Life in which she saved a district.

A rival then performed the second death, naming military failures and political enemies. Before the grave closed, a debtor woman demanded the third. She described her husband dying outside the matriarch's sealed grain court while House dogs ate funeral meat inside. Guards tried to remove her. The matriarch's grandson instead gave her his mourning mask.

From then on, every great funeral required three tellers: kin, rival and person who bore the cost of the dead one's greatness. The custom did not make the Houses just. They learned to hire agreeable debtors and stage managed hostility. Children therefore turned the rite into a satirical street play, changing masks whenever an adult version became too clean.

The Houses repeatedly banned the children's version. Every ban supplied a new villain and ensured the fourth ending spread into another market.

## Why it is remembered

The story honours lifesaving institutions while showing how gratitude hardens into oligarchy. A person's significance expands rather than collapses when the life is allowed to include people harmed by it.

## How tellers differ

House versions say the grandson invented the rite. Debtor songs say the crowd forced him to yield. Rural versions give the third mask to a child, animal or abandoned place when no victim survived.

## What remains

Three-mask funeral plays are performed by kin, rival and cost-bearer. Street children often steal all three masks and improvise a fourth ending.
