# The Road of the Empty Purse

> **Stories told.** The local telling and the material or customary remainder below are established. A claimed original object, miraculous response, famous identity or supernatural explanation is not authenticated merely by this article.

## Historical setting

**3 BR–291 AR; Redeemer and Underground.** provincial household and pilgrimage routes. This tradition belongs to [[The Age of the Redeemer]]; its cultural bearers are early Redeemer-following households.

It is told by provincial pilgrims, old smuggling families and households descended from the Underground Centuries. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## The tale

A guide who carried believers between safe houses met a mother, a wounded deserter and two children at a river crossing. They had no money. The guide's partner refused them: the next house had been paid for four mouths, the patrol was close, and mercy toward strangers might expose everyone.

The guide emptied his purse into the river and said there could be no paid place if nobody could prove who had bought it. The household beyond the ford admitted all five. Other houses copied the gesture, and an empty purse chalked beside a threshold came to mean that a guest would be fed before questions were asked.

The road grew. So did betrayal. One host informed under threat that his daughter would be tortured. A guide killed a suspected informer who was innocent. Prisoners recanted, returned, lied and sometimes died rather than name a door. Soldiers desecrated bodies to prevent secret burial. No later song can divide the road cleanly into martyrs and cowards.

The first guide eventually arrived at a house whose owner had lost two sons because of the mark. She opened the door but refused to forgive him. He spent the night in the stable and left before dawn, placing no coin in her purse because hospitality was not a purchase and grief was not a debt he could pay.

She kept the mark after he left, but drew a line through it whenever a guest arrived. Welcome survived as her choice, not as the guide's inherited achievement.

## Why it is remembered

Its centre is a small decision about four unwanted travellers within an age crowded by famous witnesses. It matters because a custom of welcome survives without pretending its makers were pure or its victims obliged to reconcile.

## How tellers differ

Pilgrims call the guide a hidden Saint. Descendant hosts call him an ordinary sinner who once chose correctly. One church omits the deserter; another insists he later became a persecutor.

## What remains

An empty purse hangs beside certain pilgrim doors. A guest is received before it may be filled, and payment is placed inside only after departure.
