# The Supper of the Unchosen Roads

## Historical setting

**612–884 AR; Long Frontier.** Atherian–Covenant frontier districts. This tradition belongs to [[The Long Frontier and the First Unsealing]]; its cultural bearers are mixed provincial, Ahdari, Velian and Jirahar communities.

It is told in Long-Frontier military schools, caravan camps and households descended from a burned settlement.

## The tale

On the eve of a siege, an old traveller with seven small birds riding his staff and shoulders laid seven pieces of bread before a commander. Each stood for a real road: negotiate, ransom captives, retreat, wait for dawn, attack the walls, strike the supply camp, or assault the river quarter at night. The traveller described the price of each but would not choose. He also read aloud a clause hidden in the safe-conducts the city's captives had been made to sign, which gave the governor their labour, their movement and their children, and named the prisoners who must not be spent. While he spoke, the seven birds kept their heads turned toward the city, where a tally stone in the gate-tower was said to whisper the captives' count at night.

The commander selected the night assault. He believed speed would save his starving army. Soldiers entered the river quarter, set warehouses alight and found prisoners chained inside. Surrendering defenders were killed in the confusion and then deliberately killed so nobody could testify that they had yielded. The tally stone was smashed, and the captives who lived walked out free of the clause. The city fell; many captives and inhabitants burned in the river quarter.

After victory, officers claimed the old traveller had commanded the chosen road. The traveller appeared at the victory supper and placed six untouched pieces of bread before them. “You had roads,” he said. The commander struck him and ordered his expulsion. The stranger's identity became the least important part of the account: Saint, angel, spy, conscience or invention, the alternatives had been understood.

Survivors repeated the supper, letting children rearrange the bread and argue what each choice might have cost. The blackened seventh piece is never eaten.

Commanders dislike the children's game because they choose foolish roads, invent eighth choices and sometimes refuse battle altogether. That disorder is part of the custom: alternatives must remain imaginable before authority narrows them into necessity. One child always eats the retreat road before orders are given.

## Why it is remembered

It refuses the comforting lie that atrocity becomes unavoidable because every alternative was dangerous. The commander remains responsible not because a better road was certain, but because he chose with knowledge and later lied.

## How tellers differ

Military versions make the traveller an experienced scout with trained birds. Devotional versions make him an angel and the seven birds his attendants, sent to expose the false clause and nothing more. River families say no stranger came; captured officers themselves listed the choices before obeying.

## What remains

Seven pieces of bread are laid at a command supper. One is blackened, and the chooser must name what each unchosen road might have required.
