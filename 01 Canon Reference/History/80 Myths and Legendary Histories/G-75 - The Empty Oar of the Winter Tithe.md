# The Empty Oar of the Winter Tithe

> **Stories told.** The local telling and the material or customary remainder below are established. A claimed original object, miraculous response, famous identity or supernatural explanation is not authenticated merely by this article.

## Historical setting

**c. 230–310 AR.** western Kedravan fishing coast before Saltborn ethnogenesis. This tradition belongs to [[The Conversion of the Empire]]; its cultural bearers are temple descendants and early Church communities.

It is told on one western Kedravan fishing coast from the late Underground into early Legalization. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## The tale

A shore-threshold court once bargained with an offshore winter-road court. In return for rowers and watchers, the offshore court kept one crossing open when ordinary water turned inward. At first the shore rulers sent members of their own household.

They soon found substitutes. Fosterlings, fishers and provincial prisoners disappeared into embodied service. The road's tokens counted completed watches but could not tell consent from coercion. Underground officials used the route to remove believers, debtors and inconvenient witnesses without corpse or public execution.

A fisher entered the winter road to recover her taken brother. She was told that freeing him would leave an empty oar and somebody else would be seized unless the obligation itself was broken. She transformed herself through cold, labour and disguise, recognised her brother despite changed skin and brought him home. On the next tide, the court took her daughter.

The fisher returned to end the compact rather than attempt another exchange. Some say she drowned destroying the route mark; others say the road remains because shore rulers stopped her. Later church polemic made every missing believer a martyr and every nonbeliever a willing pagan servant. Families kept the harsher refrain: one rescue is not freedom when the empty place still demands a body.

Her brother spent the rest of his life rowing ordinary rescue boats. He never claimed the sacrifice had been worthwhile. Each saved stranger deepened both his gratitude and the knowledge that his sister or niece had paid for the chance.

## Why it is remembered

The adventure is a rescue whose apparent victory changes meaning at the moment another child disappears. Love for one captive becomes opposition to the system that makes captives interchangeable.

## How tellers differ

Court ballads praise the compact's saved ships. Church Lives claim the fisher acted under a Saint's guidance. Coast families disagree whether her daughter ever returned and refuse to let either claim erase the forced service.

## What remains

Winter boats carry one deliberately empty rowing place. Nobody may be named as its substitute, and an empty oar is laid across it.
