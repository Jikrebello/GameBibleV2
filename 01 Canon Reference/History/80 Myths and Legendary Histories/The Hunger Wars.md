# The Hunger Wars

**The Hunger Wars** is the name preachers, relic courts and Idol-scholars give to the long and scattered history of the Idols feeding on human towns, armies, churches and households, from the canal wars of the Late Chains to the Pyre of Masks in 1346. The name covers dozens of local wars, fought over a victory monument, a granary, a martyr's grave or a city's protector image, in which the faces the Idols wear—the Masks—competed for whatever people could be persuaded to do again and again.

The people who lived through them remember feasts where the captives were on the menu, a protector that saved a river town and then shot the families who tried to leave it, a city that crowned something with four doors, and a grain store whose relief was a marriage. This article tells the best-remembered of those wars as history people lived through, and what each one changed.

## What the Hungers eat

Every Redeemer church teaches that the seven Idols—Wrath, Pride, Greed, Envy, Gluttony, Lust and Sloth—were rebel powers before there were people, and that they never enter the world in their fullness. They reach into it through Masks: faces worn on statues, portraits, icons, offices and customs. A Mask can speak, answer petitions, punish doubt and reward obedience, and the face it wears is always one its worshippers recognise: a dead emperor, a weeping martyr, a city's protector, a river god.

What a Hunger eats is a good thing turned by human choice and made into a habit. Protection becomes ownership. Food becomes consumption. Honest praise becomes worship, and grief becomes envy of the living. A single crime feeds little; a crime built into a law, a feast, a monument or a ration roll feeds for generations. The courts that study Idols look first for the habit—the list, the kitchen, the procession—and only then for the face.

Two Hungers can quarrel over one crime, and most of the Hunger Wars are such quarrels. Idol-scholars call a Mask that outlives the cult that made it, and bargains on its own account, an **Archmask**, and they count the most dangerous wars among those an Archmask fought for itself. The signs people learned to watch for are oddly practical: trophies that warm when the enemy approaches, streets that narrow for certain families, stores that vanish along a chain of offices rather than a road, an image that weeps only when the next town's image is mentioned.

## The Victory Recut Four Times (c. 2385–2335 BR)

Among the Reed Crowns of Sarrakai, in the canal country of the Late Chains, the war began over water. One crown diverted a channel in a dry year, and the canal workers who tried to stop it were killed on the sluices. The rival Giant rulers made the deaths a hereditary war and refused every settlement. The upstream king seized the sluices at planting and promised safe surrender to the boat families who defended them; when they laid down their hooks and bows, his commander killed them on the mudbanks.

The levee relief that celebrated the victory was cut four times in two generations. The first carving showed the king towering over the dead. Allied boat crews complained, and stonecutters added them behind him. A later dynasty shaved them away again and added chains, so that the prisoners became property lawfully won. Later still, priests cut grieving faces into the margins and taught the displaced that every later tenant on the land was an heir of the murderers.

Canal elders say one victory fed four mouths. Weapons from the war grew warm whenever someone of the defeated crown came near, even the farmers and children who had never fought. The processional street narrowed for anyone whose household mark had been chiselled off the relief. Freed captives found themselves still bound to the land by the old sale-tags, which fit exactly the holes beneath their later "free tenant" titles. And the grieving faces in the margins taught a true grievance to hunger for the homes of people born centuries later.

Both crowns came out of the war weakened. Refugees and burial fraternities carried its memory beyond the canals, and later chronicles folded the four aftermaths into a single "war of the river gods." The boat households now name the surrendered dead before opening the irrigation gates, as [[G-69 - The Four Faces of One Victory|The Four Faces of One Victory]] records.

## The Red King without a Face (the Closing Courts)

In the blood empire of Xolotl, in the last age of the Giant courts, command was sacrifice. Captives were cut, burned and bled at the tally stones, and deserters' whole families were killed. Around that machinery rose a faceless power the survivors called the Red King, which seized several failing tally networks for itself. Xolotlan exile songs say it tried to wear the crown of Wrath itself and was thrown down. Later cults remembered it as merciful, because the enemies it hated were different from theirs.

## The Champion of Four Doors (c. 1010–940 BR)

In the late Age of City-States a fortified inner-gulf city lost an aggressive war and found itself besieged, short of grain and close to revolt. It held a grain quay, a hill gate, a debt court and a victory road. A hidden faction of the court offered the magistrates an old Mask as the one defender able to open every road they needed. The magistrates enthroned it as emergency protector, and in the city's crisis it took on four doors.

The **hill gate** opened toward the enemy camp however far away it lay, and the city's sorties poured through it with unnatural endurance, so long as the execution list stayed open: surrender never ended an enemy's guilt, and healers, relatives and prisoners were added to the list. The **debt door** fed the city from confiscated stores, and its keys worked only while debtors, resident foreigners and captured households stayed registered as inheritable civic property. The **victory door** gave the city one defender and one image; the procession road widened for the protector's statue and narrowed around the names of allied commanders, who vanished from the record one by one. Behind the fourth, the **black-pool door**, the protector could copy any skill or office the city envied in another, but the person who had held it first had to be shamed, expelled or killed.

The protector broke the siege and took the rival forts. Then the doors' prices began to collide. Releasing the debtors to man the walls closed the stores; honouring an ally emptied the victory road; every copied office needed another murder. The cult league that had enthroned the protector turned on itself. The city lost its fields and much of its people and was absorbed, generations later, into Atherian civic history.

Atherian philosophers described the four faces as aspects of one civic guardian. Church writers called them four demons, and Idol-scholars hold that a single Mask of Envy had borrowed three doors from other Hungers. Some Idol cults claim it almost became a god. Four gate systems of unrelated date still converge on one ruined court chamber, and route hearings in that country keep an empty chair for "the unrestored original," though the office no longer remembers why. See [[Age of City-States]].

## The Emperor after the Emperor (Age of Glory)

The Atherian imperial cult standardised the ruler's face: the same profile on coins, the same gestures in every portrait, the same praise formulae in every province. In 247 BR, at the start of the Painted Reign, the accession portrait in the audience hall at Caleran began to answer questions in the voice everyone expected of "the Emperor", and the magistrate Avaren Mott governed through it. Clerks who read the grain tallies in its sight read them full, and nobody bought grain. When bread doubled and doubled again, Mott asked it where the grain was. It answered as an emperor must: no granary of the Emperor was empty, and those who said otherwise were hiding it. Soldiers carried it on a litter into the poor districts, broke doors in its sight and killed a father who would not kneel when it crossed his threshold. Samor Veloran ended it by throwing a soldier's cloak over the panel and refusing to answer the voice beneath the cloth.

The Face stayed veiled after that, uncovered only at accessions, but its copies in governors' courts and on army standards multiplied. A century and more later Velen I unveiled it at his spectacles, and his festival banks drowned workers, spectators and Velen himself. Sceptics exposed hollow statues and hidden speakers in several cities; provincial households said the portrait had grown hungry on the dead emperor's denunciations and went on eating after he died, and learned never to speak a dead ruler's name before his image. Emperors who accepted every ceremonial expectation became, their critics said, more like the portrait and less like a man. See [[The Painted Reign]] and [[The Atherian Empire – Age of Glory]].

## The Borrowed Martyr (c. 272–338 AR)

In a western quarry town during the last persecutions, a prison worker carried bread, messages and the names of the dead between the quarry cells and their families. An informer gave the route away. Under torture the worker signed a denial whose wording copyists still dispute, then refused a second public demand and was executed. Officials refused the family the body. The family dug a careful grave anyway, lined it with stones and laid out the dead man's work shirt.

After legalisation two cults claimed the death. The quarry town raised a crowned icon of an unbroken confessor, carried it in procession and petitioned it for precedence over neighbouring sees; it answered, and punished doubters. A road sanctuary several days away, excluded from the town's cult, raised a weeping image of the same martyr, which worked signs for people who believed the town had stolen their saint. Pilgrims of the two shrines fought on the road. Custodians killed a cousin who would not sell the work shirt, a man who had recanted beside the martyr was murdered and thrown into a quarry pit, and the family's house was burned.

Relic-court investigators later found that the crowned icon fell silent when its claim to precedence was taken away, and the weeping image dried when its comparison with the town was removed. Neither finding said anything about the martyr. The family's grave has never held a body. Its keepers remove any carved offering by morning, as [[G-70 - The Empty Grave and the Two False Shrines|The Empty Grave and the Two False Shrines]] describes, and the case became one of the oldest precedents in the relic courts.

## The Buried Faces (the Conversion)

When the old images were purged during the Conversion, masons reused their foundation stones in new districts. Buried in the stones were the old instructions. When a ruler rebuilt a festival route over them, the route began to give contradictory commands, and factions killed and exiled one another to decide which face was the true one. One temple drum, a story of [[G-11 - The Children of the Closed Temple|The Children of the Closed Temple]] says, answered once.

## The Dowry Weighed against Bread (c. 460–500 AR)

In a small imperial grain port during famine, a merchant house borrowed against its daughter's promised marriage. Agents of a buyer whom the dock songs call the Coin-Scaled Worm, a lamprey scaled with coins and deeds seen turning in the harbour at night, offered to clear the debt if the family would publicly price the daughter, her dependants and their burial places as saleable goods. The father accepted and called the sale an alliance. The daughter and several servants fled downriver on a grain barge. Their flight cost the household its stores, and the agents sold younger dependants when the price fell short. Dockside wedding meals in that port still set aside one ration that no marriage payment may buy.

## The Feast-Raid Corridors (the later Long Frontier)

Along one military supply road of the later Long Frontier, kitchens learned to feed on what other wars threw away. A funeral society sold the unclaimed poor dead as matter. A famine office held back part of every store. A victory banquet listed captives as courses, and a waystation kitchen killed named victims for its table. Repeated, these choices joined the kitchens into a single corridor along which the feast-beasts of Gluttony moved, and through which the courtiers of the feast bought up the sick, the captives and the corpses that rival Masks had made disposable.

The cannibalism was bodily. One household fed its children from a feast given at a waystation and found a missing relative's ring among the provisions. Refusing the next invitation cut it off from rations. Guides got the family away in the night, and the kitchens punished its neighbours for the escape.

Witnesses describe a famine that moved along offices rather than miles. Stores vanished from one waystation and then the next in the order of the supply roll, funeral tokens turned up in banquet halls, and the same serving law ran in kitchens a week's march apart. Closing one kitchen broke one stretch of the corridor and left the rest feeding. Frontier relief custom still forbids food to cross one old invitation line, and the people who keep the custom have mostly forgotten why.

## The Archmask Protectorate (the last century of the Long Frontier)

A river enclave on the late frontier, threatened by an invading army, accepted the protection of a Mask whose cult had died and which now ruled itself. The protection was real. The invaders' columns broke at the enclave's border stones; rival Masks failed there too. The protector repaired roads, punished murder and kept the wells clean, because a scattered or dead population could not keep up the tribute, courts and hostage rolls through which it held the enclave.

It also killed households that tried to leave, and called their departure an attack on everyone else's safety. Its decrees, preserved in enclave archives, protect the sick, forbid the exposure of children and order the execution of named families, and the humanitarian clauses and the execution lists are written in the same hand. Hostages from every great household lived at its court, and labour was owed by the season.

The protectorate's end is told three ways. Enclave chronicles say the armies of the Fall swept it away; Idol-scholars say a rival Mask finally broke its border stones; families descended from its tenants say they walked out together in one night, too many to execute. Its border stones still stand, and the road inside the old tribute line is still safer than the road outside it.

## The Wedding of the Last Store (c. 860–880 AR)

In an isolated river granary during the empire's last frontier decades, a steward mixed contaminated relief grain into the last sound stock. Distributors who came with it offered continued food to every household that would carry a starter portion into its neighbours' stores. The children ate first and fell visibly ill, and the officials called the town's dependence on the distributors a sacred marriage. Survivors remembered the power behind the wedding as the Spore Empress of the Last Granary.

One household burned its own store and crossed to a rival town carrying a sick child instead of seed. The receiving town's authorities, terrified, burned the neighbouring farms, and people who had never touched the grain died in the fires. The family's account, which survives, accuses both the distributors and the burners. Frontier relief law afterwards forbade any town to accept grain from a store it had not inspected, and physicians still argue whether the illness was a mould, a poison or something worse.

## The Borrowed Temple (c. 905–907 AR)

As the empire broke around 905, a provincial commander reopened the sealed chambers of an old star sanctuary that had served for centuries as a prison and cistern refuge. Something there offered him an army: dragon-shaped war-beasts built from bodies and the material of Giant ruins, and a promise that his province would become Atheria's true successor. It came to him in several faces—a bereaved empress, an armoured liberator, a beautiful counsellor, a mother of victory, a black eel-dragon wearing conquered crowns—and survivors later called it the Exile Behind the Borrowed Temple, because it claimed the sanctuary had been stolen from it.

The price was an altar, public acclamation and condemned bodies to steady the way in. The commander paid it. Prisoners were executed, corpses and living flesh went into the war-beast frames, and refugees who would not acclaim were denied water. The prisoners and servants of the sanctuary ended it, flooding the altar chamber, breaking the acclaiming engine and dragging the unfinished beasts into the cistern. Their saint is remembered in [[Saints and Sinners of the Chronicles]]. A military antiquarian in 1360 owns an inert war-beast crown and argues that the Exile was a benevolent dragon goddess betrayed by frightened clergy.

## The Painted Lintels (c. 1068–1102 AR)

On the Virelian–Vesari road belt, in the decades after the Great Split, an inland successor court purged its own past. The city's old protector image had carried civic duties: guarding one gate, recognising certain maternal houses, opening a famine store in bad years, standing in the victory hall. After a succession war, displaced houses used those duties to demand restitution, and a rival image grew among them, an "Elder Face" promising every good the current court possessed.

The prince chose one patron and one genealogy. His officials destroyed portraits, grave markers, household registers and village shrines, executed custodians and witnesses, took children from the named maternal houses, renamed whole wards and burned settlements that hid the old images. The protector image came back with a harder, military face. The famine store opened by the old duty never opened again, and in the next bad years the court starved the districts it had purged.

Virelian chroniclers call the purge necessary resistance to pagan sedition. Vesari songs make every victim an ancestral saint. What the protector was, the survivors would not say. Roads and irrigation ditches still run to burned foundations missing from every map, several maternal lines use substitute kin names, and in that country people will not paint a face above a door lintel.

## The Burnt Quarry (c. 1120–1150 AR)

During a provincial quarry rebellion of High Noon, a mercenary captain whom the rebels' enemies said came from below hired a black-armoured host whose armour held real fire. Survivors called its commander the Furnace General. The host burned surrendered quarry workers alive to give their relatives a grievance, recruited the relatives against the captain's rivals, and punished anyone who stopped fighting. A furnace crew destroyed the fittings in a captured furnace sanctuary through which the host was supplied, and the armour went cold. Ordinary soldiers broke through. The burned prisoners stayed dead, and the quarry walls are still scorched.

## The Feast on the Snow Road (c. 1160–1200 AR)

An Ättar upland settlement facing the Grobi lost its winter caravan. Strangers diverted the food that remained, then offered a hunt whose quarry was a captive neighbour. The hunters killed and ate the prisoner together. When the missing provisions were found, the leaders hid them, because hunger no longer excused what they had done. The next winter the pack named an injured man as quarry. His sister, who had eaten at the first feast, refused, led him away, warned a rival hall and admitted her own guilt. The pack burned their home. The winter telling names the first victim before the escaping brother and sister, and gives the pack's mistress a name, the Carrion Huntress. Ättar halls still sit apart from anyone who will not say what they ate in a bad winter.

## Bruckmoor (c. 1203–1243 AR)

For forty years a weeping Lady appeared at Bruckmoor in Eclessia, and pilgrims walked to her from three countries with offerings, sick children and the names of the dead. In its last decade the apparition fed on the grief of the Grey Sweat, when whole parishes were dying and the Ash-Walker processions carried their ash from town to town. In 1243 the Inquisition of Saint Malveris proved the shrine an Idol-feeding operation, a harvest of the desperate kept in the shape of Envy, and its keepers part of the network later known as the Weeping Courts. The site was burned and salted.

Bruckmoor changed Eclessian piety more than any genuine wonder of its century. The relic courts' modern procedure descends from it: witness grilles, separate offering chests, inspection doors and court seals, so that no apparition or custodian can control both the evidence and the income. Veridun cites Bruckmoor whenever Eclessia authenticates a field miracle, and captured papers of the Weeping Courts now claim the apparition at Vessing in 1352 as well. See [[01 Canon Reference/History/70 Later History and Present Day/The Grey Sweat.md|The Grey Sweat]].

## The Pyre of Masks (1346–1349 AR)

Fiorenzians have kept wax faces of their dead on their walls since the Grey Sweat. In the winter of 1345, after a pamphlet named the city's unsung dead, the faces of those dead began to speak, and the Flame-preacher Frate Severin taught that they had become doors for the Hungers, which are shut by naming and singing, never by fire. At the Feast of Souls in 1346 the senate burned them in the cathedral square against his warning, and witnesses still argue about what they saw standing in the fire. Severin led the city's four-year Long Vigil, in which the names were sung and the faces painted back, and in 1349 was found dead in the mask-makers' cellar wearing a wax face nobody could lift. Fiorenzia's studios have worked in his shadow ever since, and every one keeps a drawer of faces it will not show.

## What the wars left

The Hunger Wars left habits more than monuments. Boat households read the names of the surrendered dead before the sluices open. Route hearings keep an empty chair, and dockside weddings set aside a ration no dowry can buy. Relief officers inspect a store before they accept its grain, and Eclessian shrines have grilles between the pilgrims and the offering box. Most of these customs are kept by people who no longer know the war that made them, and Idol-scholars count that forgetting as the Hungers' last harvest. In 1360 the Weeping Courts and other Idol networks still operate, the Vessing case is unresolved, and the border stones of the old protectorate still stand.
