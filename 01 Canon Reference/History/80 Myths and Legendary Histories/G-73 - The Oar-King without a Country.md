# The Oar-King without a Country

**The Oar-King without a Country** is the rise-and-rebuke cycle of a Folk coastal captive who saved the abandoned survivors of a proto-Atherian galley, became their captain and then conquered the harbour which became his kingdom. A prophet later exposed both that conquest and the disguised murder of the fellow rower who had opposed his kingship.

## Historical setting

**c. 1750–1500 BR.** heroic-maritime Atherian coast. This tradition belongs to [[Age of Hearths and First Kings]]; its cultural bearers are Folk, related Ättar and early Atherian tellers.

It is told in the heroic-maritime Atherian world, especially at one harbour that both crowns and dockworkers claim he founded. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## Historical basis

The future king was a Folk coastal captive sold into a proto-Atherian fleet. When a damaged, unpaid ship became a liability, its officers removed the tender, pay chest, charts and other valuables and knowingly abandoned the chained rowers, wounded and lower crew. The survivors formed a bench council. They chose the captive as speaker and ration-master because he divided food fairly and understood coastal signs.

Victories and new followers converted emergency authority into personal captaincy. Later epics attach other captains' feats to him; there was no stable signature company present at every exploit. A settled harbour offered winter refuge only if his people surrendered ships and weapons and dispersed into bonded labour. Refusal was reasonable, but it did not justify conquest. He feigned departure, returned through a difficult channel and took the harbour before dawn.

The king had no recovered pay chest. He rewarded followers with houses, stores and shares in the sale of prisoners. Older inhabitants were killed, expelled, renamed or compelled to labour. His principal rival was a fellow chained rower and leader of the original bench council, who argued that emergency command must end and the company should leave after winter.

The king's agents weakened the rival's steering gear before a ceremonial boat race. The vessel struck a reef, and the court declared the death an accident. The king carried the recovered oar as proof of his friend's supposed blessing.

A genuine prophet of the Lord, holding a bounded commission and no succession from the Great Prophet, later told the king of a rich captain who stole a freed rower's only boat. When the king condemned the offender, the prophet identified him as that man and exposed both the murder and the conquered harbour's suffering. The king confessed publicly, returned the oar, freed captives still within his control and ended the future sale of people as payment. He kept the crown and most allotments.

After his death, rival heirs and factions fought a succession war which ended hereditary kingship and produced a civic magistracy. The surviving ceremonial oar is a disputed substitute, while the dockside lament preserves names of captives omitted from royal history.

## The tale

A Folk captive survived when a payless fleet's officers stripped and abandoned a damaged ship in foreign water. The remaining rowers, wounded and lower crew freed one another, formed a bench council and chose him to divide their bilge-soaked grain. He brought them through dangerous water by reading the coast. Refugees, criminals, pilots and defeated soldiers joined him from port to port.

He began as the man who shared every bench. Victories made him captain; need made him judge; conquest made him king. An inhabited harbour offered refuge only if the ships and weapons were surrendered and the company dispersed into bondage. He feigned departure, returned by a difficult channel and seized the harbour before dawn. His followers killed defenders, sold captives and took houses. The Oar-King protected his company from bondage but did not protect local families from his company.

His oldest companion from the bench council demanded they leave after winter and return emergency authority to the crews. Before a ceremonial boat race, the king's agents weakened the companion's steering gear. The boat struck a reef, and the court called the death an accident. The king then carried the recovered oar before his new crown, claiming friendship had blessed the city.

The harbour prospered. Captive pilots made it a centre of navigation; older inhabitants survived beneath new names; the rowers' children became citizens. Then a prophet told the king of a rich captain who stole a freed rower's only boat. The king condemned the imaginary offender, and the prophet answered that he had judged himself.

The king confessed the murder and the violence of his foundation. He returned the oar, freed those captives he could still command and forbade future payment through the sale of people. He did not surrender the crown or most conquered allotments. After his death, competing heirs broke the dynasty in war, and the harbour replaced its kings with magistrates.

Dockside singers continued naming those sold during the founding generation. Each year a magistrate carries a substitute oar in public while an unofficial lament follows one street behind.

Once, a magistrate turned and tried to silence the lament. The bearers lowered the ceremonial oar and refused to continue. Since then the procession can advance only while the song remains audible, binding civic pride and imperfect repentance to the victims neither can repay.

## Why it is remembered

It gives the irresistible ascent from slave to king without pretending humble suffering prevents later abuse. The audience can love the rower who saves a crew and condemn the ruler who murders the friend able to remember him before the crown.

## How tellers differ

Civic epics make the harbour empty or tyrannical before conquest. Older-family songs name markets, wells and graves already present. Royal lists turn the companion's murder into honourable single combat. Sailors attach many independent victories to the king's early company.

## What remains

A ceremonial oar of disputed age precedes one magistrate. Dockworkers trail it with a lament whose verses name captives rather than kings.
