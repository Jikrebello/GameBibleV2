# The Returners' Second Sack — Authorial Truth

> **What happened.** The central event and surviving evidence are established; no living witness knows the whole account. Its current discovery remains unfinished. This history is separate from its nearest thematic neighbours, as recorded in the integration ledger.

## What Actually Happened

**c. 957–1020 AR; one reconquered provincial town and its water court.** Exact coordinates and private names remain open; this local placement does not move the controlling chronology.

During the Fall, one provincial town surrendered after its garrison fled and its refugee elite escaped with the army. The occupiers appointed local stewards, imported officials, and occupation-born families to keep the wells, mills, hospital, grain court, and tax rolls operating. Some stewards enriched themselves, informed on resistance households, seized abandoned property, or assisted forced requisitions. Others kept people alive, hid fugitives, paid ransoms, and treated the property of absent refugees as a trust rather than spoil. Over two generations these categories became entangled through marriage, debt, coercion, and ordinary survival.

Before the reconquest, the town's water court wrote a maintenance register recording who had repaired each property, which refugee claims remained valid, which estates had been confiscated, and which occupants had earned a hearing before removal. The register did not grant permanent ownership automatically. It made return conditional upon distinguishing collaboration, necessity, service, purchase, coercion, and theft. Copies went to a merchant house, a frontier monastery, and the occupation tax office.

A returning claimant army retook the town after a short siege. Its leaders needed pay for soldiers and estates for political supporters. They also wanted a clean story in which every person found inside the walls was an occupier or willing collaborator. The commander ordered the steward council, mixed households named by refugee claimants, and several occupation-born clergy arrested before the water register could be heard. Some prisoners had committed murder or confiscation. Others were guilty only of birth, marriage, or maintaining civic works. The army executed them together outside the hospital wall.

The victors then burned the principal maintenance register and issued replacement titles dated as though the refugee houses had retained uninterrupted possession. They granted estates to supporters who had never owned them and converted repairs made by resident families into debts supposedly owed to the returning claimants. Soldiers killed people resisting eviction, and dependants were expelled during winter. This was the town's second sack: smaller than the original conquest, committed in the name of restoration, and omitted from the victorious chronicle.

The deception never became perfect. The returning administration needed the occupiers' tax figures, water schedules, and repair knowledge, so clerks scraped older tablets and wrote the new titles over them. A hostile merchant retained paired entries because it was still owed money by both sides. The frontier monastery preserved a partial copy while removing names of families it had secretly sheltered. Later descendants inherited real refugee losses, real occupation crimes, real service claims, and fraudulent titles simultaneously.

The event does not make the Reconquest illegitimate or its opponents innocent. It fixes one entirely Material town in which a just cause did not prevent victorious people from committing murder, dispossession, and documentary fraud. No Patron, Egg White refuge, portable treasury, carried-out property register, or bread-jar deed participates. Repentance by individual soldiers or later restitution cannot resurrect the executed or make the first generation of false grants true.

## What Later Traditions Say

- **The victory chronicle** says occupation troops massacred their own stewards before fleeing and that returning claimants restored the town without reprisal.
- **Current noble houses** possess apparently continuous titles beginning before the Fall, several of which were actually created after the reconquest.
- **Occupation-descended families** remember every executed steward as a civic martyr, including documented informers and confiscators.
- **Water workers** preserve the phrase *the well served the hands that kept it*, without remembering the hearing procedure from which it came.



## Discovery Interface

- **Knowledge state:** `suppressed` at first, later `lost` and `misclassified`.
- **Limited contemporary witnesses:** the returning chaplain recorded executions but believed all prisoners were collaborators; a water clerk knew the service classifications but fled before the second sack; a merchant copyist preserved paired debts without understanding the killings; a soldier's confession names forged grants but not every beneficiary.
- **Primary remainders:** the hospital-wall grave, reconstruction-era weapons among the dead, scraped tax tablets beneath current titles, repair marks predating reconquest grants, merchant double entries, and the monastery's redacted register.
- **Independent evidence classes:** material, documentary, testimonial, comparative, and negative.
- **Transmission damage:** victorious archives destroyed the main register; the monastery erased protected names; families split documents during inheritance; occupation apologists canonized criminals; later title courts treated reused seals as proof of continuity.
- **1360 AR access point:** expansion of the hospital uncovers the grave while a water-right lawsuit forces three current houses to produce their oldest title tablets.
- **Reproducible test:** warming and re-waxing the title tablets causes the deeper occupation-era impressions to take ink before the later writing. Independent tablets reveal the same removed service categories and different original holders. The process establishes rewriting but does not by itself determine rightful present ownership.
- **Plausible false reading:** occupation troops killed the stewards before retreating, and returning clerks merely reused enemy tablets during an emergency restoration.
- **Corroboration threshold:** grave dating and weapon context, under-impressions, merchant entries, monastery copy, and repair chronology must align before the second sack and deliberate title fraud are established.
- **What remains unknowable:** every prisoner's individual guilt, the commander's private motive, the number who died after eviction, which present heirs knew of the fraud, and the final equitable distribution of current property.
- **Protected Weird check:** the event neither involves nor explains the Thirty Sisters and does not predetermine present property judgements.
