# The Siege and the Returnings

The Siege and the Returnings is a coastal heroic cycle concerning the fall of High Harbour and the fate of everything carried out of it. The stronghold stood on the maritime approaches between the countries later called Arkena and Saronika. Its siege is placed broadly around 1980–1960 BR, and the voyages and household conflicts that followed run into the next generation. The participating communities included Orphaned survivors of the Giant age, settlers descended from the Glass People and households already formed from both.

The war was fought over a speaking image. High Harbour's water answered to an old bargain between a tide mistress of the coast and the image of the harbour's founder, and the people taken in the harbour's collection raids were her price. The besiegers quarrelled over whether to take the image whole or break it. Two acts decided the war, and neither belonged to a captain: the harbour's own craftspeople opened its water gate, and then they broke the image in the fire. Shore tellers say the sea took its due ever after from whatever had been carried out of the harbour as property, and that ships which carried nothing from High Harbour came home. The second half of the cycle, the Returnings, is told hull by hull. Captains' songs give prominence to prowess and rivalry. Shore traditions follow captives, bereaved relatives and a shipmistress whose rescue work continued after the man she had hoped to save was dead.

## High Harbour and its channel

High Harbour survived the destruction of its parent kingdom under a detached Giant house. A defensible landing and stores above the waterfront allowed its ruler to collect grain and labour from neighbouring settlements. Human craftspeople maintained the harbour, while merchants and service households obtained protection and opportunities through royal favour. Captives supplied labour, and collection parties took people from communities unable to meet the ruler's demands.

The harbour's water belonged to an older arrangement. In the palace stood the image of the founder, a figure in the likeness of an ancestral king that spoke with the founder's voice and remembered the harbour works stone by stone. Long before, the founder had made terms with a tide mistress of the coast. She kept the harbour deep and its storms outside the breakwater; in return an old channel beneath the stronghold was opened at each season's turn, and travellers and goods passed through it into her country. In the last generations the travellers were the people taken by the collection parties. Families along the shore knew where their sons and daughters had gone, because the tide ran smooth on the morning after each opening.

The image also promised that the reduced house could regain its former greatness. The living ruler's willingness to believe that promise prolonged resistance after several dependent settlements sought peace.

## The coalition

The conflict began with seized vessels and detained households. A shipmistress sought the release of her husband and apprentices. Other captains wanted the royal stores, and an inland ally whose villages had lost children to the collection parties hoped to end the raids. They assembled ships and fighters together, choosing a leading commander whose influence rested on what his household and supporters could supply. Other captains retained enough independence to refuse particular undertakings.

The coalition closed the shore, and the collection parties stopped going out. The besiegers lived on raided stores and supplies brought from home. Their commander distributed captives from the harbour's dependent villages among influential households. He also kept a woman seized from a headland sanctuary and ordered that she be confined for his sexual use.

The camp's great quarrel was over the image. The commander's pilots had learned what it was, and he meant to take it whole. Whoever held the image would hold the harbour and its tide, and a harbour whose sea obeyed its master was worth more than every store above the waterfront. The shipmistress, the inland ally and a captain with ships of his own wanted it broken. Their people had gone through the channel, and any new holder would keep the channel open and keep feeding it.

The captain who challenged the commander had himself accepted enslaved villagers, and he resented the senior household's growing share of stores and followers as much as he hated the channel. When the commander took his provisions for an assault on the breakwater, he loaded his ships and sailed for home before the second winter. His descendants made a virtue of the departure. Shore tellers say only that he took his share of the villages' people with him and nothing from the harbour, and that every one of his hulls reached its own beach.

## Four wreck seasons

The siege lasted four winters, which the besiegers counted as wreck seasons. The tide mistress kept her bargain. Inside the breakwater the water lay flat in every weather. Outside it each autumn's storms came down on the coalition's anchorage, broke hulls on the rocks and carried fighters under.

The fighting centred on the old breakwater and the tidal channel below the stronghold. Defenders repaired the works while the attackers drove rams and towers against them. On several days both armies saw a mailed figure standing in the breach amid the impacts, and once the force of a ram turned back along its own beam and killed the crew. Witnesses on both sides identified the apparition with Astaroth, and each side's singers claim the Red Star for their own. It was last seen in the third winter, shortly before hungry besiegers dug the old bronze out of the pier footings and traded it up the coast for grain.

With the shore closed, High Harbour had no travellers to send. At the first season's turn the channel was opened for prisoners taken from the coalition, and the tide ran smooth. By the second winter there were no prisoners left. The image named the households that would go: debtors of the house first, then servants, and in the third winter families of the lower town, drawn by lot from the craft quarters that kept the harbour's works. The masons and smiths who greased the channel gates opened them for their neighbours, and at last for their own children.

## The woman who went in

In the fourth winter the shipmistress helped the confined woman out of the commander's quarters. Her own husband had died inside High Harbour by then, and rescue had become the work through which she continued to act among the assembled crews. The escaped woman had heard in the commander's tent what he meant to do with the image. The two women went along the foot of the cliffs to the lower town in a single night, by a path shown to them by a shore-dweller who knew where it would open. Some tellers give this guide a familiar divine name. The guide's price was that both should go home, when they went, by the long road inland.

They told the keepers of the channel gates what the commander intended. Whoever won the war, the image would stand and the channel would be fed, and the craft quarters would go on feeding it. The craftspeople sent their terms back by the same path. They would leave the water gate of the lower works unbarred at the dark of the moon, and the coalition would spare the lower town. The shipmistress and the inland ally swore to it. The commander swore as well.

## The gate and the hammers

High Harbour fell on the night the craftspeople chose. The coalition's boats came in through the water gate on a tide the defenders believed was still guarding them. The High Giant ruler died in the fighting on the palace stair. The image of the founder then appeared above the palace and called for continued resistance; its promise of restored royal power had survived the king it encouraged. The commander's men were climbing to carry it off whole when the craftspeople reached it first. They were the harbour's own masons and smiths, coming up through the palace yards with the fire behind them, and they had kept the channel gates for the house all their lives. They broke the image with their hammers and left it in pieces on the stair.

The next morning the tide ran wrong. Water came in where it had always gone out, and the old channel stood open and empty. The reversed water undercut the breakwater road, and the wall above it fell on an inhabited quarter.

The commanders broke their oath to the lower town. The inland ally's men did the first killing. Through the siege a figure with the face and voice of a taken child had come to their fires at night, a different child for each man, urging them to leave no household of the harbour alive. On the night of the fall they murdered families who had opened the gate and whose own children had gone through the channel. The likenesses are remembered in some songs as loyal ghosts and in others as a deceiver feeding upon grief. Soldiers of every contingent murdered inhabitants, raped captives and carried people away for sale. One officer took a gate-keeper's daughter from the gate-house where her family had waited for the promised safety, assaulted her and carried her aboard his vessel. Nobody in the coalition called him to account, because the allies wanted his ships and crews.

The shipmistress carried some survivors out in small craft. Others fled inland, entered captivity or remained under the new occupiers. The fall ended the surviving Giant court's rule over the harbour. The victors' prize did not last. With no one to keep the water deep, the harbour silted within a generation, and storms walked over the breakwater the tide mistress had once guarded.

## The sea's due

Shore tellers explain everything that followed by the broken image. The bargain had been made for a harbour that could pay. When the image was broken nobody was left to pay, and the tide mistress took her due from whatever had come out of High Harbour by sea: its bronze, its grain and the people held as its goods. Ships carrying them met calms where there should have been wind, currents that drew them back towards the empty channel, and waves that rose from flat water. Captains' songs call it the anger of the sea. Shore songs call it the harbour's debt following its goods. They add a rule that the captains learned slowly: a captive set free on a beach stopped being cargo, and the sea lost interest in the ship.

The Returnings are told as a count of hulls.

### The ships that carried nothing

The captain who had left before the second winter was already home. The shipmistress's small craft, loaded with survivors whom nobody owned, crossed open water in the same weeks as the plunder fleets and lost no one. Shore tellers set the two beside each other on purpose: a man who quarrelled over his share, and a woman who took none.

### The commander's fleet

The commander sailed last, with the richest hulls and the most captives. His fleet met the calms within sight of the headland. His pilots told him that the sea wanted its travellers, and he had women and children of the harbour bound and put over the side, a few from each ship and then more. The calm did not lift. One captains' song praises the drowning as a hard duty that a lesser man would have shirked. The captives' kin along the shore kept a song of their own, in which he is the man who paid a dead harbour's debt with other people's daughters. No ship of his reached home. Fishing villages on the approaches say that for a season his hulls could be seen at dusk, rowing against a current that carried them back towards the empty channel. His later fame as the man who united the coast comes from the songs of households that had sailed before him, and it coexists with the account of the woman he ordered confined.

### The captain and the bay

The officer who had carried off the gate-keeper's daughter sailed with captives, treasure and companions. His first ship broke on a reef. The gate-keeper's daughter survived the wreck, escaped when the survivors reached land and joined a community whose boats were pulling people out of the next disaster. At another landing a court being of the shore took his company in. It killed and ate several companions, and it let the rest go only when they rolled most of the harbour's bronze into its cave.

He reached his own bay with one ship, a little bronze and the High Harbour women he still held. The storms came in with him. The boat-owners who had run his house in his absence lost three crews in a month, and their seers told them that the sea wanted what had come out of the harbour. They rolled his bronze into the channel at the bay's mouth, and the storms returned. They bound the captain and rolled him in after it, and the storms returned again. The new masters had by then divided the High Harbour women among themselves. They turned to them next, as the last of the harbour's goods.

The eldest of the women was a gate-keeper's widow. She told the boat-owners that the sea had never been paid in the drowned. It had been paid through an opened channel, at the season's turn, by someone who could answer for the bargain. The women cut a channel through the bar with the bay's own spades. When the tide turned she stood in it to her neck and spoke for herself and for the others by name. The storms left the bay. From that season the women kept the channel and the house above it, and the boat-owners stayed on their terms or left. For a generation captains along the coast brought High Harbour bronze to that bay, to be put through the channel for a fee. The keeper stood in the water at every season's turn for the rest of her life, and the tellers say that not every keeper after her came out.

### The house the sea could not find

A different ship, heavy with captives, reached a sheltered inlet held by a Faewild hostess skilled in bodily alteration. She fed the survivors and treated their wounds. She also told them what she had to sell: the sea knew its debtors by their bodies, and she could change a body until the sea no longer knew it. The price was a place in her household. Most of the captives took the bargain, and several sailors with them. She did not ask everyone. Once the ship's company depended on her care she changed several who had refused, to keep them. Their altered limbs and voices carried the awareness of the people who had arrived there, and the tide passed over them.

Leaving cost more than staying. One sailor surrendered an original bodily trait to gain permission to go. Another stayed beside a companion who could no longer safely travel. The captain sailed home with a diminished crew and an empty hold, and he reached it without trouble. The episode is told both as a warning about dangerous hospitality and as a story of attachment, and in most versions the sailor who stayed has a larger place than the captain who left.

### The shore of the put-ashore

Captains who understood the rule emptied their holds on the nearest beach. People from High Harbour were left on sandbars and headlands along the whole approach, some with a water skin and some with nothing. The shipmistress spent the following seasons collecting them. Her expected landing was occupied by one of the victorious allies, and returning under that household's authority would have restored dependence upon the coalition she had left. She chose a smaller shore whose inhabitants were willing to receive the newcomers' skills and labour.

Their first winter depended on shared food and boat repairs. People accustomed to giving orders aboard their own vessels had to negotiate work with established families. The old hulls were broken up for their timber, and the vessel the community built from them became the subject of a feast celebrating its first shared construction. The woman from the headland sanctuary reached that shore two years later by the inland road, as her guide had required.

## Remembrance

The cycle leaves several forms of memory: bronze shown as fragments of the god's armour, songs about rescue and the shipmistress, the boat-building feast of the new shore and the keepers' house above the captain's bay. Claims about individual pieces of bronze vary. Some shore keepers now say their pieces came from the broken image rather than the god's armour, and show the hammer-marks.

Southern shipmasters drew a rule from the war which later sea custom kept: no bargain with a power of the sea may be bound to an image or an office "that cannot drown". A living keeper can be made to answer in the water; a statue cannot.

Tellers also disagree about which of the voyagers deserve the honour given to returning heroes.
