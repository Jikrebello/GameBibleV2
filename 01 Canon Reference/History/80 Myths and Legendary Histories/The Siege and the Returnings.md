# The Siege and the Returnings

The Siege and the Returnings is a coastal heroic cycle concerning the fall of High Harbour and the voyages that followed it. The stronghold stood on the maritime approaches between the countries later called Arkena and Saronika. Its siege is placed broadly around 1980–1960 BR, with the return journeys and household conflicts continuing into the following generation. The participating communities included Orphaned survivors of the Giant age, settlers descended from the Glass People and households already formed from both.

The cycle preserves a war against a surviving Giant ruler, the violence of the victors and several very different homecomings. Captains' songs give prominence to prowess and rivalry. Shore traditions follow captives, bereaved relatives and a shipmistress whose rescue work continued after the man she had hoped to save was dead. Accounts of the intervening powers also differ, particularly where later singers gather several coastal beings under one familiar divine name.

## High Harbour and its enemies

High Harbour survived the destruction of its parent kingdom under a detached Giant house. A defensible landing and stores above the waterfront allowed its ruler to collect grain and labour from neighbouring settlements. Human craftspeople maintained the harbour, while merchants and service households obtained protection and opportunities through royal favour. Captives supplied labour, and collection parties took people from communities unable to meet the ruler's demands.

The conflict began with seized vessels and detained households. A shipmistress sought the release of her husband and apprentices. Other captains wanted the royal stores, and an inland ally hoped to end the collection raids. They assembled ships and fighters together, choosing a leading commander whose influence rested on what his household and supporters could supply. Other captains retained enough independence to refuse particular undertakings.

The Giant court maintained relationships with powers of the coast. A tide mistress protected the harbour's water in return for the seasonal opening of an old channel. Travellers and matter passing through it sustained her own territory. Within the palace, a figure in the likeness of an ancestral king promised that the reduced house could regain its former greatness. The living ruler's willingness to believe that promise prolonged resistance after several dependent settlements sought peace.

## The quarrel in the camp

The besieging coalition depended on raided stores and supplies brought from home. Its commander distributed captives among influential households and kept a woman seized from a neighbouring sanctuary. Her father offered ransom. The commander refused and ordered that she be confined for his sexual use.

A wind envoy attached to the sanctuary household had been assisting the coalition's supply vessels. She withdrew that aid and diverted a returning convoy. Food spoiled offshore, and the danger of shortage gave the dispute over the captive immediate force within the camp.

The captain who challenged the commander had himself accepted enslaved people. He resented the refusal of ransom and the senior household's growing share of stores and followers. When the commander seized some of his people and provisions, he withheld his ships from the next assault. Companions still fighting ashore lost the support on which they had relied.

The shipmistress eventually helped the captive escape. Her own husband had died inside High Harbour, and rescue had become the work through which she continued to act among the assembled crews. The two women organised passages for other captives. Their story remained especially important to shore tellers whose families remembered the siege through flight and resettlement. The leading commander's later fame as a unifier coexisted with the account of the rape and confinement he had ordered.

## Tide, wind and bronze

The heaviest fighting centred on an old breakwater and the tidal channel below the stronghold. Defenders repaired the works while the attackers drove heavy structures against them. A mailed figure appeared amid the impacts and turned the force of one machine into its destruction. Witnesses from both armies identified the apparition with Astaroth, and later singers made rival claims about which side the Red Star favoured.

As timber and bodies choked the channel, the tide mistress rose through the wreckage. She tore away part of the attackers' landing and carried fighters beneath the water. The wind envoy intervened from an exposed headland, forcing spray and water across the upper defences. Several vessels escaped through the space she opened. Falling masonry struck an inhabited quarter above them.

A pilot showed a human fighter where the old pier gathered the force of the incoming sea. Reaching the mailed apparition, the fighter broke the anchoring bronze at the moment of impact and struck the figure as it came apart. Songs describe blood running from the god's armour. They agree more closely about the broken bronze and the loss of the siege machine than about the divine wound.

During the retreat, the fighter's companion was killed. A figure bearing the dead man's appearance subsequently urged him to take revenge upon every household supporting the harbour. Its familiar speech persuaded him to extend the quarrel beyond those who had fought his companion. He murdered prisoners unconnected with the death. The likeness is remembered in some songs as a loyal ghost and in others as a deceiver feeding upon grief.

## A father crosses the shore

A father within High Harbour sought the body of his son, who lay among the dead near the enemy camp. A shore guide brought him by a path that shortened the approach. The guide's price was a long return inland, away from the easy crossing by which the father had arrived.

The meeting secured the body and a pause for burial. Communities on opposing sides retained the episode because its grief could be recognised within their own households. Some tellers give the guide a familiar divine name; others remember a particular shore-dweller who knew where the path would open. The burial remained one of the cycle's quiet passages amid accounts of prowess and revenge.

## The sack

High Harbour fell after the attackers coordinated a withdrawal with the tide and received information from inhabitants expecting negotiated terms. They returned through exposed harbour works before the defenders could close them. Their success depended upon the construction of the port, divisions within its population and the movements of its waters.

The High Giant ruler died in the fighting. The ancestral figure then appeared above the palace and called for continued resistance. Craftspeople trying to escape the fire destroyed the image through which it spoke. Its promise of restored royal power had survived the king it encouraged.

Commanders broke their assurances to the households that had admitted them. Soldiers murdered inhabitants, raped captives and carried people away for sale. One officer assaulted a woman who had sheltered under the tide mistress's protection and took her aboard his vessel. Fellow victors protected him because they wanted his ships and crews to remain with them.

The shipmistress carried some survivors out in small craft. Others fled inland, entered captivity or remained under the new occupiers. The fall ended the surviving Giant court's rule over the harbour. Its inhabitants then had to live with the victors and the promises they had broken.

## The captain pursued by the tide

The officer who violated the refuge sailed with captives, treasure and companions. The tide mistress pursued his company through connected waters and local allies. Her attacks struck the people he carried as well as the man she sought. The captive woman survived the first wreck and escaped when the ship reached land. She joined a community whose boats recovered survivors from the next disaster.

At another refuge, the captain and his men were trapped by a predatory court being. It killed and consumed several companions before they escaped by blinding the bodily form in which it held them. Once away, the captain boasted of his victory and disclosed their next destination. The pursuing court recovered their trail.

His eventual homecoming brought further violence. His household had acquired new rulers during his absence, and he killed the men contesting his return. He also ordered enslaved women hanged for assisting them. Some had obeyed under coercion. The restored house inherited a feud which continued beyond the welcome accorded to its master.

Voyage songs admire his courage, endurance and resourcefulness. Accounts preserved by people harmed at his departure and return place those qualities within a less admiring history. His homecoming restored his authority over a household whose dead could not share in the restoration.

## The changing hostess

A different ship reached a sheltered place held by a Faewild hostess skilled in bodily alteration. She fed the survivors and treated their wounds. Once they depended upon her care, she changed several of their bodies to keep them within her household. Their altered limbs and voices carried the awareness of the people who had arrived there.

Release came at different prices. One sailor surrendered an original bodily trait to gain permission to leave. Another stayed beside a companion who could not safely travel. The hostess offered the captain a prolonged bodily life and a place beside her. He chose to return to the people who had known him before the war and left with a diminished company.

The episode is told both as a warning about dangerous hospitality and as a story of attachment. The sailor who remained occupies a larger place in some versions than the departing captain. Returning alive had become a question involving friendship, dependence and the bodies in which the survivors would have to continue their lives.

## The boats that chose another shore

The shipmistress and several recovered captives found their expected landing occupied by one of the victorious allies. Returning under that household's authority would have restored dependence upon the same coalition from which they had escaped. They chose a smaller shore whose inhabitants were willing to receive their skills and labour.

Their first winter depended on shared food and boat repairs. People accustomed to giving orders aboard their own vessels had to negotiate work with established families. The vessel they eventually built together became the subject of a feast celebrating the new community's first shared construction.

In one received telling, captive women burn the old ships to prevent being carried back into slavery. In another, the hulls are unusable and are dismantled by common agreement. Both versions lead to the building of the new vessel, but they remember very different decisions at the beginning of settlement. The surviving tradition does not establish which account of the old boats is true.

## Remembrance

The cycle leaves several forms of memory: bronze shown as fragments of the god's armour, burial customs recalling the father's return, songs about rescue and the shipmistress, and the boat-building feast of the new shore. Claims about individual pieces of bronze vary. Their exhibition demonstrates the importance of the tale without identifying every object as part of the breakwater.

Tellers also disagree about which of the voyagers deserve the honour given to returning heroes. A crew can preserve a captain's skill while remembering his victims; a settlement can celebrate its survival while acknowledging the violence that carried its founders there. The cycle's different endings allowed later maritime communities to recognise more than one kind of inheritance in the war.
