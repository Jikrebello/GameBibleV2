# The Five Staffs and the Empty Granary

**The Five Staffs and the Empty Granary** remembers how one premature grain requisition became a repeated southern policy after its first expedition succeeded. Its central judgement was real but unenforced: a camp judge ruled the seizure unlawful and ordered removal and restitution, while the southern council retained the commander and refused both remedies.

> **Stories told.** The local telling and the material or customary remainder below are established. A claimed original object, miraculous response, famous identity or supernatural explanation is not authenticated merely by this article.

## Historical setting

**c. 2150–2110 BR.** southern dispersal corridors and Near-Glass borderlands. This tradition belongs to [[Age of Scattering]]; its cultural bearers are Ark-derived and neighbouring Orphaned communities.

It is told in post-Prophet Glass-camp traditions, later municipal emergency rites and northern caravan satire. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## Historical basis

A commander rival to the commissioned scout-captain of [[G-56 - The Empty Seat Facing Outward|The Empty Seat Facing Outward]] used the newly mapped southern routes and public fear of delay to take grain before provisioners had completed the dependant counts. The expedition survived and reached viable country. Its success protected the commander, and the council repeated the levy over several seasons while reclassifying grain as seed, marching provision and advance settlement allotments.

The policy harmed many rear settlements. Two poorly connected camps with unusually high numbers of children, old people and other dependants suffered the clearest famine deaths. Three staff-holding officers can be securely placed at the final hearing: the commander, the chief provisioner who had objected and the judge. Later tellers turned household muster and public testimony into a fourth and fifth staff.

The hearing was held in a stripped granary; whether the commander attended or sent a representative remains disputed. The judge ruled the requisition unlawful, ordered restitution and declared the commander removable. The southern council enforced none of it. Material success therefore explains why the policy survived but does not vindicate it.

## The tale

After the Prophet died, the people still had captains, judges, witnesses, provisioners and household callers. Later songs gave each office one staff and pretended there had always been exactly five. In truth, offices changed hands and number while nobody could inherit the commission that had held them together.

Drought emptied the rear camps. A southern expedition promised new fields if it left before the last rains. The captain took grain before the provisioners could count the sick, children and old people who would remain. He believed delay would condemn everyone. When the expedition succeeded, his allies repeated the seizure and turned emergency shares into seed, marching provision and land allotments for those who went south.

Two rear camps starved. Families boiled harness and buried children without shrouds. The judge condemned the seizure after the grain had been eaten, ordered restitution and declared the commander removable. The expedition council refused both orders because success seemed to prove him right.

At the last common meeting, three office staffs were thrown onto an empty granary floor. The songs add two more for the household count and those who testified. Southern households lifted the military staff and departed. Northern households took the provision staff and refused another levy. Nobody touched the Prophet's old walking place. The temporary expedition became a lasting separation because people had died under an authority that would not answer after necessity passed.

The expedition later found the grain country it promised. That success sharpened rather than settled the accusation. Descendants of the dead could see that their food had purchased a real future and still ask why the buyers alone inherited it.

## Why it is remembered

It makes a succession crisis hurt through hunger rather than titles. The commander may have saved future settlers, but the tale asks whether achievement can answer children who died because their food became somebody else's future.

## How tellers differ

Atherian versions make the march unavoidable. Jirahar versions make the rear camps the faithful remnant. Ahdari jokes add a sixth staff belonging to the cook whom every office ignored. Records support three historical staff-bearers, not the tale's fixed five.

## What remains

Emergency staffs are laid on an empty granary floor before stored food may become military property. No tradition gives them identical names.
