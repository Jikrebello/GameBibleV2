# Additional Mortal Lives and Frontier Traditions

These localized traditions give earlier short leads a place in the world. Their tellings and ordinary inheritances are canon; supernatural identifications and exact ancient names are not automatically authenticated. No generic attribution to a living culture replaces the named sources retained in the authorial ledger.

## Midwives at Cloudstep

**Late Chains; Qianglong terraces.** A mother hid an infant beneath drying medicinal leaves while a midwife substituted an older birth-mark. The decree still killed children in neighbouring houses. Captured attendants were tortured or executed. The district's cradle songs preserve both rescue and failure; no tally of saved infants makes the dead disappear.
^atlas-i-h02

## The Child Who Snared Noon

**Chains; Ash Calendar work estates.** A small enslaved child ignored an elder sibling and trapped an overseer's light-bird. The bird was a constructed calendar refraction, not Father Sun. Work bells and sacrifice clocks disagreed until the siblings dismantled the tether. The tale endangers the household rather than making the child a harmless benefactor; exact additional death or maiming is not invented where the named-source research remains incomplete.
^atlas-i-l03

## The Unowned Afternoon

**Late Nythalum work harbours.** A hungry slave stole a feast-shift cylinder for food, revenge and amusement. Breaking it halted kitchens but erased a labour gang's promised lunar berths. At least one worker died in the panic. Later leap-feasts praise public freedom while dock laments remember the selfish theft and the lost passage. The cylinder never contained time itself.
^atlas-i-l04

## The Loaf Shared before the Masters

**Late Chains; one Glassward famine estate.** Captive cooks divided a ration by need rather than rank. An ash-dark place appeared for omitted dependants and exposed a concealed reserve store. The loaf did not multiply. Some who shared it still died. Later cults tried to worship the Bread-Bearer; the event had exposed a predatory omission, not redeemed Gluttony or created a pre-Redeemer Saint.
^atlas-i-i04

## The Messenger of the Closing Court

**Warning years; one Copper Stair client.** A kitchen messenger carried the Prophet's warning to a ruler already responsible for murdered hostages. The ruler asked for a sign which would preserve the throne without releasing anyone. The messenger returned with the names of captives rather than a promise of victory. Court historians later replaced the refusal with a polite exchange of gifts.
^atlas-bx-01

## The House That Opened One Gate

**Great Unbinding; Glassward crownlets.** A guard household admitted refugees after neighbouring estates refused them. Pursuers executed helpers left behind. The opening saved one column and stripped the household of its position; it was not a universal weakness in Giant fortifications or the act which caused liberation.
^atlas-bx-02

## The City That Stayed Behind

**Collapse; one Urum-facing river town.** Well keepers and grain workers declined the gathering because they could not move the town's dependants. They fought retainers, bargained with a surviving Giant and kept a canal functioning. Their descendants became one Orphaned local population, neither a secret Ark branch nor the ancestor of every remnant people.
^atlas-bx-03

## The Severed Pursuit

**Glassward crossing; Unbinding.** A heavenly servant under the Phoenix's commission severed one pursuing war-brood engine while porters carried injured people away. Soldiers still died before the engine stopped. The later scorched harness shows a bounded action; it neither explains the Glass nor offers a relic which modern armies can repeat.
^atlas-bx-04

## The Hunter Who Was a Guest

**Early Hearths; Orphaned Folk woodland.** A hunter honoured a court's winter hospitality and brought food home. Next season he treated that earlier welcome as ownership, took an animal promised to another household and lost his way. His child died while waiting for the promised return. The household's empty hunting peg remembers both the first courtesy and the later presumption.
^atlas-i-l02

## The Collar before the Crown

**Late Chains object; early Hearths reuse.** A collar worn by an enslaved household was sold after an execution. A rising cattle king later displayed it as ancestral regalia. Descendants of its wearers sang the sale-price during the procession until guards drove them away. The kings kept the collar; the song made its supposed honour impossible to hear innocently.
^atlas-i-m03

## The House That Chose the Stranger

**Scattering; one riverward migrant household.** A widow, bereaved in-law, unrelated children and a foreign debtor stayed together after their host divided. Blood relatives refused them food unless the stranger was abandoned. They chose the smaller household and passed one cooking vessel to an adopted heir. Later claims by several cultures do not make them the parents of several nations. The biblical household grammar is retained; generic African or Indigenous attribution is not asserted.
^atlas-ii-h03

## The Well That Answered Care

**Northern fission; one Ahdari border well.** Migrants and residents cleared silt, repaired the lining and kept a lower recovery basin untouched. A freely offered first cup kept the spout clear; crossing the protected basin clouded it. One household lost access after refusing repair, though its sick members were not morally guilty of the water's response. This is a world-native local compact, not a generic borrowed Indigenous water religion or universal guest-law.
^atlas-ii-l02

## The Unlisted Companion

**First Kings; a southern inheritance house.** A ruler erased a brother's service from the genealogy after attempting to kill him. Later heirs inherited property made possible by the excluded household. An Envy cult used the suppressed grievance to demand the murder of replacement tenants. Recovering the brother's name corrected a lie without making that revenge just.
^atlas-ii-i01

## The Keeper Who Would Not Rise

**Early Hearths; one granary settlement.** The keeper who had saved winter stores refused the repair due at thaw. Dependants begged him to rise while a wall softened. Its collapse killed families and spoiled seed. Later songs called his stillness wisdom; farmers left his feast seat empty to remember that refusing action can be a chosen harm.
^atlas-ii-i05

## The Road of Borrowed Gifts

**Early city-state medicine routes.** A messenger accepted gifts from successive hosts to reach an ill child. Trading away a borrowed tool saved time but left its owner's well unrepaired; another child died there. The traveller returned to make restitution. The medicine road therefore remembers generosity and the permanent harm caused by treating borrowed help as free property.
^atlas-ii-l03

## The Guest at the Empty Signature

**Early royal marriage courts.** A court left a bride's place unsigned so her relatives could claim she had freely entered a marriage already enforced by guards. Refusal brought beating and threatened sale; sexual access was treated as the groom's property. Women exposed the empty place during a feast and helped one bride flee. A Lust Mask lost that route without ending the court's other coercions.
^atlas-ii-i04

## The Fountain and the Foreign Workers

**City-States; mixed coastal quarter.** Foreign repairers were accused of poisoning a fountain. An interpreter separated a description of broken pump parts from an alleged confession. Several workers escaped execution, but one had already been killed. The fountain's separate local response supported the account without becoming an infallible judge of foreigners.
^atlas-ii-l04

## The First Foreclosure

**City-States; competing grain towns.** A creditor converted pledged grain into claims upon families, selling children to recover a loan after the harvest failed. Relatives attacked the storehouse and found that the debt had already been sold elsewhere. A Greed Mask benefited from that mobility. Breaking one granary did not restore the separated households.
^atlas-ii-i03

## The Family of Stars

**Age of Stars; compiling Atherian cities.** Scholars joined local powers into a civic family while suppressing older tales of mutilation and parent–child war. Officials made households perform the new kinship order and punished rival festivals. Later beautiful images concealed the violence both of the old divine-generation traditions and of the human compilation.
^atlas-ii-m05

## The Furnace League

**Heroic-maritime recovery; c. 1800–1450 BR.** Material smith towns pooled ore, charcoal and convoy defence. Captive labour helped their furnaces prosper. When a ruler diverted food to an expansion fleet, workers broke the levy but lost two towns in reprisal. This was an ordinary industrial league, not Orqad's Titan furnaces, a Shell refuge or the deep Ash-Well Crown.

The league's work connected mining households, charcoal districts and harbours whose buyers could be far from the furnaces. Diverting food therefore damaged more than a single workplace. Families sent workers elsewhere, and fugitives carried techniques into settlements that had formerly depended on the league. Its memory belongs within [[Southern Settlement and Maritime Atheria]], while the identities of its ruler and ruined towns remain separate from the new eastern port histories.
^atlas-tf-06

## The Well of Three Laws

**Northern fission; c. 1900–1750 BR.** Three neighbouring groups used one well under incompatible inheritance and hospitality customs. A killing during drought forced them to divide times of access rather than pretend that all three laws had become one. The compromise saved herds and entrenched an unequal burden on a smaller household. It did not found Covenant law.
^atlas-tf-07

## The City That Conquered Its Ancestors

**Age of Stars; Caleran republican formation.** A civic regime absorbed older founder houses, then backdated their consent to make conquest resemble reunion. One family retained a banquet vessel depicting the defeated hostages. Its survival exposed invented precedence without disproving every later benefit of citizenship.

The remembered changes belong after the formation of the actual twelve-city Compact. Officials recast older founders with Caleran symbols and titles, suppressed contradictory ceremonies and imprisoned or killed resisting custodians. Local accounts preserved different kinships and sequences beneath that public arrangement. [[The Celestial Compact and the Caleran Republic]] supplies the broader history of incorporation and competing civic memories; the vessel's particular ancestry remains a claim within this tradition.
^atlas-tf-12

## The Keeper of Borrowed Grain

**Age of Glory; a famine province.** A minority administrator kept people alive by opening grain stores in return for land and compelled service. The saved households lost independence and passed the debt to children. Imperial Lives praised the rationing; village songs counted farms surrendered for bread. Preservation and dispossession were both real.
^atlas-b3-02

## The Crown That Would Not Conquer

**Age of Glory; provincial sanctuary.** A ruler carried a sacred object toward a conquest it had never been commissioned to support. A handler who knowingly forced its use died at the threshold. The army retreated, but the object did not proclaim the enemy innocent or award the province to another dynasty.
^atlas-b3-03

## The Emperor after the Emperor

**Age of Glory; provincial ruler cult.** After an emperor died, officials standardized his public face and demanded the same acts of loyalty. A household which remembered his living cruelty refused the new benign image. Punishment fed the Mask that had attached to the cult; the dead emperor's human soul did not inhabit the statue.
^atlas-b3-04

## The Burial Hands

**Underground; one inland purge.** A burial team assembled by accident carried a misnamed body out of a prison yard. The mistake gave one family hope while hiding another's loss. A worker returned through the cordon to correct it and was executed. The corrected grave mattered because two households could finally mourn their own dead.
^atlas-b3-07

## The Clerk Who Added Words

**Underground memory after Legalization.** A clerk combined several confessions into a heroic Life and added a denunciation never spoken by its witness. Officials used the addition against a living family. The clerk's later correction preserved the real confession but could not undo the torture and death already authorized by the false sentence.
^atlas-b3-08

## The Penitent and the Other Face

**Conversion; one provincial governorship.** The governor authorized torture, judicial murder, confiscation, forced conversion and a purification which killed his own heir. He repented and relinquished power. Victims refused reconciliation; laws and a predatory public likeness continued to harm people. Neither the heir's death nor the repentance erased earlier innocent blood.
^atlas-b3-10

## The Border Lamp and the Keelman

**Long Frontier; a Zanthiri-facing coast.** A ransom broker and a former raider shared a boat after cheating rival employers. Both had participated in slavery rather than merely witnessing it. Their attempt to free one captive betrayed a former lover who still knew their routes. The later comic voyages preserve the friendship without cleansing its history.
^atlas-b3-11

## The Physician and the Interpreter

**Long Frontier; mixed Velian border towns.** A physician dismissed a patient's account while the interpreter concealed uncertainty to preserve authority. Treatment worsened the injury. The pair then travelled together to seek help, each forced to admit a failure the other could not repair. Their continuing work never restored the patient's lost movement.
^atlas-b3-13

## The Linen Oath

**First frontier conquests; one walled town.** A household sheltered enemy fugitives behind a linen sign while neighbours prepared denunciation. The town was actually destroyed. Some sheltered people survived; other households died in reprisals. The rescued could not turn their sign into a promise that every building bearing it would be spared.
^atlas-b3-14

## The Fifth Translation

**Late Long Frontier; a separate translation workshop.** Five workers produced an elegant edition of a dangerous manual. One omitted an awkward warning to make the argument consistent, and readers were physically injured when they used it. The surviving translators circulated a clumsy corrective version and lost their patron's favour. The workshop was not the Sealed-Door Reader's college.
^atlas-b3-15

## The Lamp Answered Twice

**Split-era divided town.** Two congregations received useful responses from one sacred object under different immediate needs. A custodian then tried to force a third answer as a doctrinal verdict and was burned. The earlier aid remained real without proving either church's whole case.
^atlas-b4-05

## The Two Weeping Advocates

**Post-Split siege country.** Families endured starvation, rape and the deaths of children before rival preachers offered signs of consolation. Two Envy Masks exploited the real grief, directing survivors toward retaliatory killing. Exposing the images did not restore the victims or make the grief foolish.
^atlas-b4-06

## The Ransomed Standard

**High Noon through Qarath.** A company of professionals, hostages and exiles survived changing employers until failed ransom brought mutilation and execution. Several members murdered surrendered rivals, then concealed the order beneath a heroic standard. Its later bearer kept the torn hostage ties attached where a victory streamer should hang.
^atlas-b4-07

## The Woman Who Returned the Keys

**High-Noon provincial siege.** A woman entered the enemy camp through calculated deception, killed and beheaded the commander herself and returned with the head. Her city survived through further fighting and losses. She later relinquished its keys rather than found a dynasty, leaving the deed neither bloodless nor a coronation story.
^atlas-b4-08

## The Poor Engineer's Mark

**Post-Qarath lesser city.** A poor wall-worker repaired a pressure failure which would have destroyed a district. Officials stole the credit and withheld the promised wages. The worker died in poverty; a repair mark on a hidden stone let descendants recover the achievement without recovering the lost life.
^atlas-b4-09

## The Physician and the Wonder-Worker

**Grey Sweat; one funeral river town.** A physician and a wonder-worker saved different patients and failed others while bodies decomposed faster than they could be buried. Officials executed suspected carriers. Each healer later rejected followers who claimed that the other's dead proved fraud. Their rivalry became cooperation without explaining the plague's end.
^atlas-b4-11

## The Road That Paid Twice

**Black Wind years; merchant and farming corridor.** A road community paid one power for passage and another to refrain from destroying it. Refusal meant massacre, enslavement or seizure of dependants. Merchants called the second payment waste while farming families remembered the hostages. The compromise bought survival, not a clean liberation.
^atlas-b4-13

## The Feast Found under the Floor

**Held-Proof cult inquiry before 1360.** Investigators exposed concealed access through which custodians and their households took offerings supposedly consumed by an image. The ruler executed the guilty household and destroyed the image, punishing dependants collectively. The Gluttony Mask survived through other routes. The tale can condemn the fraud and question the executions without deleting either.
^atlas-b4-16

## Four other mortal lives

**The Roadless Rider — western Well-Tarkhan circuits, c. 820–870 AR.** A camel-route guide freed convoy captives and became a protector of mobile households. A marriage tied the guide to a settled well ruler. Refusing that ruler's war preserved independence but abandoned the spouse's town to a punitive raid. The later song disputes the choice without turning it into a horse-cavalry origin before the First Catch.
^atlas-primarch-v

**The Winter Executioner — baptized Ättar border halls, c. 1030–1090 AR.** A ruler's trusted enforcer killed an allied household under a sanction later exposed as false. Loyalty had made the enforcer useful and morally careless. He broke with the ruler, supported survivors and died without their forgiveness. Wolf imagery proves neither lunar sainthood nor inherited guilt.
^atlas-primarch-vi

**The Beloved Standard — one late-frontier command, c. 850–884 AR.** A popular commander saved mixed villages and won friendships across rank. Fear of being replaced led him to murder allied officers and attempt a provincial seizure. The revolt failed; those who had loved him inherited both genuine rescues and betrayal. It was not the hidden cause of the Fall or a universal Heresy.
^atlas-primarch-xvi

**The Two Envoys — Sestaran trading houses, c. 1160–1200 AR.** Two human agents shared a public diplomatic identity. Each had separate memories, loyalties and a complete soul. One sold a convoy believing the other would secretly rescue it; the second had accepted a conflicting commission. People died through their incompatible plans. The surviving records prove two persons, not an omniscient order behind history.
^atlas-primarch-xx

## Short praise which grows into local memory

The **child of the eighth well** carried water to a household excluded by adults and lost the family donkey as punishment. The **widow of the lean ox** refused to surrender her last draught animal to a prosperous war captain and was driven from a field she had kept alive. The **interpreter who lied once** saved a hostage by falsifying a message, then lost a friend when the lie brought retaliation. The **poor wall-mender** received a burial by the repaired wall but no wages in life. These belong to separate early settlement and city traditions; none is an unnamed founder of all civilization.

Other Praise entries deepen existing lives: dry coals belong beside the Last Cart; the confessed scouting failure belongs beside Seven Who Took the Wrong Road; iron carried to a feast belongs beside the Crownless cycle; the hostage who named children belongs beside the Oak-and-Glass fosterlings; the houseless key-bearer and separated witnesses belong beside the Bent-Key city; the singer and last milestone belong beside the survey road. They do not become duplicate heroes.
