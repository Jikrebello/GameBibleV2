# The Armour Worn by Strangers

> **Stories told.** This is local lore of an uncontacted country. Its tellers and material customs exist there in 1360, but no transmission to the known world is established.

## Historical setting

**post-Giant local generations; not securely synchronized to AR.** far-distant cypress commonwealth and its adjoining Egg White courts. This tradition belongs to [[Distant Lands and Otherworld Histories]]; its cultural bearers are the uncontacted Orphaned peoples of the Last Houses.

It is told among far-distant mobile households whose defenders inherit altered armour and unfinished duties. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## The tale

A defender died holding a pass long enough for her household to escape. Her armour was too small for the next bearer, a broad-shouldered cook. The family cut its sides, added plates and asked him to finish the road she had begun.

He did. Victories brought land and honour, and custodians began calling him by the dead defender's name. They ordered him to marry her widower, repeat her gestures and avenge enemies he had never met. When he refused, they said the armour had rejected him. The next bearer accepted every demand and repeated an atrocity the first defender had once committed against a surrendered village.

Generations of wearers followed: some brave, some cruel, some children forced into plates, some strangers who wore the armour only for one journey. Custodians taught that the office made them one returning hero. Bearers knew better because every alteration pinched a different scar.

At last two claimants split the armour between them. One defended the household; the other went to compensate the village. Neither possessed a complete suit, but both fulfilled part of the inherited duty. The households began dancing the armour in several bodies instead of seating one person on the dead hero's chair.

The cook's descendants always wear the widened side plates. They joke that heroic destiny first arrived because the original suit pinched at supper. The laughter protects a bodily truth against custodians who prefer one ageless silhouette. Armour must fit the living.

## Why it is remembered

It celebrates inherited responsibility while defending the living bearer's identity. A person may finish another's work without becoming that person, inheriting guilt or surrendering marriage and desire to a costume.

## How tellers differ

Custodian epics insist one spirit continued through every wearer. Bearer songs list changes in height, voice and appetite. Village versions say restitution, not battle, was the only duty worth inheriting.

## What remains

Visibly resized armour is danced by several living claimants. No performer wears the whole suit or the dead defender's face.

## History and belief
