# The Two Tags on One Dead Man

> **Stories told.** This is an unfinished 1360 tradition. Current rumours and the observable practice are established; no future discovery, victory, judgement or fate is implied.

## Historical setting

**1297–1360 AR; Reformation and unfinished present.** known-world towns and Anchor-facing harbours. This tradition belongs to [[The Age of Held Proofs]]; its cultural bearers are living townspeople and conflicting current witnesses.

It is told in Reformation workhouse towns and, in 1360, among two families facing the consequences of an old exchange. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## The tale

Two debt workers shared similar names. One had children who would be seized with him; the other had no dependants but was too sick to finish his term. They exchanged neck tags so the first family could flee while officials believed its father remained at work.

The sick man died under the borrowed name after overseers denied treatment for malingering. His body was sent to the escaped family, which buried him secretly rather than expose the fraud. His own sister received an empty coffin and spent years believing he had been sold elsewhere.

The escapee lived, raised his children and never told them another person lay beneath their family stone. Funeral wax eventually fused the two corroded tags. When descendants discovered them, one family demanded the grave and name restored. The other feared that confession would revive an old debt now held by a broker who had bought the workhouse claims.

The broker has threatened living dependants. One descendant wants to break the tags and preserve both families. Another believes continued silence repeats the original abandonment. No remedy has yet been chosen.

The dead man's sister is known only through a lullaby both families still sing with different names. If they meet before deciding the grave, the song may establish kinship—or prove merely that workhouse children shared music in the same dormitory. Either discovery would change who is entitled to mourn and who must answer for the years of silence.

## Why it is remembered

The clever swap first appears to save a family, then acquires the weight of a neglected dying man and a sister denied mourning. The present mystery matters because every truthful act may harm someone who did not make the bargain.

## How tellers differ

One household calls the dead worker a willing rescuer. His sister's descendants say he was too ill to consent freely. Workhouse records record only one name and therefore settle nothing.

## What remains

Two neck tags remain fused by funeral wax. Scraping them apart may destroy both inscriptions.

## History and belief

The practice, landscape or physical object described here has a place in the present world. Statements that it is the original relic, that a voice is the original dead person, or that a victory proves divine approval belong to the tradition unless a separate history establishes them.

The tale stops at the present.
