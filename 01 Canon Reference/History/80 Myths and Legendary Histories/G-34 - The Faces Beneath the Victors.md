# The Faces Beneath the Victors

> **Stories told.** The local telling and the material or customary remainder below are established. A claimed original object, miraculous response, famous identity or supernatural explanation is not authenticated merely by this article.

## Historical setting

**c. 910–951 AR.** Thalmyric refuge mountains and reconquered provincial towns. This tradition belongs to [[The Fall and the Long Reconquest]]; its cultural bearers are refugees, occupation-born households and returners.

It is told in Fall and Reconquest portrait halls, especially among mixed families painted out of official ancestry. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## The tale

A general commissioned a hall showing the liberation of seven towns. His closest companion, an artist who had fought beside him, painted what she saw: the general's missing fingers, soldiers married to occupation-born spouses, refugee children in enemy clothing and prisoners killed after surrender.

The general demanded clean triumph. The artist refused and threatened to reveal that he had ordered one massacre after the town gates opened. He loved her, feared her and strangled her during their final quarrel. His officers called the death illness. Court painters covered the wounds, lightened faces, removed mixed marriages and painted the general's hand whole.

The hall became a school of patriotism. Families whose ancestors had disappeared from it still came to point at empty spaces. Smoke from a later fire darkened the upper paint and revealed other eyes beneath. Restorers found the murdered artist's repeated signature hidden in Glass-court patterns under every scene, including one portrait painted after her death.

Some believed her spirit continued the work. Others found an apprentice who had copied the mark in revenge. Whatever the cause, the restored faces changed the hall from a procession of victors into a room where concealed witnesses looked back.

The general's descendants tried to close the hall. Mixed families occupied it overnight and cooked beneath the portraits, letting new smoke expose further paint. They did not need the murdered artist to be present for her refusal to remain active among the living.

## Why it is remembered

The tragedy is intimate: a liberator murders someone he loves to preserve the version of himself he wants history to see. The reappearing families make restoration an encounter with persons, not merely pigments.

## How tellers differ

Military Lives blame an enemy assassin. Artist guilds call the general murderer. Mixed households disagree whether all underfaces should be exposed when some living families fear renewed persecution.

## What remains

Smoke or warmth brings wounded hands and mixed households through the later paint of several victory portraits.
