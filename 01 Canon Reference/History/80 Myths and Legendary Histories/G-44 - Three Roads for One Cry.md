# Three Roads for One Cry

> **Stories told.** The local telling and the material or customary remainder below are established. A claimed original object, miraculous response, famous identity or supernatural explanation is not authenticated merely by this article.

## Historical setting

**612–884 AR; Long Frontier.** Atherian–Covenant frontier districts. This tradition belongs to [[The Long Frontier and the First Unsealing]]; its cultural bearers are mixed provincial, Ahdari, Velian and Jirahar communities.

It is told among Long-Frontier diaspora families, ransom routes and runners who carry news from prisons. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## The tale

A captive woman sent a plea naming the children held with her and the price demanded for each. A town archivist suppressed it because acknowledging the prison would expose a failed treaty. Her brother stole the message but knew one courier could be stopped.

He divided the plea among three strangers. A shepherd carried the names, a thief carried the place and a widow carried the demanded price. They took different roads and promised to meet at a kiln fire. The shepherd was killed by patrols. The thief traded the place-name to save himself, then doubled back to warn the prisoners. The widow arrived alone with a price nobody could use.

Families reconstructed the plea from what each route had touched: the shepherd's coloured cord, the thief's warning scratched on a trough, and the widow's memory of the children's order. Help reached the prison too late for several captives. The woman who wrote the message had died, but two named children survived because the rescuers knew whom to seek.

Afterward, no single runner carried a whole prison cry. The custom prevented easy suppression but sometimes slowed rescue. Every relay remembers that division can preserve truth and still cost lives.

The two surviving children met the widow years later. One thanked her for remembering the price; the other asked why she had not carried a name. She had no answer except the agreement made in panic at the first fire. The question altered the relay thereafter.

## Why it is remembered

The story turns transmission into a race whose stakes are particular children rather than an archive's completeness. It honours imperfect carriers without pretending delay was harmless.

## How tellers differ

Runner guilds say all three strangers volunteered. Prison families say the brother threatened them. Thief songs claim he died returning; town records call him the first betrayer.

## What remains

Three runners exchange red, grey and white cords at roadside fires. None knows the whole message until the recipients assemble it.
