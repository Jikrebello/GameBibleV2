# The Companions Who Entered by the Low Door

> **Stories told.** The local telling and the material or customary remainder below are established. A claimed original object, miraculous response, famous identity or supernatural explanation is not authenticated merely by this article.

## Historical setting

**c. 96–54 BR; Age of Glory.** The tradition arose in Lacaris and the eastern Atherian provinces. It belongs to the [[Atherian Imperial Age]] and was preserved especially among provincial service households.

It is told in Atherian provincial towns whose ancestors entered imperial service from kitchens, stables, prisons and hostage houses. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## Historical basis

Provincial records establish that six servants saved Magistrate Temar Avol during the Lacaris riot around 96 BR and subsequently entered imperial service. The same record preserves their names, later offices, involvement in unlawful killings and hostage-taking, the betrayal of a family refuge, and Helon Var's execution. Their careers can be followed into the middle of the first century BR.

The surviving evidence does not establish every private motive, conversation or emotional reconciliation found in later songs. Versions containing five or seven companions are later regional reshaping of a historically attested company of six. For the evidence and the companions' individual careers, see [[The Companions Who Entered by the Low Door - Imperial History|the imperial history of the Low Door Companions]].

## The tale

During a riot, six servants hid beneath a magistrate's dining platform. When fire reached the shutters, they escaped through the refuse door and dragged the magistrate with them. He rewarded them with places in his provincial household: scout, singer, armourer, interpreter, cook and keeper of hostages.

Service carried them upward. They ended a mutiny by taking officers' children. They raided a village after its surrender and called the dead rebels. When a fortress governor threatened both their birth quarter and the imperial granary, one companion revealed the refuge where her own cousins were hiding so the army would spare the larger town. The cousins were killed. The town survived and praised the six as low-born saviours.

Years later they commanded armies and married into houses that once refused them at the front gate. Their friendship did not survive their advancement. The hostage keeper freed three children and was executed for theft of imperial property. The interpreter wrote the earliest account of the betrayal, then removed her own part. The cook kept the bowl of the youngest murdered cousin and made every officer eat from it before receiving higher rank.

Later Lives turned the low door into a sign of humility and omitted the village, the hostages and the refuge. Provincial songs remember that they entered history by saving one man and climbed through choices that left others beneath the table.

At their final shared meal, only four attended. Nobody knew whether the missing two stayed away from shame, anger or death. The cook placed six bowls anyway, including the murdered cousin's, and served the same thin soup they had eaten as servants.

## Why it is remembered

It offers social ascent without pretending gifted outsiders remain innocent once power accepts them. Families love the companions for proving origin need not decide a life and fear them for proving injury does not prevent someone inflicting injury.

## How tellers differ

The historical company numbered six. Some later imperial versions reduce them to five and make the dead cousins enemy agents. Hostage-country songs count seven, adding a child who never forgave them. No surviving telling reliably identifies which companion first ordered an unlawful killing.

## What remains

Old command houses retain a deliberately low lintel. New officers must cross it carrying a child's plain bowl and cannot wear rank while stooping.
