# Saints and Sinners of the Chronicles

**Saints and Sinners of the Chronicles** gathers the holy and the damned dead whom the churches, prison roads, forge towns, refugee houses and harbour cities of the Known World remember from the Underground Centuries to 1360 AR. In the common speech of every Redeemer communion, a saint is a person the faithful believe the Lord received and sometimes sends back on an errand: a crook-shouldered porter seen holding a burning doorway, a hanged chain-tender walking the harbour wall in a storm. A sinner is a person whose name goes on doing harm after death, through cults that copy the crimes, apparitions that demand them and objects that seem to remember their maker's purpose.

Few of these people were simple. The Witness at the Red Passage hid murders before dying in a doorway. The Clerk of a Hundred Wells saved a famine country and then would not give its wells back. The General of the Glass Court strangled the one friend who told him the truth, and the Saint of Ashen Wings bought a people's freedom with prisoners fed into a broken door between worlds. Each Life below is told as its chronicles tell it: what the person did, the wonder the witnesses kept, what it changed in the history of the world, and the quarrel still fought over relics, feasts and graves.

## How the chronicles keep them

Most of the people in this article are known by an epithet. Prison rolls burned; frontier registers were scraped and written over; three towns gave one martyr three baptismal names, and the calendar-makers settled the matter by printing none. Families sometimes keep the private name and refuse to say it before strangers. The epithets travel instead—the Red Passage, the Ninth Bastion, the Black Eaves—carried by songs, feast rolls and the names of inns.

Witnesses describe a returning saint as recognisable. The crooked shoulder is still crooked, the burned hands still burned; the saint wears the clothes and carries the tools of a working life, and may come with birds, wings, a light that casts no shadow or a fire that gives no smoke. Sinners return differently. Their cults repeat the founding crime as proof of loyalty, and people report the sinner's face wherever the old crime is done again.

The churches have never agreed on how to tell the two apart. Before 1015 a bishop or council could add a name to the feast roll, and a town could simply begin keeping one. After [[The Great Split — Two Crowns, One Fire|the Great Split]], the Eclessian relic courts test every claim by protocol, with their five tests of sainthood, sworn witnesses and long waiting, while the Patriarchate of Veridun receives a well-witnessed wonder as its own proof. The Velian congregations keep their own calendar. Covenant chroniclers record many of the same frontier figures as neighbours, heretics or enemies, and Banu Mizan jurists cite some of them in water law without calling them holy. Since the False Lady of Bruckmoor was exposed in 1243 (see [[The Hunger Wars]]), Eclessian courts have distrusted any apparition that weeps, pays or demands, and that caution has cost several claimants their hearing.

One teaching is shared across the communions: a saint cannot fall. When a figure wearing a saint's face demands revenge, the churches say something else is wearing it—an Idol's Mask, a Patron's hired apparition, a residue repeating old habits. Sinner cults turn the teaching around and call their master's appearances proof of his cause. Much of what follows was written, rewritten and burned by people fighting that argument.

## Underground and Conversion (c. 150–398 AR)

For nearly three centuries the Redeemer's people were outlaws, then for a century the empire's own faith, then, under Vestian, its compulsory one. The saints of these years were prisoners, porters and clerks. Their sinners learned that escape, miracles and the names of the dead could all be sold. See [[The Ember Underground]] and [[The Conversion of the Empire]].

### The Lady of the Mire Keys (c. 150–240 AR)

The prison ports of the Underground ran on boats: flat barges carried prisoners between river gaols, and a clerk who knew which locks flooded at which tide could get a person out of almost anywhere. The Lady's Life, assembled by prisoners' descendants from boat ledgers and petitions, makes her the daughter of such a boatman who rose to keep sentences. She sold escape to persecuted believers, political prisoners and common thieves alike, then reported a chosen few, kept their fee and took promotion for the recapture. Once she held the sentence books she wrote new crimes to fill them. Shelter without a stamped sponsor became vagrancy, a woman who fled an assault was charged with absence from custody, and a crust given to a fugitive became theft of state grain. Guards were paid in access to the prisoners.

The prison songs keep her alive. They describe a crowned woman whose body ends in a coil of keys beneath a drowned city, where every key opens one cell by closing another. Escapees in later persecutions said that a pardon offered in her name was always real; someone else simply went into the cell.

A later Life makes her a secret rescuer forced to betray a few to save the many, and prisoners' descendants call it her second sale. One provincial guest-law still forbids a host to "close the second door" when buying a prisoner's release, and the boat ledgers survive, listing the same names as escaped and recaptured under the same seal.

### The Witness at the Red Passage (c. 180–226 AR)

The Witness began as a prison porter, hired because a malformed shoulder ruled out the legion. Porters learned which chains fitted which wrists, which guards took coin and which prisoners would be dead before trial. During a transfer riot the porter shut one gate to save a smaller group and left the other yard to the soldiers, who beat the prisoners there and executed several. Inside the escaping party, frightened rescuers murdered their bound guards, a captured informer and two prisoners wrongly accused of collaboration. The porter struck those killings from the escape register, because the truth would have broken the prison network.

The porter spent the next years carrying bread, letters and burial names between gaols, and tried without success to put the murdered prisoners back on the record. Then a purge drove several households toward a narrow red-painted service passage. The porter held it, knowing the stand would be fatal, while civilians and prisoners ran through. Captured, the Witness was tortured and killed. The Life has always kept the abandoned yard and the hidden murders beside the final stand, which is why prison families trust it.

The wonders came later. Dying prisoners in later persecutions said they saw a crook-shouldered figure holding a red door open on the road of the dead, while winged clerks gathered names from a burning roll. When admirers formed a fighting brotherhood and murdered suspected informers under the Witness's banner, survivors reported the Witness among them, rebuking the killers in the words once used of the hidden murders. The brotherhood dissolved within a year.

Courthouse Lives make the Witness a flawless martyr and a man; prison songs make her a woman and a coward who became brave, and [[G-24 - The Red Door and the Names Left Behind|The Red Door and the Names Left Behind]] keeps that version. Magistrates have called the cult dangerous, and sceptics call the Witness a composite of several prisons. A red-painted door hangs unhinged in an inland courthouse. Once a year it is carried flat through the streets while two lists are read, the saved and the abandoned. No lawful order claims it.

### The Clerk Who Named the Dyer (c. 244–267 AR; the reliquary, c. 404)

The clerk was the child of a kiln-tender in an inland prison-and-dye district, where the rivers ran blue and red below the vats. As a young prisoner-clerk under torture, he gave one name to stop a beating. The named worker, a dyer, was arrested and died in custody. Released, the clerk recanted in public, found the dyer's household, gave them his wages and began copying the names the prison courts struck from their rolls. The family took the money and never declared the death forgiven.

Beneath the old civic court lay a binding from the Age of Stars, a shut room the old priests had sealed with sworn testimony freely given. The persecuting magistrates had reworked it to run on erased names, forced confessions and the breath of the dying. When it began to fail they demanded that the clerk become its living mouth, because his copied roll of names could steady it. He refused, exposed the murders and moved prisoners out through the kiln service-ways. Officials sealed him inside the chamber as it broke, and he died as it closed.

Around 404, under the converted empire, custodians of a new reliquary began issuing commands in the dead clerk's voice and using them to name enemies of the state. Arrests followed the voice. Witnesses to the reliquary's last night said a second voice spoke in the chamber, the clerk's own, calling the reliquary a counterfeit and saying the dyer's name before its own. The reliquary cracked, its keepers were carried out, and the purges it had guided stopped in that province.

One church reads the dyer's name before the martyr's title at the annual reading; another reverses the order. [[G-39 - First the Betrayed, Then the Witness|First the Betrayed, Then the Witness]] keeps the dyer's mother and her lamp.

### The Borrowed Martyr of the Quarry Road (c. 272–338 AR)

An obscure prison worker who carried food, messages and the names of the dead between quarry cells and their families was informed upon, tortured into a written denial that copyists still dispute, and executed after refusing a second demand. The family was refused the body and dug a careful grave anyway. After legalisation, the quarry town's crowned icon and a road sanctuary's weeping image each claimed the martyr, performed wonders, and fought: a recanter was murdered, pilgrims died on the road, and the family's house was burned. The grave the family prepared has never held a body. The story belongs as much to the Idols as to the saints and is told in [[The Hunger Wars]] and [[G-70 - The Empty Grave and the Two False Shrines|The Empty Grave and the Two False Shrines]].

### The Finder of the Cast-Out (c. 304–323 AR)

Soon after legalisation, a wealthy household with new friends in the Church feared that its line would die out. The lord and his wife compelled an enslaved stable worker into the lord's bed under an inheritance arrangement she could not refuse. She bore a son, and the household called him its continuity. When the wife later bore a child of her own, she beat the stable worker, persuaded the lord to void every promise, and had mother and son driven into a salt-and-thorn waste with too little water. The lord sent riders to stop them coming back.

The boy was dying under a thorn bush when a pale ground bird walked out of the glare and spoke to the mother in a clear human voice. It scraped the sand from a stone lid where road-builders had once covered a cistern, named the coercion and the expulsion as the household's crime, and pointed her toward a caravan track. Carriers on that track gave them the rest of their water and a place on the wagons. Mother and son lived. The other enslaved women of the estate saw no bird.

Hospital orders still repeat a clause, "need does not end when inheritance changes," and most attribute it to a charitable widow who sheltered the pair afterwards, as in [[G-40 - The Shelter That Outlived Its Heir|The Shelter That Outlived Its Heir]]. Abolitionist preachers tell the bird as an angel; sceptics tell of a lucky cistern; the household's descendants called the whole story slander for two hundred years.

### The Preacher of the Second Gospel (c. 330–389 AR)

The Preacher began as a temple copyist who joined the Church because its new congregations fed displaced workers whom both the imperial and the old-cult authorities had abandoned. He became a magnificent preacher and organiser of soup kitchens, fever wards and lodging houses. Privately he taught that every real power deserved negotiated worship. A bishop removed him from public teaching after witnesses proved coerced confessions and a secret rite that addressed the White Flame as an appetite to be fed.

The correction was public and mild. He took it as proof that the Church feared honest revelation. In the stripped sanctuaries of the closing temples he asked the dark for an answer, and something answered; his disciples said three voices did, and that they disagreed. From those answers he built the **Second Gospel**, a counter-church whose rule was that a rite which works is a rite which is true. He presided over its first captive sacrifice himself. When another teacher refused, he ordered that a child be used in the first possession trial, and he had a former companion killed to seize the bishop's disciplinary record.

The counter-church worked wonders. Fevers broke in its wards, lamps lit themselves at its night offices, and in the years of temple closures thousands of displaced cult workers found in it a church that paid. Its success sharpened the Church's own question about who may authenticate a sign, and made several bishops readier to let the emperor answer it. Copies of the Second Gospel were said to speak in his voice when read alone after dark, and carters on one inland road claimed to have seen a figure with ash-dark wings burn a wagon-load of annotated copies centuries later.

Counter-churches remember a persecuted visionary, Eclessian preachers the first great sinner of the converted age, and some historians a reformer the bishops mishandled. Others think several preachers have been folded into one. In 1360 three mutually exclusive "complete" Second Gospels circulate, while hospitals use a fourth fragment—a fever broth and a line forbidding service-debt for food or medicine—without knowing whose it was. That fragment is the subject of [[G-25 - The Ragged Gospel in the Hospital Apron|The Ragged Gospel in the Hospital Apron]].

### The Singer at the False Choir (c. 382–391 AR)

In the years of Vestian's compulsion, a newly converted civic hymn guild was given the confiscated house and salaries of an older temple choir. One ambitious singer bought his advancement by naming five of the old custodians, including two genuine converts whose property local officials wanted. The five were tortured for further names and executed, and their families were barred from collecting the bodies. The guild then set the confiscation into a dedication hymn as proof that the city had been cleansed without innocent blood.

At the dedication the singer stopped before the altered verse, spoke the first victim's name and begged not to be allowed another profitable lie. Witnesses saw a brightness above the choir rail, shaped like a great rook in black and white, carrying a coal that gave no smoke. It touched the singer's mouth and his writing hand. The pain was public; he cried out before the governor and the guild. Then he spoke all five names and how each had died, confessed, surrendered his office and went to prison, where he disappears from the record.

Some choirs call the event a miraculous confession; temple descendants call it a church spectacle staged to keep the confiscated house, and physicians a guilty man's seizure. The hymn survives with a deliberate silent measure, kept in [[G-41 - The Silence Kept for Five|The Silence Kept for Five]]. Some choirs read the five names into it, others keep it empty, and the Church learned from the quarrel that a hymn could not absolve killings done in its name.

## The Long Frontier (398–905 AR)

For three centuries the remnants of the empire and the rising Covenant shared a frontier of wells, forts, ransom roads and mixed towns. Its pressures—water, quarantine, fortification, revolt, the closed doors between worlds—produced the densest crowd of saints and sinners in the chronicles. Eight great lives overlapped: an older generation between about 641 and 735 that shaped law, forts, revolt and quarantine, and a younger one between about 708 and 804 that shaped routes, warnings and refuge craft. Each worked in a separate place and cause. Some quarrelled in life; most knew one another only by reputation. See [[The Long Frontier and the First Unsealing]].

### The Mother at the Well (603 AR)

Rumaitha bint Mujalib was about sixty-five when the bound keeper of Bir Qassim would not draw for her household. On the sixth night her son read their safe conduct at the well-mouth, and at dawn the escort that should have disarmed them offered to carry them away. She could not sit a camel. By the Sealed Blood's account she ordered her son to go, saying that the blood had to outlive the well, and stayed with eight others who could not ride and fifteen drovers who would not leave them. She died of thirst two nights later beside a well her father's servant kept full. A column killed those who outlived her, and fourteen of the drovers with them, for giving water to rebels. The Reading of the Wells reads her name first and the drovers' last. Witnessed-Seal formalists say the keeper did what it was bound to do. Sealed-Blood households answer by setting out a filled skin for any stranger, and by drawing every bucket with their own hands. See [[The Disputed Fourth Seal]].

### The Old Traveller with Seven Birds (c. 626–641 AR)

In the first decades of the frontier, a border governor kept hostages from the last war behind the only winter pass, in a half-ruined Giant toll fortress whose hostage standard and tally stone were said to whisper. The safe-conducts the hostages had signed contained a clause that made the governor owner of their labour, movement and children. A relief force of soldiers, relatives, porters and hired engineers met an old man on the road below the pass, with seven small birds riding his staff and shoulders. He read them the hidden clause, laid out three ways to attack, and named the captives who must not be spent. He would not choose for them.

The commander chose a night assault. It shattered the tally stone and freed most of the prisoners, but a fire started too early burned captives in one tower, and soldiers killed surrendering guards in revenge. Devotional tellers make the traveller an angel and the birds his attendants; officers' manuals make him a scout with trained birds; families of the burned call him the commander's excuse. Frontier officers still call an unendorsed set of choices "the pilgrim's table," and [[G-42 - The Supper of the Unchosen Roads|The Supper of the Unchosen Roads]] keeps his seven pieces of bread.

### The Clerk of a Hundred Wells (c. 641–708 AR) and the Bitter Reaper (c. 676–735 AR)

These two quarrelled in life, and the frontier's law of water and plague came out of the quarrel.

The Clerk copied well allocations for wages so low that he slept in the archive. In a drought made worse by siege, he devised a common register that stopped the great households from taking all the water. It saved thousands. It also confiscated private wells, compelled labour and turned independent households into dependants of the court. Warned that some households would resist, he signed armed seizure warrants himself; defenders and pressed labourers died, and one petitioner was executed under a procedure the Clerk had written. When the rains returned he defended every measure as necessary.

The Reaper began carrying corpses down from an estate whose owners lived above the poison line. With field medicine and stolen equipment he led the workers into the high houses and killed the overseers, and survivors hailed a liberator. His new order required every household to undergo repeated exposure rites so that nobody would ever again depend on the masters' medicine. The rites injured children and killed some adults, and they left the survivors dependent on treatments only his clinics supplied. Anyone who refused lost access to water and to burial.

When the Clerk challenged the Reaper's permanent quarantine, he found families still fenced off from their own wells and graves years after the fever had gone, and the question he put to the Reaper came back to his own register. He spent the rest of his career restoring hearings, returning land where he could, and writing expiry clauses that later copyists often left out. The Reaper answered that vulnerable people were safer without choices, and in a later outbreak, which frontier physicians agree he did not cause, made whatever bargain kept him in command.

The tellers keep both men walking. A century after the Clerk's death, when a frontier court used a copied Hundred-Wells manual to register kidnapped people as contributions to the waterworks, witnesses saw a thin clerk with ink-stained sleeves enter the hearing, suspend the court's seal and refuse to leave until the captives had been heard. Travellers on closed roads describe clinics kept by a man with a black cup where no clinic stands. The cordial there cures road-fever, and patients who leave without more of it die. Banu Mizan jurists claim the Clerk as a founder of their water law, colonial chroniclers call him an administrator who never stopped, and clerks' guilds keep his feast. Courts across the frontier still ask whether an emergency order contains "the Clerk's ending," a date after which it must justify itself again. The Reaper's bitter cordial remains good medicine in small doses and a sacrament in his cults. See [[G-30 - The Clerk Who Would Not Stop|The Clerk Who Would Not Stop]] and [[G-31 - The Bitter Cup of the Reaper|The Bitter Cup of the Reaper]].

### The Mason of the Ninth Bastion (c. 646–716 AR)

The Mason began as a quarry measurer whose job was to stop tunnels falling in. He saved a town with an improvised retaining wall, and the officials who took the credit gave him impossible works ever after while noble patrons received the honours. He recorded every death and every slight. Resentment hardened into a doctrine that only those willing to spend lives understand building. When sabotage delayed a great course, he had one worker in ten from every surviving crew killed by lot, including the workers who had exposed the saboteurs and crews who had never touched the damaged stone. At the ninth bastion of the line he shortened the evacuation to keep a foundation geometrically perfect. A workers' quarter collapsed, and he sealed prisoners inside because reopening the course would have delayed the campaign. He published their names as proof of his honesty and refused any guilt; the dead, he said, finally belonged to something that would last.

His fortresses were the best of their age, and his school's tables of safe measure saved countless lives. Garrison cults claim he still builds, below: that his ninth chambers are linked, that a massacre in one fortress opens a way into the next, and that something too large for a door walks between them. Three ordinary fortresses still contain sealed ninth chambers that engineers refuse to open and cultists try to fill. A respected engineering school teaches his measurements and keeps his "necessary loss" appendix locked away; a cadre of his followers keeps only the appendix. See [[G-27 - The Ninth Room of the Bastions|The Ninth Room of the Bastions]] and, for the saint who opposed him, the Warden of the Last Redoubt below.

### The Chain-Bearer of Sarrat (c. 670–727 AR)

The Chain-Bearer began as a slave infirmary attendant at Sarrat, able to tell another captive's pain before it was spoken. His owners sank a chain engine into his body that turned the distress around him into strength and into pain he could not control, and meant him for the frontier wars. He helped organise a revolt, and officials pulled him out of its final stand because they wanted the engine. His companions died believing he had abandoned them.

Freedom came later, and it came wrong. The liberated commander killed overseers who surrendered, fitted new soldiers with copies of his engine and massacred a neutral household accused of hiding former owners. Offered removal of the engine at the price of his command, he refused. States have cited that massacre ever since to discredit every slave revolt.

Recurrence cults say that wherever prisoners are handed from keeper to keeper toward a mass killing, the Chain-Bearer rises again in the iron, and that only breaking the chain of custody before the killing stops him. Prison-road tellers add that the Witness at the Red Passage once held a prisoner route against him and refused to call his victims innocent of every crime. Abolitionists wear broken chain links with the gap left open; the cults keep a missing joining link hidden. See [[G-29 - The Missing Link of the Chain-Bearer|The Missing Link of the Chain-Bearer]].

### The Hunter of the Shutwood (c. 708–781 AR)

The Hunter began as a hostage-guide, traded between a forest household and a frontier governor, and could find anyone in the Shutwood, a dense frontier woodland where Covenant roads, older courts and fortified refugee estates overlapped. He rose to command and would never say how he knew what he knew. In one pursuit a guilty scout was executed after trial and a coerced scout died under an unlawful sentence. When a third scout refused a secret interrogation and demanded an open hearing, the Hunter killed him with one blow, then entered all three deaths as a single lawful traitor case. The secrecy saved the route network from an infiltrator and poisoned the household that ran it.

Years later the Hunter handed over the records, resigned his command, and led the recovery of families once condemned as collaborators. He died during a Shutwood evacuation in which guilty fighters were carried out beside their children and their victims. Route families say he came back long after his death to lead households out of roads that had closed behind them, walking ahead through fog until the last of them reached a hearing, and that he came once to a town where a fear-court had marked refugees as murderers-to-be.

A later guide fellowship claims him as founder, and a secret tribunal claims he authorised permanent concealment; neither can prove descent. Covenant chroniclers call him a foreign occupier, Folk tellers say a forest court made him its guest, and others call him the founder of the Long Watch. The fellowship leaves one stool empty at every route hearing for the scout he killed, as in [[G-26 - The Empty Stool of the Slain Scout|The Empty Stool of the Slain Scout]].

### The Reader Behind the Sealed Door (c. 714–779 AR) and the Smith of the Refuge Bell (c. 721–804 AR)

They were friends, and the Reader's warning destroyed the friendship.

The Reader was an underpaid assistant at one of the frontier's sealed crossings between worlds, measuring heat, tremor and the warp of the seal. He found that something was approaching from the far side. His seniors believed him and forbade the proof, because sending the warning would complete the road it warned against: whoever received the message would bear what came back. A trial message maimed one student. The Reader hid how badly, overruled students who withdrew their consent, and stationed them as receivers for the forbidden final message.

The warning reached the distant authorities, and the road opened. Custodians, nearby households and the student receivers died holding back what crossed. The Smith, a refugee craftsman who had helped the Reader identify the threat, lost members of his refuge that night. The Reader spent his remaining years speaking of the injustice of being disbelieved.

The Smith carried his own guilt. Earlier, in a siege, he had built a heat weapon meant to clear an empty approach. The first discharge showed civilians driven into the district; given the chance to lock the weapon, he fired a second time himself, believing a breakout would kill more refugees. Civilians burned with the fighters, and his commanders hid the order while he blamed himself. He spent the rest of his life dismantling copies, forging evacuation bells and folding ladders, testifying against his patrons and teaching that an artefact belongs to the community that bears its danger. He refused both revenge on the Reader and the Reader's plea that knowledge excused the breach.

Couriers say the Reader still writes. Perfect warnings arrive in sealed packets in his hand, accurate in every detail, and the house that reads one becomes the road it describes. Refuge families answer with their own story: once, when such a road was opening, the Smith came back with soot-black arms and tall shapes like heat-shimmer at his shoulders, and the duty-marked tools along the road rang one after another until the last refugee crossed. Three archives keep sealed warnings unread, and collectors still hunt a complete set of the Smith's tools that never existed. See [[G-32 - The Letters That Must Not Be Opened|The Letters That Must Not Be Opened]] and [[G-33 - The Tools That Remember a Duty|The Tools That Remember a Duty]].

### The Smith Who Broke His Hands (c. 729–792 AR)

This Smith was a foundry bondservant whose resistance to heat became the workshop's excuse for giving the worst tasks to other children. Freed, he taught that weakness must be exposed and beaten. During a test a gifted apprentice rang the stop bell and begged to be let out; the Smith held the mechanism shut to prove that fear could be mastered, and the apprentice died. Maimed witnesses were expelled from the school because their injuries supposedly proved them unfit.

In the end he saw what his school had made. Before the hearth and his former students he broke his celebrated artificial gauntlets, confessed, sold his share of the workshop to pay the victims and spent his remaining years designing tools that maimed workers could use. Former followers attacked the new workshop and killed another apprentice.

Forge-town tellers say a generation later, when a strength-order began cutting away healthy flesh for iron and abandoning its injured, the Smith walked into its chapter hall with ruined hands, named the apprentices it had left to die, broke its seal across his knee and was gone. Guilds call him the patron of disabled artisans; his old order calls him a hypocrite; burned-house descendants say his restitution was forced on him, as [[G-28 - The Gauntlets Broken at the Hearth|The Gauntlets Broken at the Hearth]] tells it. Rival cities display rival broken gauntlets.

### The Commander on the Voluntary Wall (c. 744–752 AR; the hearing, c. 811)

The commander began as a ditch-digger and conscript stone-hauler in a frontier water-city, learned fortification from maintaining drains and rose through the militia after exposing an officer who sold repair stone. In early command the commander ordered beatings, seized hostages and had one deserting worker killed as an example. Later Lives give those orders to a deputy.

During a siege that coincided with a failing floodwall, the commander planned for thousands to hold an outer retaining wall while families crossed the inner causeway. Some stood freely. Others were debtors, prisoners, foreign labourers and conscripts held there under threat, and the commander used them anyway because the inner city would otherwise fall. Before taking a place on the wall the commander confessed the old killing, revoked the penalties on deserters' children and ordered the volunteer and compulsory rolls kept separately. The wall failed; the commander and most of those on it died; the city lived.

Around 811 a confraternity bearing the commander's name used the old emergency law to seize minority labour for a prestige wall. Witnesses at the hearing said the commander appeared before them, mud to the knees, read both rolls aloud and forbade the confraternity the name. The seizure was revoked that day. The city now sings two songs for its wall, one from each end of the street, as [[G-45 - The Two Songs at the Wall|The Two Songs at the Wall]] records.

### The Heir with Five Remembered Wings (c. 781–826 AR; the claim at the door, 907)

The heir, whom devotees call the Saint of Five Remembered Wings, was born into a frontier client house whose ruler, the whole province whispered, had bargained its heirs to something below. Its children were hostages and possible vessels for a hereditary office. When the heir refused a custody rite, the parent cut five winglike scars across the child's shoulders and side. Servants smuggled the child out, and several were executed for it.

Years later the exile came back with an armed household, overthrew the parent and killed the parent personally, then murdered surrendered relatives and an unarmed ritual custodian. After victory the heir refused the inherited office, confessed the killings before the victims' households, gave up the throne and spent the rest of a long life winning freedom, burial and property for the house's servants and child hostages. Some accepted restitution. Others named the penitent a murderer to the end. The heir died defending an evacuation from old allies who wanted the dynasty back.

In 907, as Caleran's fall emptied the frontier, a claimant came to an escape house demanding the dynasty's surviving children under the old blood clause. Witnesses said the heir stood in the doorway with five scars burning white like folded wings and spoke one sentence while the house destroyed the compact: "No wound inherits the child." The claim failed, and the hereditary office died with it. Custody courts still preserve that sentence as law. [[G-46 - The Child Who Owed No Wound|The Child Who Owed No Wound]] tells the same house from a fleeing girl's side, and its tellers still argue over who stood in the door.

### The Captain of the Sacrificial Wind and the Drowned Banner (c. 850–875 AR)

Two rescues of the late frontier are told together because they ended so differently.

About 866, when floods and raiders had driven the garrisons back from the lower fords of the river marches, refugees reported a dead captain at the head of bright riders, coming down a wind that smelled of altar-smoke to hold the road behind their convoy. Veterans swore she was a ferrywoman of those fords whom many of them had known, drowned some years before while bringing children over in a flood. When the convoy was safe, the army's commander, carrying Corvenas II's orders, demanded that she lead them on into the Velian belt. The captain withdrew, and by morning the host was gone. One of the commander's officers rode on without support and died with all his followers. Caleran's clergy never recorded her; Velian songs claim her as a daughter of the belt; Aurelium entered the rescue in its register of answered fire. Frontier tellers count her among the very few saints said to have commanded angels in a campaign, and every account remembers chiefly the refusal. Corvenas II remembered the rescue instead. Sure that Heaven would fight for a better cause than the one she had refused, he carried the Velos Standard into the belt in 874, and before Oronda it did not kindle.

In another retreat of the same decades, a cult carrying a black, dripping banner drove off raiders and brought a convoy home. Then its officers demanded the eldest children as replacements for the dead. They executed a father who said the rescue was over and seized the ferry that had carried the families. A former banner-bearer cut the ferry loose in the night, and the households escaped across the farmland while the stranded rearguard took the cult's punishment. Those families still untie a boat's rope once a year.

## The Fall and the Reconquest (905–1015 AR)

Caleran fell to the armies of Idris al-Qari in 905, and for a century the Atherian world was refugee camps, occupied towns, mountain resistance and returning claimants. Its sinners rebuilt the world as they wanted to remember it; its saints were remembered for opening doors. See [[01 Canon Reference/History/60 Fall and Successor Empires/The Invasion and the Fall.md|The Invasion and the Fall]], [[The Fall and the Long Reconquest]] and [[01 Canon Reference/History/60 Fall and Successor Empires/The Reclamation of Caleran.md|The Reclamation of Caleran]].

### The Saint at the Last Door of the Borrowed Temple (c. 905–907 AR)

As imperial order broke, soldiers, prisoners, servants and refugees packed into an old star sanctuary long since turned into a provincial prison and cistern refuge. A commander reopened its sealed chambers looking for weapons and found an offer instead: dragon-shaped war-beasts built from bodies, and the promise that his province would become the empire's true successor. The price was an altar, public acclamation and condemned bodies. He paid it. Prisoners were executed, corpses and living flesh went into the frames, and refugees who would not acclaim were denied water from the cistern. Then prisoners began vanishing from locked cells under false evacuation orders.

Refugee and garrison accounts, which agree on almost nothing else, agree on what followed. A figure they called the Saint of the Last Door stood at the lowest cell door with bright attendants, naming the prisoners one by one and holding the way. Meanwhile living prisoners and servants flooded the altar chamber, broke the acclaiming engine and dragged the unfinished war-beasts into the cistern. Many of them were killed. The province's bid to succeed Atheria died in that sanctuary. Descendants of the rescuers keep a duty at one sealed door, which answers only when someone names a threatened captive and promises to carry them. The power that made the offer is described in [[The Hunger Wars]].

### The Herald in the Signal House (c. 910–913 AR)

In the first years after Caleran's fall, a Covenant occupation governor held prisoners and negotiated surrenders through a mountain signal house above a captured city. Rebel tellers say a Herald angel was sent to announce one protected surrender and name the prisoners it covered. The governor's warlocks had built a ward around the signal mouths. When the Herald entered, the ward caught it in a repeating false signal, crushed and stripped its visible pinions and split its beaked trumpet, and demanded over and over that it proclaim the prisoners had surrendered their names and their property. The Herald refused.

A rescue party broke into the lower room and was discovered, and most of the rescuers and several prisoners were killed. The survivors damaged the ward enough for the Herald to sound the true proclamation, three cracked notes across the valley, and withdraw. The occupation executed more witnesses. Covenant penitential jurists still teach the governor's conduct as "lawful and wrong". Rebel commanders suppressed the story for a generation, fearing that word of a defeated angel would break their soldiers. Military signal law since then names the protected persons before the property in any surrender, and a broken three-note call marks any message suspected of coerced authorship. [[G-47 - Three Notes before the Spoils|Three Notes before the Spoils]] keeps the human horn players, and the devotional version keeps the Herald.

### The General of the Glass Court (c. 910–951 AR)

The General began as a camp tailor and armour-mender, so good at making defeated people look dignified that a claimant's household took him in. He rose through logistics, pageantry and genuine courage in the War of the Burning Banners (922–949). One fellow craftsman, a field officer who had served with him from the start, remained the only person who could tell him the truth.

As victory came near, the General turned reconstruction into cleansing. Maimed veterans were hidden from parades, mixed households were expelled from the highland towns the banners took back, and surrendered rivals were displayed before execution. In a private confrontation the officer threatened to expose his sexual abuse of office, his diversion of refugee funds and the expulsions. The General killed his friend with his own hands, forged assault orders and a field-death record, and laid the body among soldiers abandoned in an unsupported attack. In his last years visitors found him attended by officers who had died in the wars, restored and unscarred, and by none of the living who had served him.

Descendants say the General still holds court in a hall of glass that moves from city to city by night, offering families a flawless version of a dead hero in exchange for the bones and letters that contradict it. Formal portraits of the victorious generation had their wounds and mixed marriages painted out, and restorers have found one repeated signature beneath several of the faces. Pageant schools call him a civilising hero, his enemies a decadent traitor; some say he was possessed, and some that a beautiful Patron wore his name. See [[G-34 - The Faces Beneath the Victors|The Faces Beneath the Victors]].

### The Warden of the Last Redoubt (c. 915–965 AR)

The Warden began as a refugee quartermaster who learned masonry because nobody else would inspect the sleeping vaults. In a siege the quartermaster became commander and held the inner redoubt by sealing the outer ward before every family had crossed. The fort held. The civilians outside it and a relief company died.

The Warden refused the promotion offered for the victory, wrote down the names of the shut-out and spent later years building evacuation depth into provincial forts. Grief also turned cruel: subordinates died in assaults on Caleran's outer works in 956 and 957 whose real purpose was to prove that the Warden had not chosen cowardice at the gate. A last confession named both acts. Where the Warden died, and whether the body was recovered, the forts cannot agree.

Soldiers of a much later siege swore that a one-handed warden appeared on their wall, opened the outer ward first and held it until the last child was through. In that fort's ninth chamber the knocking stopped. Masons tell this as the Warden's victory over the Mason of the Ninth Bastion: a massacre that the Mason's linked fortresses needed was refused. Several fortresses claim the Warden's severed hand; the relic that fort-builders trust is an ordinary measuring cord, knotted again by refugee children to the distance a child can carry a baby through smoke, as in [[G-35 - The Child's Cord at the Redoubt|The Child's Cord at the Redoubt]]. Some Thalmyric tellers make the Warden one of Caedros I's hidden lieutenants.

### The Wardens of Caelmount (917 AR)

The cave-wardens of Caelmount kept the refuge's grain in galleries where the mountain's breath killed rats, mould and men alike, and kept the upper galleries sweet by opening stone doors in the vents. On the night before a Covenant column reached the caves they shut every door. They knew that the column would sleep there, that their own granary would be a year in airing, and whose bread lay in the low galleries. Eleven hundred men were drawn out dead in the spring, and the valley went hungry for years, because nobody would eat grain that had wintered among them. The founding sermons give the victory to Caedros, who was two days away. The wardens' families keep a fast on that night, and nobody in the valley has ever called it a feast. See [[01 Canon Reference/Peoples and Cultures/Thalmyric Empire/70 History and Conflicts/The Battle of Caelmount.md|The Battle of Caelmount]].

### The Prince of the Clause (929 AR)

Ruzbeh ibn Idris was fourteen when he was brought to the Chain Furnace yard to take up the instruments of his father's army. He laid his hand on the first engine's feeding-plate. At the second, by the foundry masters' account, he asked what was in the frame, was told that it held a condemned man, and took his hand away. He said he would hold nothing that had not been given. His father died two years later with no other heir sworn in, every oath in the Covenant lapsed, the silt hounds climbed out of the northern wells and a walking engine killed nineteen people in the dyers' quarter. Iqashar lays those dead at the boy's door. The people of the Clause, who had taught him, call it the one day their argument was obeyed. He refused again when captains came to force him, was lodged, and lived thirty years in a walled garden at Al-Qahur under a name nobody was allowed to say.

### The Woman Given a False Family (c. 930–960 AR)

During a hostage exchange early in the Reconquest, a woman was ambushed and then rescued, cleanly and kindly, by the people who had arranged the ambush. They told her that her travelling companions had sold her, and presented strangers as the household that had paid her ransom. One true companion who tried to reach her was killed as an impostor. Years later she heard her old nurse's work song sung by a voice her rescuers had taught her to fear, recognised it and fled with the nurse, leaving property and gratitude behind. Other survivors stayed with their false families. Returnee communities blame such rescues on a power they call the False Prince, and since then they have asked a rescuer who paid for the danger before they ask who paid the ransom.

### Gulnar of the Dyers' Hall (931 AR)

The Covenant had its own wonders in these years, and Iqashar tells this one. In 929 Idris al-Qari's heir, Ruzbeh, refused at fourteen to take up his father's instruments, and in the autumn of 931 Idris died of fever before anyone had sworn again to a successor. Every oath sworn to the two of them by name lapsed at once. Levies walked home, and in the Chain Furnace yard at Iqashar the great Depth-Core engine stopped answering its feeding names. On the ninth night after the funeral the frame walked. It broke through the foundry wall, went downhill to the dyers' cisterns, the nearest open water, and killed nineteen people in the dye-yards.

A Vashari-born engine feeder named Gulnar followed it down. From the threshold stone of the Dyers' Hall she called its feeding name again, and gave it to no man: she spoke it "to the threshold of the house of Qahir, whoever stands in it". The frame stopped in a vat of indigo and lay down. Iqashari crews have called it the Elder ever since, and the blue still shows in its seams.

Her sentence named a throne. Oaths since have been sworn to the Sublime Threshold, an office that cannot die, witnessed at Al-Qahur. The dyers' quarter credits Gulnar and pours a measure of indigo into the cistern on the anniversary; foundry masters of her day said the beast had drunk itself quiet in the vats; Qazmari preachers say Iqashar invented the story to give its new throne a miracle. See [[The Fall and the Long Reconquest]].

### The Eleven Keepers (957 AR)

The third generation of the Covenant keepers who held the Eternal Ember as a deposit for the final audit had been born in occupied Caleran, and their names stood pledged that the lamp would be neither divided, removed nor put out. When their own amir's guard came in the second week of the funeral revolt to carry the lamp to his ships, they barred the basilica, and eleven died at the lamp before the funeral crowds came up. The survivors would not open the forum precinct to the Thalmyric army until it had heard terms for the garrison families their amir had shut out of the harbour fort. Caleran's clergy pray for them by name each spring; Iqashar counts them among its honest dead; Thalmyric zealots have never forgiven the clergy for praying for unbelievers. See [[01 Canon Reference/History/60 Fall and Successor Empires/The Reclamation of Caleran.md|The Reclamation of Caleran]].

### Hedwen of Kaltfurt (960 AR; d. c. 1010)

In 960 Grass-Tarkhan riders caught a column of refugees and grain carts at the ford of Kaltfurt on the eastern roads. The march chaplain ran. Hedwen, a miller's widow walking with the carts, pulled the march banner out of the mud and stood with it in the water between the riders and the children. The banner took the Flame, the horses shied from it, and the raid broke on the ford. The commander watching from the bank was Adalmar, a Markish margrave's son, and the next year the marcher lords made him king of the eastern marches.

She refused to carry the banner into his wars on the Folk. In 1006 the Sanctum's relic court examined it, recorded it answering and credited the sign to the king. Hedwen, by then an old woman, walked to Aurelium and told the examiners that the banner had never burned in Adalmar's hand or anyone's since the ford. Her testimony went into the register, and Lord Primarch Valerin II would crown no man over a living witness. She died around 1010. In 1013 a hand in the next Primarch's chancery struck her testimony through, and the line can still be read under the stroke. In 1017 Adalmar was crowned Emperor of the Atherians in the ford where she had stood, and the millers of Kaltfurt shut their sluices and left him standing in mud. Millers keep her feast, and reformers in the Markish country hold her up as proof that the Fire came to a widow while the Church gave the credit to a king. See [[The Great Split — Two Crowns, One Fire]].

### The King of Unburied Hosts (c. 970–1010 AR)

In the 990s, when the Breakers had driven out the flame-monks who buried the dead of the reconquered valleys, a commander in one small valley forbade burial while a corpse-working emissary rebuilt the dead into a road gang. A mother saw her son's mended coat among the workers and followed it, because the emissary said he still served. At the camp she found several bodies answering to his name and a copied voice rehearsing words he had never spoken. The families smashed the fittings that drove the workers and burned the whole gang, coat and all. Soldiers killed several mourners and walled the surviving prisoners up among the remaining machines. The valley's tellers say the bodies were bound for a dead king's necropolis below, a city of corpses that work, and that the burning cut one of its roads. Burial law in the reconquered provinces still forbids any commander to hold the dead.

### Varkas of Anavra (975 AR)

Varkas was an old lamp-sergeant who had carried a kindled war-lamp before Caleran. In 975 riders from the amirate of Meliton came up the reconquered valley to the walled town of Anavra, and the garrison's chaplain, a court bishop's nephew in a bought post, raised the war-lamp on the gate. It stayed dark. The riders burned the granary quarter and carried some three hundred people south. Varkas led the survivors forty miles to the bishop's house with the dark lamp and broke it on his doorstep. "It did not burn for us," he is supposed to have said. "Show us one that does." The bands that took up his question were called the Breakers. They held every lamp in Thalmyria to it and smashed the ones that stayed dark, the lamps of true flame-monks and of ordinary households among them. Preachers still argue whether he asked an honest question or taught a country to put God to the test. See [[The Great Split — Two Crowns, One Fire]].

### The Custodian Who Gave Up His Name (1003–1011 AR)

In 1003 Tarkhan riders took the son of the Custodian of the Vault of Names on the road north of Iqashar. The ransom was beyond him, and he pledged his own true name to a lending house that kept its desks below the Ruzbeh Weight House. The loan was built to fail. By 1006 the house held the Custodian, through him the keys of the Vault, and through the Vault the name of every bound levy in the Covenant's army. No commander could say whose soldiers he led, and the army sat in its camp through the years when Thalmyria was breaking its own lamps.

In 1011 the Custodian went down into the Vault's deepest custody and surrendered his own name into it. Registrars say the ink of it faded from every page where it had been written, from the moneylenders' ledger to his marriage contract, and that his wife could no longer say it aloud. That night the Vault's guards burned the lending house with its books. His son, ransomed at last, came home to a father he could not call by name.

Every Custodian since has surrendered his name at investiture, which is why the Unnamed Custodian is the one officer in 1360 whom the Guard cannot bind and the court cannot blackmail. Registrars give the first of them three former names, none found in any document older than 1011, and keep a niche for him in the Vault's outer hall, where petitioners leave blank slips. Qazmari preachers call what he did the suicide of a soul, and Thalmyric polemicists of the Breaking years counted him among the damned.

### Makarios at Sarmene (1009 AR)

In 1009 a fever was in the hospice at Sarmene, and a Breaker band came to put the hospice lamp to the question and burn the house if it failed. A flame-monk of about thirty-five named Makarios stood in the door with the lamp, between the crowd and forty sick, and it took the Flame. The Breakers knelt, and the old emperor made him Patriarch that year because they would follow him. Makarios the Adamant spent the rest of a long life telling them that he had passed no test. The lamp had burned for the sick behind him, he said, and would have stayed dark for the crowd in front, and the asking was the sin. In 1015, when Aurelium's legate came to have the hooded Great Lamp of Veridun examined, he drove four nails through the bronze hood into the lamp-stand while the legate waited on the cathedral steps, and the churches split over it. He drew the nails himself at the Rekindling of 1052, and the witnesses said the lamp under the hood was burning. Those who distrust the story say the hood had a vent and the keepers had fed it. See [[The Great Split — Two Crowns, One Fire]].

## Two Crowns and High Noon (1015–1290 AR)

The Great Split of 1015 gave the Known World two churches with two calendars, and every saint who had lived before it became something to own. High Noon brought prosperity, mines, Qarath, the Grey Sweat and the Black Wind. See [[The Great Split — Two Crowns, One Fire]], [[High Noon and the Qarath Disaster]] and [[01 Canon Reference/History/70 Later History and Present Day/The Grey Sweat.md|The Grey Sweat]].

### The Saint of the Two Ferries (c. 996–1035 AR)

Before the Split, a holy ferry-keeper on the border river the Thalmyrics call the Lissos and the Markish the Lessen carried travellers of every allegiance and buried the enemy dead that came down with the current. Both banks kept the keeper's feast. In the ferry riot of 1022 a mob from one bank seized the ferry shrine, killed its custodian and threw the keeper's bones into the river. The other bank imprisoned clergy and expelled households in revenge. Apparitions then appeared on both banks blessing the seizures and the reprisals, and the relic courts later agreed that at least one of them was false. Each church wrote a complete Life making the keeper its own. The ferry households kept a shorter rule about whom the boat must carry. In 1360 the two banks' icons hide one matching correction under different overpainting, and once a year a crossing is made with no church's banner aboard.

### The Judge Beneath the Black Eaves (c. 1018–1080 AR)

The Judge began as a roof-runner in the border town of Dvorna, carrying warrants over the slates between two courts that no longer recognised one another's seals. Up there, in rain, the visions came: murders, riots and betrayals, true and incomplete, seen before they happened. The runner prevented several crimes and became a celebrated examiner. Gradually the Judge began to torture people for acts only foreseen, to display mutilated offenders under the courthouse eaves and to terrorise whole households, because fear seemed to reduce visible crime.

The visions were neither invented nor inevitable. Several atrocities happened because the Judge's preventive executions destroyed the friendships that might have stopped them. When an assassin finally came for the Judge, the Judge ordered the guards to stand aside, so that the death could be used as proof that every prophecy was fixed.

Black-roofed tiles scratched with future crimes appeared in markets after the Judge's death, some in the Judge's hand. In the decades of the Split, magistrates of each church secretly bought visions of the other church's future crimes and persecuted in advance, and the hardening of the Split owes something to that trade. Route families say the Hunter of the Shutwood came once, long dead, to lead out refugees a fear-court had marked as murderers-to-be. Courts in both churches now forbid conviction for "the future fact," while secret magistrates still pay for Black-Eaves forecasts. See [[G-36 - The Black Eaves and the Uncommitted Crime|The Black Eaves and the Uncommitted Crime]].

### The Saint at the Enemy Gate (c. 1020–1060 AR)

Around 1026, in the war among Wolmar's sons, two of their armies met at Grauwacht, a mountain fortress of the middle march guarding a grain road and three winter passes, and both venerated the same early military saint, an army physician remembered for refusing to certify tortured prisoners fit for execution. One army called the saint the Physician of the Western Host; the other, the Keeper of Captive Soldiers. Each demanded a sign that the saint belonged to it alone.

Signs came. An armoured figure showed the attackers an old supply postern, and inside the walls a campaign icon sweated while officers dreamed of their enemies begging beneath the fortress standard. The garrison stole badges and finger-bones from enemy graves, beat prisoners, mutilated messengers and killed suspected collaborators. Prisoners from both armies, local porters and the families of suspects were penned by the refuse gate, which both sides called the enemy gate because it faced away from the road. During the withdrawal the commander executed several captives and ordered the rest sealed into a granary already burning.

Witnesses described the saint at the enemy gate in different uniforms but agreed on what was done there. The saint broke the gate's windlass, dropped its iron screen between the captives and a revenge company, carried wounded children out through the ash passage and named three tortured enemy messengers missing from both armies' rolls. When soldiers carried the gate key to the command tower, it was ordinary iron in their hands. The attackers' order still celebrates the postern as the saint's victory; mountain porters say the armoured figure there was hired, and that the saint came to the enemy gate because prisoners, enemies and children were there. The key in the old arsenal releases one windlass, for a named prisoner under an unlawful death order, and does nothing else.

### Arsen at the Lamp-House Door (1049 AR)

For thirteen years the crown had impounded every household lamp in Thalmyria in its lamp-houses, which had begun as a refuge from the Breakers. The keepers were paid a measure of oil for each lamp, and most of them sold it. In the dry summers of 1046–1048 families went down their failing wells, lifted the lamps they had hidden there, and began to ask for the ones the crown held. In 1049 the households of Veridun came into the square before the capital's lamp-house carrying their lifted lamps, a thousand by the chroniclers' count. Arsen, captain of the guard on the door, had orders to hold it, and his mother was standing in the square. He broke the seal himself. The emperor Tavoran IV, seventy-three years old, went in alone and found the lamp from his own saddle-bag cold on the first shelf, where he had set it as an example thirteen years before. Crowds in Veridun and the march towns dragged the keepers out and hanged them from the lamp-house beams, the honest with the thieves. The empty house is unlocked once a year, on the Night of the Cold Houses, and the palace guard still swears its captains at that door.

### The Saint of Ashen Wings (c. 1087–1146 AR)

The Saint began as a slave and courier in the silver mines of the Heldenberg, worked by convicts, Two Winters bread-rioters and the children of Folk captives. Around 1116 a revolt through the old prison tunnels freed several valleys. When the counts' army came back up the valley in the winter of 1123, the rebel leader sent condemned overseers first through a broken passage between worlds in the mountain, the Cold Way, to test it. Survivors came back changed and dangerous, and through them the rebels learned the passage, came out behind the counts' army in the snow and broke it. The leader killed some of the changed survivors personally and hid the experiment from the liberated valleys. In 1124 the free valleys swore the Oath of the Shafts, the first of Korrheim's cantons.

The lie protected the valleys long enough to survive and left the test party's families without bodies or truth. Around 1137 the leader confessed before the canton assembly, gave up office and went into exile rather than make the victims keep the heroic story. In exile the former commander rescued people from experiments like the first and died closing a breach in 1146.

Captives freed from breach-experiments in later generations described a rescuer with ash-dark wings who walked into the experimenters' halls and broke their gates, and carters on an inland road claimed to have seen the same figure burning wagons of annotated Second Gospels. The settlements' liberation feast is followed next morning by an unofficial reading of the experimental victims, with black bread made from the feast's ashes, as [[G-37 - The Feast Followed by Ashen Names|The Feast Followed by Ashen Names]] describes. Liberation songs call the Saint immaculate; victims' families call him unforgivable; covert rescue orders all claim him as founder.

### The Chain-Tender above the Harbour Wall (hanged 1102; the siege of 1166)

In 1102, the second of the Two Winters, famine ships from the Eclessian coast came to Sermora, and the council kept the great harbour chain raised against them. A foreign-born chain-tender lowered it. Some refugees lived; some harbour workers died when desperate ships forced the gap. The city hanged the chain-tender for treason.

In 1166 a rival's fleet and hired Ättar keels besieged Sermora with a leased storm-engine. Attackers killed civilians on the walls and defenders fought them stone by stone. Sermoran veterans, and the attackers' own chronicles, describe a figure in a chain-tender's leather apron walking the harbour wall in the worst of it; the storm-engine broke and the siege failed. When the city's soldiers then began murdering prisoners and foreign residents blamed for the breach, the same figure was seen to turn and rebuke them, and official songs left the rebuke out along with the hanging. Admirals cite the defence for offensive wars, minority families keep the rebuke and the names of the murdered prisoners, and merchants sell fragments of the wall. On the feast, the harbour chain must be lowered for one refugee or relief vessel before it is raised for the civic procession, and one stretch of wall cannot be demolished without riots.

### Despina of Kolonna and the Widow's Lamp (1159 AR)

Caedros III, the Wall of the World, died in 1159, believing that the Fire watched the throne. Twenty years earlier, at Kolonna, he had left his succession to it, to bar his son Orestan, who had cut down surrendered men at Vresna, without naming him. Seven claimants, his son, two sons-in-law, three nephews and a guard captain, stood at his bier in Veridun holding lamps. None kindled. Orestan's flared, an old warlock in the choir smelled script-fire under the wick and was walked out of the cathedral, and the court acclaimed the emperor's son before morning. In the nave, among the district honour guard, Despina of Kolonna, a soldier's widow who had walked to Veridun to pray for her three sons on the frontier, was holding a lamp of her own. It took the Flame.

The court suppressed the report, and the district soldiers carried it home. In the War of the Seven Lamps that followed, the district armies made the widow's lamp their warrant and acclaimed their own commander, Andrion, whom the court called the Widow's Emperor and who wore the name as a badge. It took them three years. When they brought the beaten Orestan to Kolonna in a farm cart and asked her what should be done with him, she said he should keep a grave, and he kept the chapel over the dead of Vresna for twenty years. Despina went home to Kolonna and refused every summons to court. Thalmyric villagers venerate her without waiting for a synod to canonise her; Eclessian examiners note that no one ever tested the lamp; court historians call her the districts' pretext. The Widow's Lamp is kept in Kolonna's church, and district families still carry their door-lamps to it on the anniversary.

### The Angel at the Threshing Floor (c. 1171–1173 AR)

Between the golden age of Caedros III and Qarath, the prince of Elenara, a tributary river principality, ordered a census. Its open purpose was grain relief; its hidden purpose was to seize dependants for road labour, list taxable refugees and pick hostages from doubtful households, so that a complete register could support his bid for a crown of his own. Clerks and soldiers broke doors, dragged families to counting stations and killed those who resisted.

Then death came down the census roads. Within hours of each sealed schedule reaching a counting-house, clerks, soldiers and the nearest households sickened with the same rapid fever and failing breath. Witnesses all along the courier roads saw something above the leading edge of the dying: a dark, immense raptor-shape, filled with white fire, moving at the pace of the couriers. It killed clerks and nobles, labourers and children. The prince blamed foreigners, then confessed the census's true purpose, freed the surviving hostages, burned the seizure schedule and walked barefoot to an ordinary threshing floor at Kremna, owned by a household the census had already bereaved. The dying stopped there.

Royal Lives make the prince the saviour of his people; victims' records insist he confessed only after mass death. The independence bid died with the census, and Elenara is a duchy still, and law codes across the successor world now require a count to state its purpose and forbid any later use for hostages or labour seizure without a new public act. Jurists call the limit "the floor beyond the count." See [[G-48 - The Threshing Floor beyond the Count|The Threshing Floor beyond the Count]].

### The Prince Who Would Not Sell the Peace (c. 1190–1220 AR)

In Vlastrava, around 1220, in the War of the Seal that followed Qarath, an agent arranged a surrender that saved the household of King Stanimir. Once the captives were safe, the same agent offered the king his enemy's defenceless children and urged him to avenge an old murder; the treaty had been designed so that breaking it would be easy. He refused. His own brother called the refusal disloyal and killed the mediator. The children went home under guard, one household had kept its word, and one murder went unpunished. Later tellers made Stanimir universally merciful. Vlastravan Lives call the mediator a devil's agent, Vlastrava's word became the one men trusted, and its treaty law hardened around him. Vlastrava's backing later helped crown Nikandros the Restorer. Agents of what border clergy call the Oathbreaker are said to be waiting still for a peace that someone will sell.

### Aubert of Corvel, the Ferryman Primarch (1240–1251 AR)

In 1240 the plague-thinned conclave elected Aimery of Corvel, who sickened that evening and died before dawn, before he had sung a single rite. Under the quarantine rule the conclave would have been sealed in with the fever. His twin Aubert buried him in the conclave garden without a rite, put on his ring and came out to the loggia in his place, and for twenty months reigned as his brother. His Ferry Rule of 1241 let any baptised person who knew the names of the dead sing the funeral where no priest lived, and thousands of parishes buried their dead by it. In the spring of 1242 sacristans saw a cardinal in red at the garden wall at night, with the Primarch's face; one said the name *Aimery* and sang, and the figure went. Aubert confessed, the two colleges split over whether his acts stood, and families who had buried by the Ferry Rule did not know whether their dead had been sent. Gelasin II confirmed the Rule for the plague years only, and Aubert died in a Brauvalis abbey in 1251. Aurelian canonists call him the Usurper of the Ring; Brauvalis abbeys and the Freeflame call him the Ferryman Primarch. See [[01 Canon Reference/History/70 Later History and Present Day/The Grey Sweat.md|The Grey Sweat]].

### The Rider of the Benches (d. 1241 AR)

Prince Mihrzad, Suhrab's most gifted son, rode the Qarath-frontier forts in the Emptying of the Benches as a living witness so that their garrisons could re-swear and stay; the forts he reached held. His escort carried the Sweat into three forts that had been clean. Frontier villages remember the rider who brought the plague; the Iqashar bazaar sings the son whose death made his father give the Guard the throne's witness. See [[Historical Spine of the Covenant]].

### Gavrin the Farrier, the Grey Uncle (c. 1215–1281 AR)

Gavrin was a Velian farrier and village deacon in the Covenant's client belt, entered in the Living Register as a bound craftsman and sent north around 1235 to shoe horses at a well-fort on Iqashar's Tarkhan frontier. When the Grey Sweat emptied the Covenant's benches, the garrison's oaths lapsed and its soldiers marched home; the craftsmen, bound to the fort, stayed, and Tarkhan raiders took them. Gavrin was traded twice before he came to the herd-master of a minor Grass-Tarkhan clan.

In the late winter of 1249 a grey mare of Grey Sister's line dropped a black colt too weak to stand, and the herd-master's small son wept over it. Gavrin knelt in the snow, laid his hands on the colt and sang the old remount hymn that Velian villages kept as a blessing for sick animals. The colt stood that night. It grew into a stallion that would walk up to a bound well-warden as if it were a fencepost, and its foals did the same. The herd-master took the horse's name, Qarayel, and with its line built the Black Wind. Gavrin blessed sick foals and sick children and refused to bless anything for war. Offered a place in the clan, he said he would take it when the camp's other slaves were freed, and stayed a slave. The camp called him the Grey Uncle. In 1279 envoys of the Thalmyric emperor Evandros came to buy him for the imperial studs, and he would not go. In 1281 he was found in the horse lines with his throat cut. Qarayel killed the envoys and sent their heads to Veridun in a feed-bag.

Thalmyria canonised him in the 1290s as Saint Gavrin of the Halter, a martyr of the Covenant's name-hunters. Velian congregations, whose fairs his horses burned, call him the Wind-Saddler and keep no feast. The Tarkhan honour him at foaling and say the envoys killed him; some steppe tellers say Qarayel did it himself, to keep the grace from being sold. Covenant registers list him as an absconded craftsman whose name was never released. The Moon Horde keeps his cairn and will not let it be opened. See [[Qarayel and the Black Wind]].

### Saint Gerlind of Lindenfurt (c. 1228–1279 AR)

Gerlind was a miller's daughter of Lindenfurt, a ford-and-mill town on a tributary of the upper Weiss. In 1236 the Sweat killed the parish priest, her mother and her two brothers, and her mother was seen at the mill-race for nine nights. On the tenth, eight years old, Gerlind made the neighbours say aloud the names of their dead and sang the one funeral hymn she knew, and her mother was not seen again. From about 1248 she walked to parishes whose priests had died and whose dead still walked, named every dead person by the people who had known them, then sang. She took bread and refused coin: *the dead are named, not bought.* She was flogged at a Virelian magnate's gate in 1257 for burying bond-folk, and in 1266 a Sanctum relic court whose own observers had watched a thin place close at her singing acquitted her narrowly, on condition that she sing only with a priest present. She brought whatever priest she could find. She died of a winter fever in 1279 and was buried by the mill-race.

Her death made her useful. Alessior II canonised her in 1284 with unusual speed, and his Chancery printed her face on its assurance letters; the Gerlind seal outsold every other. Her grave was enclosed in a Sending House chapel that charged pilgrims, her bones went to the Sanctum in 1290, and in 1285 an apparition of Gerlind herself was seen condemning the Saint-Leon singers who sang the dead out for nothing, before a riot burned their quarter. The Sanctum honours her as the obedient saint who sang with a priest present; the Freeflame claim her as the first of their elders and quote her saying against every Sending. The boy who held the chaplain's book for her at Brenholt in 1267 was Ansgar, and Brenholt miners say a woman's voice joined his night singing in the 1280s. Lindenfurt people still go to the mill-race to say names. See [[01 Canon Reference/History/70 Later History and Present Day/The Grey Sweat.md|The Grey Sweat]].

## Held Proofs and the Present (1290–1360 AR)

The assurance trade made the dead a matter of paper, and the age of reformations and relic courts made saints a matter of evidence. In 1360 several Lives remain open. See [[The Age of Held Proofs]] and [[Freeflame Reformation]].

### Silvio Quarne, the Bridge-Seller (c. 1238–1299 AR)

Quarne was a Fiorenzian notary's son whom the last wave of the Sweat left with a ledger and a gift for figures. He came to the Chancery of Sendings about 1265 and made it efficient: the printed letter, rates by age and estate with a cheaper rate for a child, the blank letter sold with the name left open, and the household bond that covered a family for three generations. As Alessior II's Cardinal-Treasurer from 1282 he put Saint Gerlind's face on the letters, and Lantern investigators later traced the apparition that condemned the Saint-Leon singers to his office.

By 1288 his own audits told him the Sending Houses sang perhaps one rite in ten of those sold. He sold the unsung letters too, in bundles, to brokers in Vorthara, and the money went to Alessior's election debt. When the Houses of the upper Weiss stopped sending money in 1297 he left Aurelium with his private ledgers, and he died in 1299 in a rented room in the Black Spire wards of Vorthara. The ledgers were gone when the room was opened.

The Freeflame named him from Ansgar's First Grievance, *you have sold the poor a bridge that is not yours*, and found his office's countersign on the dead paper in brokers' houses. Sestaran historians call him a gifted administrator blamed for a Primarch's credulity, and Fiorenzian counting-houses teach his methods without his name. In 1360 letters bearing his countersign are the most prized dead paper in the trade, and Vortharan tellers say his ledgers still add up at night.

### Ansgar of Brenholt and Irmel of the Blackwater (1290 AR)

Ansgar was chaplain of the Sending House at Brenholt, in the iron country of the upper Weiss, and the son of the man who sold the House's letters at its door. From about 1284 he kept two tallies in the margins of his psalter, the letters sold and the rites he was given time to sing, and they ran at better than ten to one. He sang the unsung names himself, at night and for nothing, and beside them he wrote what he could not say from the pulpit. In the late spring of 1290 a thaw-flood broke into the Blackwater pit on the night shift, and the pithead could hear men knocking behind the water. Ansgar went down the ladderway to sing for them. He gave his psalter to the pithead tally-keeper, a young woman named Irmel, and told her to keep his count. The second inrush took the ladderway, and forty-one miners and their chaplain went out by the adit into the river.

In the morning his father set up the trestle at the pithead, and the widows queued to buy. Irmel read the forty-one names off the shift board, then the chaplain's, then the margins, and chalked his notes on the board as she read. A hundred fitted. The queue turned from the trestle to listen, and the upper Weiss began to bury its dead the Brenholt way, with the names said aloud by the people who had known them, a hymn and no letter. Nine years later a synod at Aldersbruck laid the psalter on a brazier and it would not burn. The legate sealed it in lead and had Irmel flogged at the bridgehead for keeping a dead priest's book from his superiors. She watched from the bank when the princes swore the Korrheim Accord in 1343, and died the next year.

The Freeflame say the House sent its chaplain down alone and sold over him in the morning. Some teachers of the old Church answer that he died the Sanctum's own priest, and that the Church has tried a book and never its author. Bargemen say the river took back a man who had named its drowned, and the hill parishes say he came out by the adit. The river people who followed Irmel give their dead to the Preacher's Water and keep no churchyard, and they still repeat her answer to the Night Count: *the dead are named, not counted.* See [[Freeflame Reformation]].

### Father Anselin of Saint-Leon (c. 1283–1315 AR)

Anselin was carried out of Aurelium's Saint-Leon quarter as a child of two on the night in 1285 when a riot burned it. The quarter's lay choirs had sung the poor dead out for nothing, and an apparition of Saint Gerlind had been seen condemning them. The survivors went on singing after dark, by lantern, because by day it was forbidden. Anselin studied under Doctor Lucian Ferro at Veridance and became the night-singers' priest, so that they sang as Gerlind's judges had required of her, with a priest present. The Inquisitors of Saint Malveris took him at a night funeral in 1314, and he died in one of their prison-houses the next winter without a trial. In 1320 the Council of Veridance licensed his brotherhood to sing the Great Arrears, and in 1324 it chartered them as the Order of the Lantern and placed the Malverine prisons under their review. The Lantern's first great case proved that the apparition at Saint-Leon had been staged. Freeflame congregations call the order a stolen choir. The Order has never been told which house Anselin died in, and in 1360 its investigators are hearing the case of one such house, told below.

### The Guardian at the Workhouse (c. 1327–1331 AR)

A baptised tallyman falsified warehouse measures; poor households lost grain, and a guard died in the riot the shortage helped provoke. He confessed part of the fraud, returned what money remained and gave evidence against a larger broker, and was still convicted and sent to a workhouse infirmary, where overseers ignored repeated warnings that he was dying. After his death a clerk swapped his corpse tag with another debtor's, so that a contract broker could treat the wrong name as proof that an infernal assurance had come due on the tallyman's dependants. The washer who laid out the bodies swore that all night a small, pale, winged figure kept turning the false tag over on his chest, woke her twice, and would not let her rest until she had spoken his baptismal name aloud before the bodies were sealed. Records and testimony later proved the exchange, but the broker's office still disputes whether the corrected register defeats its claim, and the hearing is unfinished in 1360. [[G-49 - The Two Tags on One Dead Man|The Two Tags on One Dead Man]] tells a related workhouse story.

### Frate Severin and the Pyre of Masks (1346–1349 AR)

Fiorenzians keep a wax face of each of their dead on the house wall, a custom of the plague years. In the winter after the Sermoran pamphlet of 1345 the faces of the dead it had named began to sweat and to speak, and families queued to hear their mothers. The Flame-preacher Frate Severin taught that the faces had become doors for the Hungers, which are shut by naming and singing the dead behind them, never by fire. At the Feast of Souls in 1346 the senate ordered every speaking face burned in the cathedral square, and he begged the city not to light the pyre. Witnesses still argue about what they saw standing in that fire. The crowd went to the Medori palace for the names, and Lauro Medori opened the vault and gave the friar the city's sheets of the registers. For four years Severin, holding no office, led the Long Vigil, singing every name on them while the painters painted the burned faces back; he preached against the crowds who drowned mask-makers as door-makers and was not obeyed. In 1349 the citizens voted the Medori a hereditary chair, and he preached against that too. On the eve of the Feast of Souls he went alone into the mask-makers' cellar, where the moulds of the burned faces were kept, and was found in the morning wearing a wax face nobody could lift from his own. He was buried in it outside the walls. The Freeflame claim him as a kindled prophet, the Order of the Lantern studies him, many Fiorenzians believe the Medori had a hand in his death, and every painter in the city keeps a drawer of faces he will not show.

### The Lady of the Sloe-Field at Vessing (1352 AR)

A Virelian runaway's child died on the road near Vessing, and no churchyard on the Schedule would take it. The mother, the parish sexton who had refused her the yard and a reformed carter who had carried the body buried the child at dusk in the sloe-field outside the wall, and all three swear that a figure of banked fire stood at the grave. Their testimonies cannot be reconciled, and each tells against the witness's own church. The sexton, a Sanctum man, says the figure told them the Fire dwells where it is welcomed, which is Veridun's case. The carter, a Freeflame elder's son, says it told them the Fire is carried by the hands of the Church, which is the Sanctum's. The mother, raised in Virelia's village piety, says it said nothing, wept and pointed over the wall at the graveyard, and reformers read that as a command to sing for the dead. Captured papers of the Weeping Courts claim the apparition as their work, which may be a confession, a lie or the infiltration of something genuine. The Sanctum has not ruled.

### The Closed House and Saint Malveris (1357–1360 AR)

In one Eclessian jurisdiction, laundresses, guards, clerks, a physician and gravediggers kept evidence that a prison-house of the Inquisitors of Saint Malveris had tortured detainees, seized property and executed at least one suspect on fabricated evidence. Several witnesses say that Saint Malveris, or a figure using the name, appeared inside the sealed house, named the victims, condemned the order's practices and refused the senior inquisitor's demand for a sign that would prove the order alone was right. Order of the Lantern investigators, secular officers and church courts are hearing the case in 1360. Founder's correction, reformers' forgery, Idol revenge, Freeflame propaganda: every party in the hearing has its own explanation of the apparition.
