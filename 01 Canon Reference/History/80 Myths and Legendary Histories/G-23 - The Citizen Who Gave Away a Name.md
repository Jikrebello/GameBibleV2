# The Citizen Who Gave Away a Name

> **Stories told.** This is an unfinished 1360 tradition. Current rumours and the observable practice are established; no future discovery, victory, judgement or fate is implied.

## Historical setting

**1297–1360 AR; Reformation and unfinished present.** known-world towns and Anchor-facing harbours. This tradition belongs to [[The Age of Held Proofs]]; its cultural bearers are living townspeople and conflicting current witnesses.

It is told as present-day harbour gossip in one Anchor-facing port where veterans have recently disappeared. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## The tale

An old naval veteran noticed that men who had served on one forgotten expedition were vanishing. Officials said they had left voluntarily. The veteran recognised the same grey carriage outside three homes and learned that its servants could find a person through civic rolls, medals and the public use of a full name.

He sought a false keeper who offered to hide him by taking his civic name. The bargain did not remove or divide his soul. It changed how the city's habits found him. Neighbours forgot why they knew his face. Rain washed the figures from his medals. His grandchildren remembered affection but argued whether he was uncle, father or an old family servant.

Unseen by officials, he entered the carriage house and rescued one captive. The price followed him home: his wife opened the door and treated him kindly as a stranger. He could tell her memories, but every proof sounded stolen. When he tried to reclaim the name, the keeper demanded the rescued captive in exchange.

The tale has reached only this point. The grey carriage still moves. The veteran still wears the coat. One grandchild has begun remembering the blank silhouette in dreams, while another intends to sell the coat to pay a debt.

The rescued captive refuses to explain why the carriage wanted them. Protecting that silence may be gratitude, fear or another betrayal. The veteran can no longer rely on public recognition to decide whom to trust.

## Why it is remembered

Its pull is a live identity mystery rather than a completed legend. The wound is being loved without being recognised, and the danger is that restoring public identity may sacrifice the person rescue made possible.

## How tellers differ

Dockworkers call the veteran a spy. His neighbours remember different family roles. The port authority denies any name can be removed and quietly confiscates drawings of the carriage.

## What remains

An old coat shows blank human shapes on its medals whenever rain darkens the cloth.

## History and belief

The practice, landscape or physical object described here has a place in the present world. Statements that it is the original relic, that a voice is the original dead person, or that a victory proves divine approval belong to the tradition unless a separate history establishes them.

The tale stops at the present.
