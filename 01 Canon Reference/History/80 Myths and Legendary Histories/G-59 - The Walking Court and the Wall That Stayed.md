# The Walking Court and the Wall That Stayed

> **Stories told.** The local telling and the material or customary remainder below are established. A claimed original object, miraculous response, famous identity or supernatural explanation is not authenticated merely by this article.

## Historical setting

**c. 1950–1750 BR.** Tarkhan-facing Near-Glass wells. This tradition belongs to [[Age of Scattering]]; its cultural bearers are Ark-derived and neighbouring Orphaned communities.

It is told among northern descendants of the Near-Glass camps, especially Jirahar route families and Ahdari well communities. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## The tale

When Tarkhan riders were reported beyond the salt flats, the judges of one camp lifted their staffs. Their fathers had survived by moving before danger closed, carrying names, measures and judgements from fire to fire. They ordered the tents struck before sunset.

The keeper of the new mud wall refused. Snowmelt had filled the well, women had planted barley inside the enclosure, and the fever house could not be moved without killing its patients. The keeper accused the judges of loving the road more than the people. The eldest judge answered that a wall teaches frightened people to mistake possession for safety.

Half the households walked. Raiders caught their slowest wagons and killed two of the judge's sons. Half remained. The first attack broke on the wall, but the second poisoned the outer cistern; children and old people died before rain returned. Neither choice saved everyone. When the walkers came back with allies, the wall keepers opened the gate, but the two groups could no longer agree which duty had proved true.

At the funeral they tried to raise one memorial. The travelling families wanted the dead named beside the road so warning would move with the living. The settlers wanted stones facing north so the dead would keep watch. At last they made both and buried siblings under different signs.

For a time they still exchanged spouses, animals and winter news. Distance grew through repeated ordinary choices, not one proclamation. Children who visited cousins learned that the same dead were being asked to guard two different futures.

## Why it is remembered

It gives sorrow, not abstract doctrine, to the later separation between portable courts and defended oath-lands. The relatives are not fools or villains. Each saves people the other could not, and each pays for what the other feared.

## How tellers differ

Jirahar tellers say the returning walkers brought the allies who preserved the settlement. Ahdari tellers say there would have been nothing to preserve without the wall. Tarkhan versions sometimes claim the first riders were traders killed after being mistaken for scouts.

## What remains

Some funerals still use both a staff laid toward the road and a stone set toward the threatened quarter, especially when kin lived under different laws.
