# The Hostage Children of Oak and Glass

**The Hostage Children of Oak and Glass** is a border tradition about captives whom a proto-Atherian fort and a Folk great clan regularised as fosterlings and sureties. Its central victim was a fort-born man raised among the Folk, married and made adult under their rites, then executed by his birth community as a traitor for warning his wife's kin of an unlawful cattle seizure.

## Historical setting

**c. 2020–1920 BR.** one Folk–proto-Atherian fortified border. This tradition belongs to [[Age of Scattering]]; its cultural bearers are Ark-derived and neighbouring Orphaned communities.

It is told on one proto-Atherian–Folk border where fort households and an old great clan traded children to hold a truce. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## Historical basis

Both the fort and the great clan already held children taken in raids. Their leaders converted this captivity into fosterage and political surety, then added negotiated exchanges among elite households. Rank shaped the child's treatment: some became honoured fosterlings and marriage prospects, while others remained servants under a more respectable name.

The executed man was born at the fort, captured young and raised by the Folk. He completed their adulthood rites and married a Folk woman before returning to the fort as a liaison and interpreter. During a genuine winter meat shortage, the fort commander openly planned to seize clan cattle. The liaison warned his wife. Fort officers called him a birth subject when asserting jurisdiction but cited his marriage and completed Folk manhood rites as proof of treason.

Only fort witnesses were admitted to the night hearing; his wife and Folk kin had no standing. He was executed at dawn. Young Folk warriors then launched an unauthorised reprisal against settlers and captives. Their elders did not order it, but became culpable by sheltering the killers and adopting the violence as a clan grievance.

Fosterlings used knowledge and signals shared across the border to open the oak palisade and the glass-bright fort door. They evacuated children and captives to neutral river ground; they did not admit either side's army. No settlement immediately followed. The dual-kin witness rule developed only generations later, and the paired beads are a still later memorial.

## The tale

Repeated raids left both the southern fort and the Folk great clan holding children captured from the other. Their leaders renamed some as fosterlings and sureties. The children learned one another's songs, insults, kinship rules and weak gates. Some were treated as honoured guests and future spouses; others as servants who could be punished for battles fought far away.

Trade grew around them. A fort-born fosterling became a man under Folk rites and married into the clan before returning to the fort as liaison. When its commander openly prepared to seize the clan's cattle, he warned his wife. Officers arrested him, used his birth to claim him and his marriage to condemn him, then executed him at dawn after a one-sided night hearing.

Young clan warriors retaliated without the elders' authority, killing settlers in the fields and captives in their hall. The elders sheltered them and made the crime their own. Surviving fosterlings ran between both sides, opening one oak palisade and one glass-bright fort door so children and captives could escape to neutral river ground. They admitted no army and were thereafter suspected by everyone: too southern in the woods, too Folk behind the wall.

Generations later, descendants required that no hostage or fosterling be punished unless witnesses from both kin-groups stood present. The custom did not undo the execution or reprisal. It made people who had belonged uneasily to both sides into guardians against either side telling the whole story alone.

Some former hostages refused the duty and moved away. Others married one another and were accused of founding a secret third nation. They had founded households, which was politically troublesome enough without making them a hidden people.

## Why it is remembered

The developing relationships among the foster children pull the tale forward. What matters is not diplomacy in the abstract, but young people used as security who acquire real loyalties their rulers cannot control.

## How tellers differ

Fort songs say the warning endangered civilians. Folk songs say it exposed planned theft. The marriage is historical, though purist tellers on both sides try to reduce the wife to a foster-sister or unnamed messenger.

## What remains

Oak and green glass beads are tied together at later hostage exchanges and border fosterings. They commemorate the older crisis but were not its original surety tokens.
