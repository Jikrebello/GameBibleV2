# The Wolf's Two Sons

The Wolf's Two Sons is a foundation tale associated with [[Avren and Caleth]] and the establishment of Caleran's first royal household. It is told at meals and commemorations in the Aurin country. Its principal variants disagree about the killing of Caleth and the purpose of the wolf who raised the brothers.

This Aurin household telling preserves the rescue, the wolf's purpose and the murder while compressing years of political struggle into a story told over one evening.

## The tale

When the men who had killed their father came for them, Avren asked whether his mother was waiting in the boat. The men told him she was. He climbed in with his little brother, and they bound the children to the seat.

The water had begun to lift the boat when a grey wolf came down through the reeds. Avren pulled Caleth behind him and shouted until his throat hurt. The wolf put one forefoot on the gunwale.

“You will turn it over,” Avren said.

“Then hold still while I bite the rope.”

She carried Caleth by his coat. Avren followed, stumbling, with the loose rope still round his wrist. At the edge of the wood he stopped and looked back for his mother. The wolf waited until he could walk again.

In the wood lived Alvessa, who had once mended Meral's sail. She washed the boys, warmed milk and made beds for them beside the hearth. The wolf brought meat. In winter she lay across the doorway, and the children slept with their feet against her flank.

One day Avren asked why she had taken them.

“Nereth hunts in the hollow where my young were raised,” she said. “When you have a house of your own, I shall need men who will walk beside mine.”

Avren promised her a hundred spears. Caleth promised her a boat, because he had never seen a wolf in one and thought she would like it.

Their mother came back with a scar across her palm. Caleth hid behind Alvessa, for the woman at the door did not resemble the mother he had made from his brother's stories. Meral sat by the hearth and repaired his torn sleeve. By evening he had brought her the other things that needed mending.

The boys grew. Avren learned to take a ridge before his pursuer reached it. Caleth learned to bring a loaded boat through shallow water without losing the grain. Their mother took them downriver, and the people who had known their father found work for them. In time, those people found weapons too.

They fought for the landing and drove its masters from the river. They raised a hall where the families could eat together. Avren sat nearest the weapons; Caleth sat nearest the door, because somebody was always arriving with a broken fitting, a sick child or a complaint about the boats.

The wolf came to the feast. Everyone moved a little farther from the meat.

“Now,” she said, “you have your house.”

Avren rose to call for the spears. Caleth asked who would bring in the harvest and guard the river while the men were gone. The wolf laid her chin on his knee, as she had done when he was a child, and he stroked the grey hair between her ears.

“I can give you food,” he told her. “I can give you shelter. I cannot give you every man who trusted us.”

Avren said that a promise made in the wood was still a promise beside the river. Caleth said the people at the tables had made none. They argued until the food grew cold. Their mother took the children outside.

When the brothers met again, the hall still smelled of fresh timber. Caleth had come to send the grain home. Avren barred his way with a spear.

“Will you kill me for it?” Caleth asked.

Avren thrust before he had found an answer. Afterwards he called for water, cloth and the healer. Sareth held her husband while the healer tried to stop the bleeding. Caleth died on the floor of the house they had built together.

The wolf came when she heard the mourning. She smelled the spear and stood for a long time beside the body. In the telling, nobody hears what she says to Avren. When she leaves, she takes no food.

Sareth carried Caleth to the hill country. Meral walked beside the bier until her feet failed, then rode in the cart with her grandchildren. Avren sent a horse and an escort. Sareth sent the horse back.

The king held the landing. His men went with the wolf into the trees, and fewer came home. At the next planting feast, a child asked why there were two small bowls beside the hearth when only one founder sat in the hall. Meral filled both, and told him how the wolf had found them.

## Variants and customs

Royal tellings make Caleth the first to draw a weapon or attribute his death to a confused struggle among followers. Thesran versions give Sareth a larger part and describe her refusal to let Avren pay for the funeral. Stories told among hunters retain Ravessa's speech about the lost basin; later civic versions often turn her into an ordinary animal and leave out the purpose of her fosterage. Some temple poems call the boys sons of Orionis, a claim which sits uneasily beside traditions that continue to name Damaris and Meral.

The two small bowls belong chiefly to household commemorations. Families set them beside a shared meal and give their contents to children before the adults eat. Some tellers emphasise the children rescued from the boat, others the brothers who failed to share their house. Potter families associated with Sareth preserve pairs whose decoration differs slightly, allowing both resemblance and separation to remain visible.

Wolf images belong to a wider range of later uses. Royal houses display her as a protector; hostile songs give her hungry eyes fixed on the boys. A few local traditions leave food at the edge of a wood, although the sites they identify cannot all be Ravessa's old approach. The survival of an image or custom supplies no complete account of the being whom it represents.

## The tale and the historical account

The narrative preserves the family relationships and murder around which the founding traditions developed. Its dialogue and compressed sequence belong to the telling. Some details, including the precise words exchanged in the timber hall, are unavailable to ordinary historians. The paired bowls continue to be made; no surviving pair has been established as Meral's own vessels.
