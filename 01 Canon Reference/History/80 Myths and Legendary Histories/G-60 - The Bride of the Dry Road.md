# The Son-in-Law and the Stolen Gods

**The Son-in-Law and the Stolen Gods** is a Near-Glass household cycle about a route-wise man who entered a Tarkhan-facing coalition through service and marriage, escaped an exploitative father-in-law with two wives and a mixed travelling household, and unknowingly pronounced death upon the favourite wife who had stolen her father's gods. The former title, *The Bride of the Dry Road*, belongs to a later inversion which made the wife the founder of a separate maternal people.

> **Stories told.** The local telling and the material or customary remainder below are established. A claimed original object, miraculous response, famous identity or supernatural explanation is not authenticated merely by this article.

## Historical setting

**c. 1950–1750 BR.** Near-Glass caravan and well-country. This tradition belongs to [[Age of Scattering]]; its cultural bearers are Ark-derived and neighbouring Orphaned communities.

It is told along Near-Glass caravan routes and among communities formed through marriage, refuge and negotiated departure from Tarkhan-facing coalitions. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## Historical basis

After a conflict over caravan access and wells, a male Ark-descended route guide entered the household of a Tarkhan-facing coalition leader. His service was at once employment, son-in-law alliance and hostage-like dependence. He married women of two coalition branches under separate witnesses and property obligations; the wife he favoured bore fewer sons. Their father repeatedly reinterpreted wages, allotted stock and departure rights to retain the household's labour and route knowledge.

The favourite wife stole portable idols of her father's gods because she feared the consequences of departure and did not understand or trust the God of her husband. The objects were genuine cult images, but no reliable account establishes that they spoke or housed the gods like persons in containers. She hid them in a travelling saddle-seat and invoked genuine menstrual privacy when her father searched the camp. Her husband did not know she had taken them and declared that the thief should die.

The party included both wives, their children and dependants, protected fugitives, allotted stock and copied records. When senior kin demanded the fugitives, the husband refused surrender under guest protection; they killed acknowledged peace-kin and made return impossible. The pursuit ended at a witnessed boundary settlement which barred forcible return.

The favourite wife later died young in childbirth. Many tellers connect her death to her husband's words, but the causation is unconfirmed. Before dying she entrusted the idols to the smaller branch of the household. Their later discovery caused religious and succession conflict between the sons. The husband destroyed or ritually buried the images and refused inherited punishment; the elder branch interpreted that mercy as further favouritism.

This was one mixed travelling household. It did not found the Jirahar, Ahdari or Tarkhan peoples.

## The tale

A route-wise stranger entered the well-country with nothing but his service. The coalition father gave him work, then one daughter and then another, while changing every portion as soon as it became due. The son-in-law multiplied his household by skill, marriage and endurance until the father could no longer retain him without openly breaking the witnessed terms.

They departed with wives, children, dependants, fugitives, records and the stock recognised as theirs. The favourite wife also took the portable gods of her father's house. When the riders overtook them and searched every tent, she remained seated above the images and demanded the privacy due to a menstruating woman. Her father withdrew rather than violate the taboo. Before knowing this, her husband had declared that whoever stole the gods would not live.

The two parties raised a boundary and recited what neither could thereafter seize. The travelling household escaped, but its peace was incomplete. The favourite wife died giving birth, and the hidden gods passed with her trust to the smaller line of sons.

Years later the images emerged during a quarrel over inheritance. The husband broke or buried them and would not punish children for their mother's fear. The elder sons heard only that the favourite had again been favoured. The road songs end with the household still moving and the brothers carrying a dispute their fathers' boundary could not settle.

## Why it is remembered

The cycle joins exploited service, marriage politics, guest protection and religious misunderstanding. Its danger lies less in an idol acting like a speaking device than in fearful people making vows and succession choices before they understand one another.

## How tellers differ

Some versions call the husband a patient servant and others an ambitious hostage who manipulated both marriages. The wife's death may be divine consequence, coincidence or a later moral connection. The red-veil bride who leads widows away is a much later inversion of her story.

## What remains

Boundary stones associated with the tale bear paired witness marks. Several communities bury broken household idols near such boundaries, although no object has been authenticated as one of the stolen gods.
