# The Wolf, the Lantern and the House That Heard Them Laugh

**The Wolf, the Lantern and the House That Heard Them Laugh** is a comic and frightening road cycle about two historical rogues who sought refuge with an immortal witch called the Old Mother. Wolf came from the far-cold hall populations which later contributed to the Ättar; Lantern was a Hartheni outcast. Their attempt to exploit the Mother's hospitality produced three ordeals, a dangerous visit by her Daughter to a village revel and a bargain which cost Wolf two fingers.

## Historical setting

**c. 1900–1400 BR; Hearths and First Kings.** Folk forest margins adjoining proto-Atherian roads. This tradition belongs to [[Age of Hearths and First Kings]]; its cultural bearers are Folk, related Ättar and early Atherian tellers.

It is told in early Folk forests and along the first Ark-settler roads that crossed them. Its survival does not make every singer's version a complete account of the event.

## Historical basis

Wolf and Lantern were real travelling companions beneath the later comic cycle. A loose pursuit company followed them into the forest after the pair had robbed, deceived or betrayed several different households. The Old Mother gave them shelter and safe passage from those pursuers, but did not initially promise to release them from her House. The rogues then mocked her, planned to steal from her treasury and insulted the Daughter attached to her household.

The Old Mother was an ancient human witch whose occult work had made her long-lived, mobile and difficult to distinguish from a court-being. Traditions connect her earliest power to Hecatara and her later independence to bargains, substitutions and rites centred upon the walking House. The House acted as an extension of her presence. The Daughter encountered by Wolf and Lantern was a dangerous nonhuman person; later mortal apprentices and other court-daughters entered her legend. Neither woman was a Menhir goddess, and the surviving tales do not authenticate every claimed bargain or gingerbread house as the same encounter.

## The tale

Wolf was tall, hungry and quicker with a knife than a promise. Lantern was hooded, eloquent and capable of selling the same ward against spirits in three villages before breakfast. The households behind them had compared stories and discovered that each rogue had survived by leaving somebody else with the debt. Pursued into the forest, the pair reached a walking House and asked its Old Mother for shelter and safe passage.

She excluded their pursuers. She had not promised to let her guests leave.

The rogues might still have departed politely. Instead they laughed at the Old Mother's apparent frailty, whispered about robbing her and cruelly mocked the Daughter who served at her table. The House heard all three offences. It answered them in order.

First came the **Borrowed Years**. Wolf and Lantern woke bent, weak and ancient. Doors which had resisted force opened only after they completed the household work they had treated as beneath them. They survived because Wolf accepted Lantern's failing weight and Lantern measured medicine for Wolf's shaking hands. Patience returned the years they had squandered.

The **Treasury That Takes** offered every object they had ever wanted. Each desired or stolen thing became heavier when lifted. The pair first discarded gold, then their loot, weapons and dishonest tools. They escaped the chamber only after abandoning the goods by which they had expected to purchase freedom.

The final ordeal belonged to the **Bride of Teeth**. The Daughter treated one of their boasts as a formal proposal and began preparing a wedding from which the unsuccessful suitor would not return. When the pair asked what she wanted instead, she chose a night of food, music and dancing at a nearby village revel, under their escort and guest-right.

At the revel a coercive man mistook the Daughter's presence for an invitation. Lantern diverted him with a challenge to empty the village's greatest cup. He tried to drink the well dry, fell inside it and was rescued in the morning after the travellers had gone. The Daughter later noticed an unguarded village child who spoke to her without fear and entertained the idea of eating the child. Lantern invoked the hospitality already exchanged; Wolf gave two fingers from his knife hand to seal the release. The pair left the village alive, poorer in weapons and richer in obligations.

Later episodes do not agree that they became good. They learned courtesy well enough to survive powers stronger than themselves, and each retained the other's humiliating true history as both restraint and weapon.

## Why it is remembered

The cycle teaches that a witch's bargain follows its wording, that hospitality creates duties for guest and host, and that cleverness may divert danger without making the clever person innocent. Its village episodes also warn children to respect elders, mind their speech and never assume a strange old woman is powerless.

## How tellers differ

Some tellers make the three ordeals a single night; others spread them across three visits to the House. The Old Mother may appear as helper, enemy or neutral bargain-maker. Several witches claim descent or apprenticeship through the Daughter, while road tales often treat every dangerous hag as another of the Mother's children.

## What remains

Bridges and guest houses bear a long-legged wolf beside a hooded lamp. Travellers leave food, jokes or two small carved fingers where a road enters old forest, although neither offering proves that the walking House ever used that route.
