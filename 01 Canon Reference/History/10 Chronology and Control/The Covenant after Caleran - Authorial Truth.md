# The Covenant after Caleran — Authorial Truth

Author-only rulings behind the reader history in [[The Covenant from Idris to Suhrab (905-1190 AR)]] and the Reconquest articles revised with it. Source decisions: [[Causality Pass - 06 The Covenant after Caleran, 905 to 1190 AR]] under [[Causality Pass - Direction]]. Reader articles show these events through what in-world witnesses claimed; the rulings below say what happened.

## The widowing rule

- A personal instrument binds through its named holders. When the holder and every named successor-holder are dead and no continuing holder is named, the instrument is **widowed**. Human obligations under it become legally doubtful: they bind exactly as far as the parties keep honouring them. Compulsions laid on bound fauna through feeding names keyed to the holder **lapse in fact**. The silt hounds and the Elder genuinely slipped their names in 931.
- An instrument addressed to a holder that cannot die does not widow. That is the single rule behind three events: Gulnar's formula worked (the dyers are right; the foundry masters' "drank itself quiet" is false); Idris's Ember deposit, addressed to the final audit, survived 931; and the Sublime Threshold has held since 938.
- The Threshold's continuity is witnessed at Al-Qahur. Al-Qahur's acknowledgment is therefore a working part of the instrument, which is why its silence can wound a Sultan-Caliph in 1360.
- The hounds were re-bound to the Threshold in 938. The Elder's feeding names have been addressed to the Threshold since the night in the dye-yard; the indigo stain in its seams is real.

## Names, custody and souls

- Custody of a living person's name in a register compels and restricts the living person. It does not touch soul-routing. Only a foundry name-cage closed at the moment of death intercepts a soul ([[Covenant Siege Golems]]). The occupied clergy's fear that held names held souls was false in general; it had a real case behind it only because the foundry took condemned prisoners for its cages and nobody outside could tell which deaths were which.
- An unreleased name after death is a bookkeeping failure and a legal injury to heirs, never a soul trap. The same holds for the Unreleased of the Grey Sweat ("the Emptying of the Benches", c. 1236–1243): the Vault cannot know this, and its living and dead registers never balance. Neither the Vault nor the Church's relic courts knows the other's books also fail. Do not tie the Vault's discrepancy to the Quiet Ninth.
- **Lodging.** A lodged name is taken into Vault custody while its owner lives. No oath sworn to it binds, no instrument the person signs holds, and the person cannot be named in a formal prayer. Lodging is a contract restraint on a living person and is not soulbinding; at death the soul routes normally.
- **The Unnamed Custodian (1011).** A deep surrender of a person's own true name into the Vault's lowest custody withdraws the name's handle from Material record. Written instances fade and those close to the person lose the ability to say it. The ink in the lending house's bond and in his marriage contract faded exactly as the registrars said. None of the three former names in registrars' tradition is correct. The surrender is irrevocable for life: a Custodian holds no property, signs nothing in his own right and acts only through the office. His soul remains his own and routes normally at death. Since 1059 the Custodians' names lie in the Deep Vault's lowest stratum, near the Creditor's catalogued fragment ([[The Creditor]]). Whether that proximity matters is **left open**.
- **The lending house (1003–1011)** was an [[The Under-Ledger|Under-Ledger]] cell, the first of the two the Vault has burned. The loan was designed to default; its object was the Vault's keys, not the Custodian's money.

## The Long Reach

- The remount blessing is a grace on the stock line, laid by the lay brothers who kept the studs (see the march-stud interface in [[Causality Pass - 05 The Guttering, 400 to 905 AR]]). It gives steadiness before **bound things only**: Depth fauna, bound spirits, devil-office manifestations and Slave-Soul engines. It does nothing against fire, stampede or ordinary fear, and horses never needed it against pure constructs.
- It roughly halves with each generation bred outside the purest lines. It decided wars c. 951–1020 and was negligible on most of the frontier by the 1100s. It returned only in one line, through Gavrin's blessing on the black colt (1249), which is why Qarayel won in 1261 by stud-right over that colt's sons.
- Caedros I did send envoys to the khans in 950. They reached one camp, were fed, and were ignored. The raid season of 951 had been decided already for Tarkhan reasons. The Thalmyric claim is true as to the envoys and false as to cause.
- The Talking Hill was the first mercy tower (finished c. 944), not a second engine.

## Caelmount, 917–918

Bent 14 November 2026 by [[Causality Pass - 10D Bending the Long Frontier and the Covenant]]; the Covadonga shape (ambush in a pass, road cuts dropped behind a vanguard, a refuge lamp as the battle's miracle, coronation from the victory) is retired.

- **The Red Star answered the road-menders.** The menders of Caelmount kept the wardens' load-test on their ash road after 383 without licence, as a builders' practice; Astaroth answers builders, so this falls inside the standing practice ([[01 Canon Reference/Cosmology/30 Menhir/10 Second Circle/Astaroth.md|Astaroth]]). Asked where the road would fail under a great load, he showed the span above the ravine and set the price in his grammar: the field (the span), the load (the column's heaviest wagons) and the terraces above the span. The menders paid by turning the terrace channels into the road bed; the soaked ash carried away span and terraces together. Morally blind, he answered Caleran's besiegers in 904 and Caleran's refugees in 917 by the same rule.
- **The lamp.** The hermit's lamp in the store caves was an ordinary lamp kept lit by a hermit, and the deserters who surrendered their instruments by it were real. The Thalmyric Church's preference for the lamp over the Star is a deliberate silence, begun because a Star-rite underwrites the founder's first victory. Eclessian polemic about it is accurate in substance.
- **The break in the middle.** The span failed under the engine and custodians' wagons because that was where the load was, not because the menders timed an ambush. The custodians' rolls lost in the ravine left Qazem's levies unable to say whose instrument they stood under, a small foretaste of 931.
- **Caedros** was not at the road. His act was the promise to feed the valley, and the taking of Virelos (921) to keep it. The coronation of 922 follows from a paid debt.

## Caleran, 931–957

- **Durayd ibn Sahl** did not surrender Caleran. In the second week of the funeral revolt of 957 he sent his household guard to carry the Ember to his ships as the price of a welcome at Iqashar; the keepers barred the basilica, eleven of them died, the funeral crowds and deacons drove the guard out, and he sailed that night for Qabir. He grew old there under the emir's protection and died c. 971. The story of his death in the forum conflates him with the captain of his guard, who died in the basilica. From Qabir he did write offering the southern amirs releases for silver; the amirs had mostly re-sworn already.
- **The keepers' terms.** The surviving keepers made the protected-first terms, not Durayd: the abandoned garrison families, the surrendered quarters and the deposit's discharge on payment. They acted because their pledged names bound them to the lamp, and because they were born in Caleran. This inverts the analog's capitulation by its own ruler (Toledo 1085).
- **The 956 engine.** Its cage held a genuine human soul, released towards judgement when the kindled lamps opened the cage. The cry in "a Caleran voice" was the prisoner's last act of will through the engine's speech lattice. If 05's option of Second Crusade prisoner cores is adopted, the prisoner was a Caleran-born soldier taken at Oronda in 876 and condemned under the 884 ruling; otherwise his identity is open.
- **The Ember's second measure of oil.** The Thalmyric tradition is true. One keeper of the second generation let a deacon light a single lamp from the Ember on each Feast of the Mother, a division the deposit forbade, and entered the oil honestly without a reason. Each year's lamp was carried to a different parish. Covenant jurists' denial is false. The keeper is unnamed in every surviving record.
- **The keepers' pledged names** were genuinely released by Veridun's payment in 957; the Covenant jurists' account of the discharge is accurate.
- The Thirty Sisters and the Thirty-First Crew are untouched by this file. Nothing here explains the Sisters ([[The Weird of History — The Unexplained Register]]).

## The amirates, 957–1052

- **Why they split.** Durayd alive in Qabir left the southern garrisons sworn to an absent living man: their instruments neither widowed nor servable, and useless for defending a wall. Each amir re-swore locally. This keeps the separately sworn amirates and replaces "surrender widowed his instruments".
- **How Thalmyria took them.** Most occupation estates were irrigated from canals whose heads lay in hills the mountain leagues had held since the 910s. Caedros II's engineers took the canal heads valley by valley and set Thalmyric water-courts there, borrowing the Banu Mizan hour-cup. Registration at a water-court was the practical form of accepting return law. River citadels on the Aurin and the two anchor works (the Kedravan Frost Gate and the Arkenan Basalt Draw) needed sieges and Vortharan counter-script. No tribute ever passed between amirs and Thalmyria in either direction: the taifa-and-parias pattern is retired.
- **The bronze tokens** the Southern Houses carried north in 1052 were the occupation water-courts' tokens for canal shares, voided in practice by Thalmyric registration. That is why the War of the Southern Deeds (1131) concerns deeds and water together.

## Saints and sinners carried into reader prose

- **The signal-house governor (HC-N08, c. 910–913).** Authorial truth stays in [[HC-N08 - Taken Trumpet]]; his name, the signal house and the ward's ultimate source remain unknown. The Covenant penitential teaching of the case as "lawful and wrong" is an in-world reaction added in this pass.
- **The General of the Glass Court (GS-11)** and **the Warden of the Last Redoubt (GS-12)**: truth stays in [[GS-11 - General of the Glass Court]] and [[GS-12 - Warden of the Last Redoubt]]. This pass places the General's banner campaigns in the highland towns retaken during the Burning Banners (922–949) and the Warden's punitive assaults at Caleran's outer works (956–957). The Warden's after-death manifestation keeps the date and setting fixed in GS-12.
- **The false rescuers (AF-R15, c. 930–960)** served the Envy Archmask of [[AF-R15 - False Prince Behind the Rescue]]. Reader prose places the episode at a hostage exchange between Durayd's towns and the banner valleys and leaves the power behind it as "something that feeds on gratitude".
- **The burial ban (AF-R11, c. 970–1010)**: the persons were never in the corpse gang ([[AF-R11 - King of Unburied Hosts]]). The commander was Thalmyric.
- **Gulnar** is a real person; the dye-yard event happened as the dyers tell it, with nineteen dead.

## The classical age

- **The two houses of the Vault (1059).** Compartmentalisation means nobody inside can audit the architecture upward. The reform that shielded the Vault from mortal creditors also shielded the Creditor. Whether the 1059 recataloguing noticed an unaccessioned entry is **left open**; it connects to the archivist hook in [[The Creditor]].
- **The Muster at the Wrong Well (1041)** was a genuine copyist's error. Nobody paid for it.
- **The Two Winters (1101–1103)** remain unexplained. Nothing in the Covenant record explains them, and no future document may ([[The Weird of History — The Unexplained Register]]).
- **The War of the Southern Deeds (1131–1139).** The mercy towers' failure is a modelling failure in pure constructs: trained on Long Frontier cities that bargained, they could not represent settlers for whom surrender meant forfeiture. No mind was misled; the engines have none.
- **Suhrab's renunciation (1190).** The Court of Breaches' ruling was sincere reformist doctrine, not a palace bargain. Suhrab did not arrange his brother's death; the stand at Mazrakan collapsed because a contractor skimped the timber.

## Suhrab the Debtor, 1190–1243

Bent 14 November 2026 by [[Causality Pass - 10D Bending the Long Frontier and the Covenant]]. The Suleiman shape (the Lawgiver epithet, a harmonised code, the gifted son imprisoned and killed on the soldiers' evidence) is retired.

- **The *Ledger of Ledgers*** is a book of debts, not a harmonised code. Each book is the account of one occasion on which Covenant law killed the Covenant's own people, entered like a debt (dead, wells, fields, silver) and closed by the clause owed, which overrides every provincial code in that matter only. Suhrab chose the form because the commission of 1151 had spent forty years unable to rank the codes; a debt-clause sidesteps precedence. The books named in reader prose are the Dry Well (Bir Qassim, read on site 1194), the Stand (Mazrakan, 1189), the Dye-Yard (931, the clause against armies sworn to a living man), the Wrong Well (1041), the Two Readings (1137), the Southern Deeds (closed by the Unburdened Deeds, 1212) and the Benches (1243). There are others.
- **His motive** was sincere and partly self-serving: his own title rested on a ruling that an oath made in fear is void, and he wanted a law that admitted its costs. He also wanted the Sealed-Blood minorities inside the Ledger. The Qazmari reading, that entering Husan's blood as a debt was a way to call it settled, is half true.
- **The epithet** "the Debtor" began as an Iqashar crowd's joke after the reading at Bir Qassim and became honourable. Any resonance with [[The Creditor]] is the author's irony only; the Creditor took no part in the Ledger and nothing in-world connects the names.
- **Prince Mihrzad (d. winter 1241).** Not imprisoned, not lodged, not accused. He held the Qarath-frontier forts in the Emptying by riding fort to fort as a competent witness (a prince of the house qualifies under the Ledger), carried the Sweat into three clean forts in his Guard escort, and died at a well-fort where only that escort survived to see it. His name could not be released for want of a competent witness. Suhrab's last book (1243) was written to release him; naming the Guard bench as competent witness was the means. The Guard's constitutional power therefore comes from a father's grief and the jurists' deaths, not from Guard intrigue. The forts he reached held; those he did not opened, which is the shape of Nikandros's half-recovery.
- **Suhrab died in 1243** at Iqashar of old age (b. c. 1165), after the book of the Benches was read.

## Recommended rulings for the 1190–1360 writers

- **Suhrab's son** is Prince Mihrzad, above. Earlier versions in which Suhrab lodged him on forged Guard evidence are superseded.
- **The Cull's turn to the knife** belongs after 1243. Its cause in-world: a court had voided a renunciation in 1190, and in 1249–1252 a court let a lodged prince walk free because his page had burned (the Pageless Prince), so princes stopped trusting either protection.
- **Engine count to 1360.** At 1190 the Covenant holds three great engines: the Elder (Depth-Core) and two mercy towers. Reaching 1360's three operational and two dormant frames requires two more frames, or recovered husks, between 1190 and 1360 from the one foundry. Whether a great Slave-Soul engine is ever rebuilt after 956 is open.
