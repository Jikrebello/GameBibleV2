# The Historical Seed Garden

> [!info] Atlas harvest
> All seventy-five traditions now have localized articles in [[Myths and Legendary Histories MOC]]. Those articles—not this exploratory nursery—state what has entered canon. A tradition and its surviving custom may be real while its heroic or supernatural explanation remains disputed. [[World Historical Atlas - Integration and Coverage Ledger]] records the individual destinations and protected limits.

This is a garden of possible histories, not a second chronology. Nothing becomes true merely because it is told here. Each root is a story strong enough to leave a song, scar, joke, feast, ruin or dangerous habit behind; the exact names, dates and supernatural explanations remain open unless a canonical note says otherwise.

The Garden now leads with the tale. Source comparisons, metaphysical classifications, collision rulings and canon-readiness are held separately in [[Historical Seed Garden - Mythic Voice, Variety, and Localisation Review]].

Plant lightly. Harvest late.

# I. Giant Time, Chains and Exodus

## G-01 — The People Beneath the Bowl

*Where it is told:* among descendants of Giant-household slaves across several former imperial horizons.

### The tale

A kitchen child once dropped a golden bowl at the feet of a Giant steward. The steward struck him, wrote his name among the dead and ordered the cooks to replace him before supper. But the boy was not dead. A washerwoman pulled him beneath the preparation table, covered him with an overturned mixing bowl and told the steward that rats had carried the body away.

The mistake should have ended by morning. Instead, the servants discovered that the dead were less closely watched than the living. A dead scullion could carry bread to a bricked cellar. A dead groom could sleep behind the feed bins. A dead nurse could move through the children's wing after dark. Soon every household had two populations: the numbered servants seen by the masters and the unnumbered people who lived in walls, culverts, ovens gone cold and rooms too small for Giant hands.

They stole food, buried bodies the reclamation works wanted, moved children from cruel households and sometimes opened service doors for whole families. They did not save everyone. When one refuge was exposed, the masters boiled its grain, sealed its passages and left the hidden people to suffocate. Some escaped toward the Glass gathering. Some remained and disappeared during the Drinking Wars. Others betrayed neighbouring refuges to preserve their own.

The tale never names a commander because there was none. It begins with one frightened child and becomes a people only because hundreds of servants decide, separately, to continue the same mercy.

### Why people still tell it

It makes the smallest act of concealment the beginning of resistance. Children love the cleverness; adults hear the cost of surviving through compromises nobody celebrates.

### How tellers differ

Some say the first hidden child later led an Exodus column. Others insist he died under the same bowl and that the game remembers only the lie that kept later children alive.

### What remains

In the game *Beneath the Bowl*, one child hides while one place at the table remains empty. Nobody may claim the empty portion until the hidden child is found.

### What remains open

The first household, the child's fate and any connection among distant versions remain provisional. All participants were ordinary human persons; no secret Saint or Patron directed the network.

## G-02 — The Company of the Broken Measure

*Where it is told:* in the Glassward marches and among peoples descended from Late-Chains estate slaves.

### The tale

A Giant estate measured food, sleep, marriage and punishment with one black rod. The rod allotted half a ration to anyone too sick to work and no medicine to anyone receiving half a ration. When a kitchen girl fell feverish, the steward's commands required that she be both alive for the tally and dead for the stores.

Six household strangers carried her between those two orders. A cook hid her in a flour chest. A stable thief changed the work marks. A bastard of the Giant house—human despite the blood that made him unnaturally strong—held the door while the others crawled through a channel the estate could not acknowledge without admitting its measure had contradicted itself.

The escape succeeded, but its strongest member did not become clean through victory. During a rage supernaturally distorted by the household powers, he killed people he loved. The masters found him beside the bodies and offered an impossible bargain: labour for them and perhaps his guilt would become useful. They sent him against beasts, rebel slaves and rival Giant kin, praising every victory as payment toward a debt that could never resurrect the dead.

At last he returned to the measuring house, snapped the rod and opened one route for the remaining slaves. Some followed. Some spat at him. The family of those he killed refused every gift.

When later singers called the final labour an atonement, that family stood up and left. Their absence became part of the performance: a row of places no triumph could fill, even though other captives owed their freedom to the guilty man's return.

### Why people still tell it

The company proves that an unjust order can break upon its own contradictions. The strongman's story warns that useful service does not transform murder into innocence.

### How tellers differ

Courtly versions make the strongman a flawless liberator. Household songs give the final word to the woman who refuses to forgive him.

### What remains

Children balance seeds on a snapped stick while chanting, “The measure broke first.” Three shrines claim pieces of the original rod.

### What remains open

The company, estate and sequence of labours remain provisional. The contradiction belonged to one estate and was never a universal weakness in Giant law.

## G-03 — The Last Cart

*Where it is told:* throughout the Ark-descended cultures as a tale of the Great Exodus across the Glass.

### The tale

The final cart carried nobody important. It held an old woman who could no longer walk, two fevered children, a potter with crushed hands, a pregnant stranger and the bodies of three people whose families would not leave them to the Giant carrion works. When its axle split, the rearguard captain ordered the drivers to abandon everything that could not move before the pursuing dust reached them.

The drivers threw down their own chests first. They burned spare tent poles into a crooked axle, poured drinking water over the heated wheel rim and cut ceremonial cloth into bandages. When the repair failed, they broke the cart apart and made litters. One soldier surrendered his shield to carry the potter. A provisioner emptied the Prophet's own ration sack and found only enough food because several families quietly added what they had hidden.

The high signs answered only where human effort reached an impossible bottleneck. Shade fell for an hour where no cloud existed. A dry skin gave one last cup. Neither wonder prevented the pursuit from taking people at the rear. The old woman died within sight of the next camp. One child survived; the other did not. The carters returned with fewer people than they had sworn to bring, but more than the tally had judged worth saving.

At the next muster, the provisioners asked how many had arrived. The cart leader answered by placing the dead child's shoe on the counting board. From then on, the rear was counted before the front, though the custom was often ignored when flight became frightening.

### Why people still tell it

It asks who belongs inside a remnant when speed, fear and arithmetic say otherwise. Its significance lies in particular unwanted people, not in claiming the cart saved the Exodus.

### How tellers differ

Jirahar versions remember the names carried with the bodies. Ahdari tellers praise the shared water. Southern songs argue that the pregnant stranger became their ancestor.

### What remains

A damaged wheel-shaped loaf is broken at departures and winter funerals. The first portion goes to someone unable to repay the gift.

### What remains open

The drivers, passengers and surviving child remain unnamed. No wheel, loaf or road miracle can be repeated on command.

## G-18 — The Falling Wards

*Formerly: Petitions of the Cut-Loose Wards.*

*Where it is told:* among Late-Chains descendants whose districts were abandoned during one Giant court's escape.

### The tale

When the Giant court learned that the Great Unbinding could not be stopped, its engineers promised to lift the palace, orchards and noble compounds bodily into the Egg White. They also said the foundation could not carry every district. The ruler ordered each ward to surrender stone, metal, cattle and workers so that “the whole people” might escape.

But the palace gardens were never weighed. Neither were the bathhouses, the princes' tombs or the private aviaries. Engineers stripped the tenant wards instead. They tore anchor mass from houses while families still slept inside, pulled paving stones from wells and closed the climbing gates before the poor understood that the evacuation was not theirs.

At the appointed hour the court rose. Streets bent upward like wet boards. The protected estates vanished into white distance while severed wards collapsed, slid into ravines or remained as islands of floor with no walls. People clung to roads that ended in clean air. Some Giant kin judged expendable were thrown down with them.

The wandering court later sang that its ruler had saved everyone who could be saved. The people below taught their children a different verse naming, one by one, the luxuries never considered for sacrifice.

Their descendants did not agree whether the spared palaces should be reclaimed or destroyed. One band settled beneath a fallen pleasure garden and refused to move, forcing every passing caravan to cross the broken estate whose ornament had weighed more than their grandparents' lives.

### Why people still tell it

The myth gives a face to the word “necessary.” It asks who gets weighed, who does the weighing and which comforts are protected before lives.

### How tellers differ

Court descendants call the severing tragic arithmetic. Surface families call it murder conducted with scales.

### What remains

Several roads end at foundations sliced smoother than glass. Children build houses from flat stones and knock down only the smallest rooms while chanting the names of spared estates.

### What remains open

The escaped court, its present whereabouts and its eventual Patron relationships remain provisional. This is not the copied-ancestor caravan.

## G-62 — The Three False Processions

*Formerly: The Caravan That Cast Off Its Wards.*

*Where it is told:* along ruined Late-Chains pursuit routes and in the contradictory memories of copied Giant ancestors.

### The tale

Another lesser Giant court escaped by a subtler cruelty. Its rulers copied the speech, gait and household memories of honoured ancestors into processional bodies. They dressed these copies in royal colours, gave each a different itinerary and sent all three toward the passages most likely to draw the Hunt.

The copies believed they were the true evacuation. One remembered a childhood orchard that had never existed. Another knew the private name of the ruler's mother. A third begged the servants to hurry because the palace children were already aboard. Human tenants, expendable Giant kin and condemned soldiers marched among them, unaware that they had been chosen to make the deception convincing.

The Hunt found every procession. Route stones split beneath its passage. Bodies, wagons and copied memories were torn apart. While the hunters followed three royal scents, the actual caravan crossed elsewhere after stripping its dependants of food and shelter.

Centuries later, recovered ancestor-patterns still accuse one another of being the decoy. Each remembers watching the others depart. None remembers the hidden fourth road. The escaped court calls the manoeuvre a heroic rescue; the route dead call it a procession held for people who never intended to attend their own funeral.

One copied grandmother repeatedly asks after a living grandchild abandoned with the tenants. The court keeps silencing her because the question survives every restoration. Whether love in the copy is memory, performance or something stranger, the child was real and no procession returned for him.

### Why people still tell it

It asks whether memory makes a victim less real and whether believing oneself royal excuses the ruler who made that belief into bait.

### How tellers differ

Some hunters' songs claim one copy understood and chose sacrifice. Surface traditions insist that this is later propaganda meant to make abandonment look voluntary.

### What remains

Three routes contain palace hinges, royal cloth and ancestor-patterns that cannot all describe the same childhood. The fourth itinerary has never been found.

### What remains open

The original ruler and caravan remain lost. No copy is a human soul, but each separately established personhood and culpability must still be judged rather than assumed.

## G-66 — Not for the Lower Roster

*Formerly: The Lower Roster Refusal.*

*Where it is told:* among mortuary workers whose predecessors served a lesser Giant court during the Late Chains.

### The tale

The court employed specialists whose crafts were too valuable to lose: surgeons, astronomers, furnace masters and interpreters of dangerous roads. When one died, the overseers continued issuing orders in the dead person's name. At first apprentices merely repeated remembered habits. Later an infernal custody route held impressions of skill and forced them through living replacements.

A new surgeon woke with an old master's hand motions but none of his love for the patients. A scribe signed a dead woman's mark while screaming that the fingers were not hers. The overseers declared these successes and placed every useful dead name upon a lower roster from which service never ended.

Burial workers learned what was happening because the bodies arrived with tools tied to their wrists. They began closing each funeral by naming the ordinary duties that died with the person. Then they broke the tools. When the court arrested them, the fraternity attacked the roster house and drove the records, copied habits and custodians down one service stair.

They sealed it from the Material side while some of their own people remained below. The last words heard through the stone were not a spell but a refusal: the full name, followed by “not for the lower roster.”

For three generations, burial workers broke a tool whenever a master tried to issue commands in a dead specialist's name. The practice cost families their livelihoods and sometimes their lives. It also made the difference between remembering someone's craft and pretending the worker remained available to serve.

### Why people still tell it

The tale defends the difference between a person and a use. It matters most to workers whose employers praise them as indispensable.

### How tellers differ

Some fraternities say a soul was rescued. Others insist the soul had already gone and only coercive habits remained. Both agree that the court committed evil.

### What remains

At certain funerals, tools are laid down and struck once after the refusal is sung. Nobody agrees where the sealed stair lies.

### What remains open

No formula proves that a soul is imprisoned or that an infernal route remains open. Names, skills, offices and persons remain distinct.

## G-69 — The Four Faces of One Victory

*Formerly: The Victory Recut Four Times.*

*Where it is told:* along old Sarrakai canals and among descendant boat households.

### The tale

Two river crowns fought over a canal first dug by prisoners. The upstream king seized the sluices during planting season and promised safe surrender to the boat families who defended them. When they laid down their hooks and bows, his commander killed them on the mudbanks.

The first victory relief showed the king towering over the dead. After soldiers complained that allied boat crews had been erased, stonecutters recarved the scene and placed the allies behind him. A later dynasty shaved them away again and added chains, presenting prisoners as property lawfully won. Generations after that, priests cut grieving faces into the margins and taught displaced descendants that every later tenant was an heir of the original murderers.

Thus one victory offered four different meals. Wrath took the massacre. Pride took the triumph. Greed took the sale of families and water rights. Envy took a true grievance and taught it to hunger for the homes of people born centuries later. The human rulers and commanders chose the crimes; no Hunger forced their hands.

Boat households eventually stopped singing the triumphal verse. Before opening the irrigation gates, they began naming those who had surrendered and were killed.

One conquered family refused the new recitation because the same crown still occupied its land. Another joined it so later tenants would not inherit the rulers' lie. The rite never produced agreement, only a moment when water could not flow without the victory revealing whom it had drowned.

### Why people still tell it

The relief shows how a crime changes meaning without ceasing to be a crime. It also warns that truthful grief can be taught to consume innocent inheritors.

### How tellers differ

Royal chronicles compress the recuttings into one restoration. Canal workers say each dynasty revealed the Hunger it preferred.

### What remains

The levee relief still bears four depths of carving. During the boat song, one verse is left wordless while the sluices rise.

### What remains open

The crowns, local Masks, casualty totals and modern property claims remain unsettled. The tale establishes no Idol succession.

# II. Scattering, Hearths, First Kings, City-States and Stars

## G-04 — The Seven Who Stopped Seeking Home

*Formerly: The Seven Who Took the Wrong Road.*

*Where it is told:* across southern Scattering routes by communities that claim one of the travellers as a founder.

### The tale

Seven scouts followed a bell-like sound they believed belonged to the moving Glass people. It came each evening from beyond the next ridge. By the time they learned that the sound belonged to an old Giant marker repeating a command to workers long dead, the main host had moved beyond every road they knew.

They spent years trying to return. At the first winter well, a wounded scout married the woman who kept them alive. At the second, two siblings quarrelled over whether adopted children should be left behind when the search resumed. At a flooded crossing, the eldest died and was buried beneath a cairn that later travellers claimed had always marked the road home. One member deserted with a ferry keeper; another stole food from hosts and was driven into the hills.

By the seventh stopping place, the survivors no longer agreed what “return” meant. One wanted the Prophet's people. One wanted the spouse waiting three winters behind. One wanted only to stop burying friends beside paths they would never walk again. They separated without blessing one another.

Later singers turned the years into a single impossible journey and made all seven founders. The better versions allow that some died forgotten, some became guests and one may simply have gone home to a village that never knew it had entered legend.

At each stopping place, the travellers had to decide which belonging could still be carried: a language, a marriage promise, a burial song or the direction of a lost hearth. By the final winter, “home” no longer meant the same thing to any two of them.

### Why people still tell it

The tale gives dignity to lives altered by delay rather than destiny. Its ache lies in the home that could be recovered only by abandoning the homes made along the way.

### How tellers differ

Seven wells, cairns, towers and ferry stones claim them. No route can connect all seven, and no culture agrees who first chose to remain.

### What remains

At disputed stopping places, travellers leave one shoe pointing onward and one turned toward the house behind them.

### What remains open

The number seven may compress several lost parties. No single genealogy founds every later people.

## G-05 — The Iron Ring before the Crown

*Formerly: The Crownless Company.*

*Where it is told:* in the southern hearthlands among peoples who remember the first cattle protectors and coercive kings.

### The tale

The company began when raiders stole cattle from three villages that hated one another. An outlaw cook, two widowed herders, an unwanted prince and several killers recovered the herd by following smoke no trained warrior had noticed. Their success made them protectors. Soon people called them whenever judges failed.

Then a woman was raped and murdered beneath the protection of a neighbouring settlement. Her body was divided and carried as a summons. The company joined the vengeance. They helped kill men who had fought, children who had not and households that had offered shelter to neither side. When the defeated people faced extinction, the victors murdered another settlement for its women and later seized women dancing at a feast. Some future company members objected; others planned the taking.

The outlaw cook fed the abducted women before he accepted acclaim as the first ruler. He carried his plain iron cooking ring before the crown to show that he had once served the hungry. His victims said it proved only that a man could remember humility while exercising domination.

Protection brought roads, safer herds and coercive taxes. The company that had broken one tyranny became a court capable of another. Later kings preferred to end the story at the coronation. The women taken at the feast taught their daughters a longer ending.

Those daughters did not agree about the company. Some remembered rescuers who later became oppressors; others remembered only the armed men beside their abduction. Their counter-song made the crown hear not one founding judgement but the households that its victory had treated as repair material.

### Why people still tell it

It refuses to make liberation morally simple. People may be genuine protectors and still commit crimes that no later peace cleans away.

### How tellers differ

Royal versions say the iron ring represents service. Women's laments call it the cooking ring of a household stolen before it was founded.

### What remains

Several coronations carry a black iron ring ahead of the crown. Someone strikes it once before the cheering begins.

### What remains open

The company's membership and first kingdom remain provisional. The ring is not the Giant-age collar, though later regalia may mingle their metal.

## G-06 — The Wolf, the Lantern and the House That Heard Them Laugh

*Formerly: The Wolf and the Lantern.*

*Where it is told:* in early Folk forests and along the first Ark-settler roads that crossed them.

### The tale

The Wolf was tall, hungry and quicker with a knife than a promise. The Lantern was small, hooded and clever enough to sell the same charm against spirits in three villages before breakfast. They stole onions from a widow, betrayed a lover who had hidden them from a feud and mocked the household shade blamed for every sour pot in the valley.

The shade was a fraud. The house was not.

When they crossed its threshold carrying stolen food, every doorway returned them to the supper room. Each lie made the ceiling lower. Every guest they struck appeared again at the table with the wound upon somebody else. At first the pair treated the danger as another joke. Then they realised that the betrayed lover had followed them inside and that the house was taking payment from her body.

The Lantern learned the place's courtesy by watching ants carry crumbs through a crack. The Wolf fought the house's chosen champion and won only by surrendering the knife he had used in the betrayal. They escaped with the widow and the lover, but not unchanged: the lover never recovered the hand wounded by their violence, and the widow refused them food for the rest of her life.

The pair wandered on. Some episodes make them kinder. Others show them repeating old selfishness with new excuses. Their friendship endures because each knows exactly what the other did.

That knowledge is both mercy and weapon. Whenever one begins performing heroism for a new audience, the other tells the most humiliating true story available, usually at supper and never without making the consequences worse.

### Why people still tell it

The stories are funny because the rogues deserve humiliation. They matter because cleverness can end danger without undoing the harm that invited it.

### How tellers differ

Folk versions let the lover become a feared road-witch. Southern bridge tales say she went home and wanted nothing more to do with heroes.

### What remains

Bridges bear a long-legged wolf and a hooded lamp. Travellers leave onions and insulting jokes, never prayers.

### What remains open

The pair may compress several wandering duos. The house expressed one local Menhir-facing law, not a universal fairy morality.

## G-07 — The Key Bent in a Murderer's Hand

*Formerly: The Bent-Key Fellowship.*

*Where it is told:* in the heroic city-state belt before Atherian political synthesis.

### The tale

Two elders demanded sex from a married woman and, when she refused, accused her of adultery before a city eager to execute her. A debt thief heard the men's stories differ over the tree beneath which the supposed crime occurred. He cared less for justice than for the tablet locked in the same archive—the record that would sell his sister's children.

He gathered an unlikely fellowship: the condemned woman's servant, a disgraced soldier, a locksmith, a market singer and a foreign widow who had entered the enemy camp under a false promise. Their first aim was theft. Their discoveries linked the false accusation to a network of seized houses, bought judges and military grain.

The woman lived because the elders were questioned apart. The city did not become clean. Riots followed. The foreign widow deceived the besieging commander, killed him with her own hand and returned carrying his head. The fellowship opened prisons, burned some debt tablets and preserved others so that powerful thieves could still be named.

After victory, the locksmith bent the archive key rather than hand it to a new ruler. One companion wanted an amnesty. Another wanted executions. A third used the restored city to begin a dynasty that later removed the original theft from every public account.

The falsely accused woman declined every office they offered. She chose instead to sit outside the rebuilt hearing hall, where anyone summoned inside could see a living person who had survived respectable men's desire, lies and intended execution.

### Why people still tell it

It joins bodily danger, criminal courage and political ambiguity. The woman is not merely a reason for men to become heroes: her refusal and testimony change the city before their weapons do.

### How tellers differ

Legal schools praise separated questioning. Soldier songs praise the widow's killing. Thieves insist the key was bent because no straight key should open a crooked city.

### What remains

At some amnesties people heat keys, bend them and throw them into a common brazier.

### What remains open

The city, final ruler and membership remain provisional. No relic or augury proves the fellowship righteous in every act.

## G-08 — The Road That Refused Its Last Stone

*Formerly: The Road Without Milestones.*

*Where it is told:* along the imperial corridors first measured during the Age of Stars.

### The tale

A young surveyor settled a feud by placing three stones where two villages claimed the same grazing ground. The judgement held through a winter, and an imperial governor hired the surveyor to do the same elsewhere. Soon the measuring cord went ahead of soldiers.

Lines drawn to end quarrels became routes for forced labour. Villages were moved because a road required a straight approach. Workers died hauling stone through heat. Settlements that removed the markers were burned, and their graves became fill beneath the paving. The surveyor protested each extension and accepted the next commission anyway, arguing that somebody gentler must hold the cord.

Years later the road had changed width, language and rulers many times, but it continued pointing armies toward places the first surveyor had never seen. Ordered to place the final milestone, the surveyor left the stone blank. Without the mark, no office could claim the entire road as one measured possession.

The refusal came too late to save the dead or close the route. The surveyor went into exile while traffic continued. Children from road villages began piling small false milestones at the unmarked interval, each bearing a private destination: grandmother's orchard, the burned village, the spring taken for the garrison.

Officials repeatedly cleared the piles. Children rebuilt them farther down the road, so travellers could never be certain whether they had reached the missing measure. The empire gained a reliable highway and lost the power to make its distance mean only what surveyors intended.

### Why people still tell it

The tale asks when useful craft becomes complicity. The blank stone matters because it is both a refusal and an admission of how much had already been done.

### How tellers differ

Imperial engineers call the gap an unfinished triumph. Road people say the surveyor withheld the one stone rulers still wanted.

### What remains

For nearly two hundred leagues the ancient road bears no official milestones, only children's cairns that no census keeps.

### What remains open

No single surveyor made the whole road, and one refusal did not defeat imperial conquest.

## G-19 — The Hearth Carried Twice

*Formerly: The Second-Hearth Clause.*

*Where it is told:* among Scattering cities founded by refugees who later received refugees of their own.

### The tale

The first refugees arrived during rain with no founder's names, no ancestors in the graveyard and no wealth except a covered coal. The city allowed them to build a second hearth beyond the old wall for one winter. They remained for generations, repairing drains, marrying neighbours and burying children in ground everyone had once called temporary.

When the original dynasties returned from exile, they remembered only the first hearth. They said the outer district belonged to guests whose welcome had expired. Officials stopped carrying water to it and withdrew the ward stones during a night attack. Houses burned. People who had maintained the city died outside gates they had repaired with their own hands.

Survivors carried a coal from the second hearth into diaspora. At each new settlement they required two fires at weddings, adoptions and the reception of displaced families: one for memory, one for dwelling. Neither fire could burn without fuel from the other household.

Centuries later, people who knew nothing of the lost city still said that a home had to be carried twice—first in memory, then in the work by which strangers kept it alive.

At weddings, one coal was taken from each household. If either family refused the stranger-descended line, the couple lit no ceremonial fire and cooked outdoors instead. Thus a ruined city's argument survived as a choice made beside ordinary evening food. Outdoor marriages became common enough that refusal itself created a new neighbourhood custom.

### Why people still tell it

The story makes belonging something people do together rather than something only founders can inherit. Its sorrow comes from citizens made foreign by those absent during the labour that saved the city.

### How tellers differ

Restoration songs blame a military emergency. Diaspora versions remember deliberate abandonment. Patron traditions add that an old anchor shard punished the district, but the human betrayal stands without it.

### What remains

Two coals are exchanged at some weddings and adoptions. If one goes out, both households relight it together.

### What remains open

The city's site and any Patron involvement remain provisional. The custom establishes no universal law of citizenship.

## G-56 — The Empty Seat Facing Outward

*Formerly: The Empty Seat at the Outward Threshold.*

*Where it is told:* among Jirahar, Ahdari and southern Ark-descended peoples remembering the Prophet's death.

### The tale

When the Prophet died, the people waited beside his empty seat for someone to rise and speak as he had spoken. The commander held the staff. The provisioner held the keys. The judge held the disputes. The archivist held the remembered words. The household elders held the obedience of families. Each office was real. None contained the commission that had died with the man.

For three days factions tried to place one holder in the seat. Soldiers threatened the judges. Families hid stores from the provisioners. A singer recited the Prophet's last rebuke—that no child of the camp should pretend to own the Voice—and mourners overturned the chair before anybody could sit.

The crisis did not end. Southern captains argued that the people must seize gates and farmland before other powers did. Near-Glass households wanted to remain close to the refuge and carry its practices onward. Families split slowly. Offices travelled together for a generation and then learned to function differently in different landscapes.

The empty seat therefore marks no single council that divided three nations. It marks the loss from which many later disagreements grew.

In one remembered winter, a soldier sat there and declared that the Prophet had named him privately. His own mother pulled him up by the ear and made him repeat the true commission he held: guarding the west ditch until dawn. The laughter ended the claim but not the hunger for a successor. He guarded the ditch.

### Why people still tell it

The story protects the Prophet from being converted into a dynasty. It also admits the terror of having to act when no successor can remove responsibility from ordinary leaders.

### How tellers differ

Jirahar seats face the remembered road. Ahdari seats face defended water. Some Thalmyric memorial seats face south toward conquest. Each orientation claims fidelity without proving possession.

### What remains

An empty place appears in several unrelated assemblies whenever someone claims authority no office can inherit.

### What remains open

No original chair is authenticated. The death began a succession crisis but did not cause the later fissions in one moment.

## G-57 — The Gate Opened during Supper

*Formerly: The First Southern Gate.*

*Where it is told:* in proto-Atherian war-settlement country and among descendants of the gate's earlier inhabitants.

### The tale

Southern settlers reached a fortified gate guarding good water and offered military service in exchange for winter ground. The host people admitted their captains, took some children as sureties and prepared a feast to seal the bargain. During supper, one settler opened the gate to companions waiting outside.

The attackers killed armed defenders and guests who had put aside weapons for hospitality. Others were enslaved. Captive masons rebuilt the damaged wall and pressed their hands into wet mortar so their children would know who had raised it. The settlers divided houses by conquest lots and later called the gate their first lawful foundation.

Yet the city that grew there was not merely a camp of victors. Captives escaped and returned. Hostage children married into both populations. A generation born after the betrayal defended the gate against another invader and refused to abandon either ancestry.

The official foundation feast celebrated opening the gate. Descendant households gathered the night before and ate in darkness, remembering that the first opening happened while people trusted their guests.

At dawn they pressed clean hands against the repaired stones, beside the marks left by captive masons. Children asked which hands belonged to founders. Elders answered that a city can be inherited from both the people who took it and the people forced to build what the victors later praised. No hand is labelled innocent merely because it appears lower in the wall. Several bear broken fingers.

### Why people still tell it

It gives proto-Atherian achievement a human beginning without pretending conquest was divinely commanded. It also explains why descendants can belong to a place whose founding they condemn.

### How tellers differ

Victors say the host ruler planned betrayal first. Host-descendant songs say this lie was invented after the massacre. Mixed families often refuse to decide what no surviving witness can settle.

### What remains

An old repaired gate contains rows of handprints beneath later stone. At the private feast, diners leave their weapons outside.

### What remains open

The gate's exact region, captain and host polity remain nursery material. It was one conquest among many, not the origin of all Atheria.

## G-58 — The Harbour Founded Twice

*Formerly: The Harbor Founded Twice.*

*Where it is told:* around a late-Scattering coast by a Braided harbour people and by inland families who remember losing the shore.

### The tale

A storm drove thirteen southern boats into a bay whose inhabitants had survived the Giant fall by fishing from skin coracles and sleeping above the winter tide. The shore folk gave the strangers dry ground until spring. They even showed them where the current bent around a reef shaped like a drowned hand.

The boat captains did not leave. They cut a ditch across the borrowed ground, raised a stockade and began charging the hosts to cross it. When the shore headwoman tore down the toll rope, the captains seized her sons. Fighting followed. Houses burned on both sides, captives rowed for the newcomers, and an inland kin-band carried away every child it could reach, including children born of both peoples.

Years later a second storm wrecked the harbour fleet. The inland exiles could have watched the stockade drown. Instead, the headwoman's surviving daughter led boats through the drowned-hand passage and rescued the grandchildren of the men who had enslaved her brothers. She asked no pardon and offered none. She demanded the ditch be filled, the captives released and the harbour rebuilt with two open paths to the water.

The rebuilt town kept both launches: the first landing that began occupation, and the rescue that made a shared harbour possible. Its people say their city was founded twice because the first founders possessed it and the second taught it how to belong.

No family may lead both launch days. The rule keeps either ancestry from turning two painful beginnings into one hereditary claim.

### Why people still tell it

It makes mixed descent neither a romance that erases conquest nor a stain that descendants must endlessly repay. The decisive inheritance is the choice to rescue neighbours without lying about what their grandparents did.

### How tellers differ

Harbour songs call the inland leader the Second Founder. Some inland songs say she should have let the stockade drown. Captains' descendants insist the first ditch was protection against raiders; shore families answer that it faced inland.

### What remains

Boats are launched on two feast days. The first uses a single southern-patterned oar in silence. The second uses alternating planks cut in the two ancestral styles.

### What remains open

The bay, peoples and number of boats remain provisional. This is one Braided harbour origin, not the ancestry of all Sestarans, Vesari or coastal peoples.

## G-59 — The Walking Court and the Wall That Stayed

*Formerly: The Court That Walked and the Wall That Stayed.*

*Where it is told:* among northern descendants of the Near-Glass camps, especially Jirahar route families and Ahdari well communities.

### The tale

When Tarkhan camel scouts were reported beyond the salt flats, the judges of one camp lifted their staffs. Their fathers had survived by moving before danger closed, carrying names, measures and judgements from fire to fire. They ordered the tents struck before sunset.

The keeper of the new mud wall refused. Snowmelt had filled the well, women had planted barley inside the enclosure, and the fever house could not be moved without killing its patients. The keeper accused the judges of loving the road more than the people. The eldest judge answered that a wall teaches frightened people to mistake possession for safety.

Half the households walked. Raiders caught their slowest wagons and killed two of the judge's sons. Half remained. The first attack broke on the wall, but the second poisoned the outer cistern; children and old people died before rain returned. Neither choice saved everyone. When the walkers came back with allies, the wall keepers opened the gate, but the two groups could no longer agree which duty had proved true.

At the funeral they tried to raise one memorial. The travelling families wanted the dead named beside the road so warning would move with the living. The settlers wanted stones facing north so the dead would keep watch. At last they made both and buried siblings under different signs.

For a time they still exchanged spouses, animals and winter news. Distance grew through repeated ordinary choices, not one proclamation. Children who visited cousins learned that the same dead were being asked to guard two different futures.

### Why people still tell it

It gives sorrow, not abstract doctrine, to the later separation between portable courts and defended oath-lands. The relatives are not fools or villains. Each saves people the other could not, and each pays for what the other feared.

### How tellers differ

Jirahar tellers say the returning walkers brought the allies who preserved the settlement. Ahdari tellers say there would have been nothing to preserve without the wall. Tarkhan versions sometimes claim the first riders were traders killed after being mistaken for scouts.

### What remains

Some funerals still use both a staff laid toward the road and a stone set toward the threatened quarter, especially when kin lived under different laws.

### What remains open

The attackers' identity and the precise camp remain unsettled. This episode reveals the logic of a long fission; it did not create two peoples in a single winter.

## G-60 — The Bride of the Dry Road

*Formerly: The Bride of the Dry Road.*

*Where it is told:* along Near-Glass caravan routes and in mixed maternal communities formed beside Tarkhan-facing country.

### The tale

Two houses exchanged a young woman to end a feud over wells. Her father's people sent her with a little brother as peace-brother; her husband's people kept her first child as surety that she would not flee. At every feast men praised her as the knot joining the road, though nobody asked whether she wished to be tied.

During a drought, her husband's cousins killed the peace-brother after accusing him of stealing water. Her father's riders retaliated by taking the hostage child. The boy died while they argued over ransom. Both houses demanded that the bride condemn the other and remain where she had been placed.

She washed the two bodies herself. Then she stood before the well and recited each promise the houses had broken. When the elders declared the marriage still binding, she answered that they had already destroyed the peace for which it was made. She cut her red veil into strips, tied them to the cradles of widows, servants and mixed children, and led them down the dry road.

Archers followed. One of her husband's sisters held a narrow pass long enough for the travellers to escape and died there. The fugitives found no promised country; they made camps beside whatever road would receive them. Their descendants remembered kin on both sides but inherited rule through the women who had walked out together.

The first roadside birth was given both dead boys' names and neither house's ownership mark. That decision turned flight into the beginning of a people rather than another bargaining party between the same elders.

### Why people still tell it

The tale is about refusing to let violated obligations become another chain. It also refuses a clean escape: the new people are born from bereavement, and the woman cannot recover her brother, child or sister-by-marriage.

### How tellers differ

Some songs call the bride a lawful judge; others make her a fugitive who stole dependants. A Tarkhan variant says riders sheltered the walkers, while Near-Glass tellers insist the women survived without a patron.

### What remains

A red strip is tied to a travelling cradle when a child belongs to more than one road. It is cut, never knotted, so no house may claim the child as a bond.

### What remains open

Her name, route and descendants remain open. The tale does not establish a universal maternal law or the sole origin of any present people.

## G-61 — The Hall That Faced the Grobi

*Formerly: The Hall That Faced the Grobi.*

*Where it is told:* in northern Folk and Ättar winter country, especially where old halls still face bare uplands.

### The tale

Two related clans held halls a week's march apart. The southern clan became trapped in a feud and sent the ancient split antler that required every cousin to come armed. The northern hall made ready to answer until its youngest herder found Grobi signs under the snow: split hoofprints around the lambing caves, stolen iron and a child's mitten pinned to a thorn.

The northern captain kept the warriors home. The southern hall burned before relief came, and the survivors spat at their cousins for choosing sheep and walls over blood. Then the Grobi attacked the north. They came through the lambing caves while the warriors watched the open slope, killed children in the smoke and carried off captives before the defenders drove them away.

The northern survivors marched south too late for vengeance and too ruined to offer shelter. Around the ashes, one widowed aunt struck the captain with half the antler. He did not defend himself. He set the other half above a new hall facing the caves and swore that his people would never again leave the threatened house for a distant feud.

The southern kin answered that a people who chooses walls over cousins will one day have walls and no cousins. From then on, the two branches shared marriage and song more easily than command.

Years later, southern children sheltered in the northern hall during another raid. They were received as kin but their warriors were not given authority over the watch. Affection survived where the old political obligation did not.

### Why people still tell it

It makes cultural divergence grow from a choice in which every path contained real duty and real death. Neither people owns courage. The wound lies in learning that kinship alone could no longer decide where warriors must stand.

### How tellers differ

Ättar songs say the Grobi scouts planted the antler summons to empty the north. Folk songs call that a later excuse. Grobi tales, where known, make the captured children the ancestors of a clever border clan.

### What remains

Old winter halls keep one smoke hole or roof beam pointed toward the most likely approach of danger, even when every other wall follows the sun.

### What remains open

The clans and attack remain locally placed but unnamed. Grobi pressure contributed to divergence; it did not single-handedly create the Ättar.

## G-63 — The Peace Feast at Nine Hearths

*Formerly: The Peace Feast at Nine Hearths.*

*Where it is told:* among descendants of one Scattering refuge and the settlement that received it.

### The tale

A refugee people asked winter shelter from a rich settlement of nine hearth districts. The ruler admitted them beyond the cattle ditch, but his son took a refugee woman by force. Her brothers demanded judgement. The ruler offered grain, cattle and marriage; the brothers publicly accepted an admission rite that required the settlement's fighting men to lay aside weapons and remain indoors.

On the appointed night the refugees went from hearth to hearth with hidden blades. They killed the ruler's house, then men who had taken no part in the assault, then boys old enough to avenge them. Houses that resisted were burned. Women, children and field workers were bound and divided among the victors. Some refugee elders tried to stop the killing; others seized grain while claiming they had always opposed it.

The assaulted woman vanished from the earliest songs after the first demand for justice. Later genealogies made her the willing bride of the dead prince and called the massacre a wedding battle. Yet the enslaved survivors taught the conquerors how to keep the canals alive. Their words entered lullabies; their daughters became ancestors of the people who denied them.

Generations later, a famine council discovered that the strictest rule against punishing a household for one member's crime had been preserved in the speech of those enslaved after the feast.

The council adopted the restraint while refusing to name its source. Survivor families began teaching it beside the ninth hearth, making the community's best protection speak in the accent its founding story had tried to erase.

### Why people still tell it

The story will not let sexual violence justify collective murder, nor let the horror of retaliation erase the first victim. It matters because the community's best restraint came from people its founders treated as spoil.

### How tellers differ

Official marriage songs say the prince and woman loved one another. Hearth laments call that the second assault. Some refugee descendants insist only the ruler's fighters were targeted; the ash line beneath children's rooms contradicts them.

### What remains

At wedding dances the circle stops at the ninth hearth and leaves one place empty. No bride or groom may be pulled across it.

### What remains open

The woman's later fate and the motives of every participant cannot be recovered. The assault, deceptive disarmament, massacre and enslavement remain the tale's fixed wound.

## G-64 — The Prisoners Who Became a Border

*Formerly: The Prisoners Who Became a Border.*

*Where it is told:* in one City-State and Star-age corridor where travellers still argue about the length of a road.

### The tale

A damned map-lord serving one infernal dominion opened a wound inside a rival's claimed road. Distance ceased to behave. A runner could walk one mile and arrive older; a soldier could forget his name between two doors; a village appeared on both sides of the same hill.

Neither infernal host could hold the wound with stone. They branded captive humans with place-names and chained them at intervals along the road. As long as each prisoner answered to the place cut into the flesh, the road held a shape useful to both armies. When someone died, another captive was given the dead person's mark and ordered to become the same mile.

The prisoners learned to whisper their birth names at night. A surveyor among them carved the imposed names onto loose plates and persuaded the guards that metal would strengthen the line. During a battle, the captives exchanged plates. Roads folded into fortresses; pursuers arrived inside their own prisons; watchtowers opened onto the quarry where their foundations had been cut.

Many captives died in the collapse, and the wound did not heal. The survivors escaped because the two dominions could no longer agree where ownership began. They destroyed the last plates but kept one sled to teach children how rulers had tried to turn persons into places.

The children pulled one another around a short loop, changing names at every marker until the game collapsed in laughter. Adults found the play disturbing. Survivors protected it because choosing and discarding a false name was precisely what captivity had denied them.

### Why people still tell it

It makes the defence of a human name into an adventure rather than a lecture. The prisoners do not win because either infernal side becomes necessary or good; they exploit the fact that two systems of possession cannot finally agree about a person.

### How tellers differ

City-State versions praise the surveyor. Prisoner-descendant songs say a child first exchanged the plates as a joke. Infernal cults claim the collapse was a planned withdrawal and cannot explain why their maps still disagree.

### What remains

A forty-pace road repeats itself when a false name plate is tied to a sled and dragged at dusk. It never repeats in precisely the same direction.

### What remains open

The damned soul, its office and later copies are not the same being. The episode was one summoning disaster, not the outcome of the wider War of the Last Map.

## G-67 — The Marks of Necessary Release

*Formerly: Marks of Necessary Release.*

*Where it is told:* among related Folk and Ättar winter communities formed during the Scattering and early Hearths.

### The tale

After three bad winters, rulers of several halls made a preservation compact. Seed grain was sealed, breeding animals penned and bodies kept in frozen houses until the ground softened. The bargain saved the people. When spring came, the rulers refused to release what they had gathered. They claimed one more month of keeping would make the next winter safe.

The meltwater rose, but the dead did not go to earth and no seed touched mud. Then the High Hunt was declared through the old seasonal roads. It was not a permanent court or an army of human dead. It was a terrible procession of court riders, winter beasts and moving weather that took whatever had been withheld: corpses from roofs, pregnant animals from stalls, sacks from locked floors and living people standing between the Hunt and its due.

Some halls opened at once. Others armed themselves. At one fortified barn, a woman cut the first release mark into the harness of her only mare and led every animal into the thaw. The Hunt passed her house but seized her eldest son from a neighbouring granary where he had gone to free prisoners.

The season turned. Hunger followed because stores had been hoarded too long and then lost all at once. The woman spent the next winter teaching that release can be righteous without being safe.

Her neighbours never agreed whether the Hunt had spared or punished her. She rejected both readings. Her mare returned thin in spring; her son did not, and no correct seasonal lesson was permitted to exchange one fact for the other.

### Why people still tell it

It names the point at which prudence becomes devouring possession. Its grief prevents the custom from promising that correct observance will protect everyone.

### How tellers differ

Folk songs make the woman a widow; Ättar songs make her the compact ruler's sister. Menhir-facing tellers say the Hunt enforced a broken season. Church polemic often calls it only a demon raid, while farmers insist the marks work even when nobody believes the old account.

### What remains

Release cuts appear on harness, burial cloth and barn lintels before midwinter stores are opened or spring bodies are buried.

### What remains open

No mark commands a Menhir or guarantees safety. The Hunt was temporary and local, not the Hunt of the Escaped Courts or the whole War of Unfinished Seasons.

# III. Atheria, the Redeemer, Conversion and the Long Frontier

## G-09 — The Companions Who Entered by the Low Door

*Formerly: Low Door Companions.*

*Where it is told:* in Atherian provincial towns whose ancestors entered imperial service from kitchens, stables, prisons and hostage houses.

### The tale

During a riot, six servants hid beneath a magistrate's dining platform. When fire reached the shutters, they escaped through the refuse door and dragged the magistrate with them. He rewarded them with places in his provincial household: scout, singer, armourer, interpreter, cook and keeper of hostages.

Service carried them upward. They ended a mutiny by taking officers' children. They raided a village after its surrender and called the dead rebels. When a fortress governor threatened both their birth quarter and the imperial granary, one companion revealed the refuge where her own cousins were hiding so the army would spare the larger town. The cousins were killed. The town survived and praised the six as low-born saviours.

Years later they commanded armies and married into houses that once refused them at the front gate. Their friendship did not survive their advancement. The hostage keeper freed three children and was executed for theft of imperial property. The interpreter wrote the earliest account of the betrayal, then removed her own part. The cook kept the bowl of the youngest murdered cousin and made every officer eat from it before receiving higher rank.

Later Lives turned the low door into a sign of humility and omitted the village, the hostages and the refuge. Provincial songs remember that they entered history by saving one man and climbed through choices that left others beneath the table.

At their final shared meal, only four attended. Nobody knew whether the missing two stayed away from shame, anger or death. The cook placed six bowls anyway, including the murdered cousin's, and served the same thin soup they had eaten as servants.

### Why people still tell it

It offers social ascent without pretending gifted outsiders remain innocent once power accepts them. Families love the companions for proving origin need not decide a life and fear them for proving injury does not prevent someone inflicting injury.

### How tellers differ

Imperial versions count five companions and make the dead cousins enemy agents. Hostage-country songs count seven, adding a child who never forgave them. No version agrees which companion first ordered an unlawful killing.

### What remains

Old command houses retain a deliberately low lintel. New officers must cross it carrying a child's plain bowl and cannot wear rank while stooping.

### What remains open

Names, campaigns and later judgements remain open. They were neither one sacred company nor proof that Atherian conquest enjoyed divine command.

## G-10 — The Road of the Empty Purse

*Formerly: Empty-Purse Road.*

*Where it is told:* by western pilgrims, old smuggling families and households descended from the Underground Centuries.

### The tale

A guide who carried believers between safe houses met a mother, a wounded deserter and two children at a river crossing. They had no money. The guide's partner refused them: the next house had been paid for four mouths, the patrol was close, and mercy toward strangers might expose everyone.

The guide emptied his purse into the river and said there could be no paid place if nobody could prove who had bought it. The household beyond the ford admitted all five. Other houses copied the gesture, and an empty purse chalked beside a threshold came to mean that a guest would be fed before questions were asked.

The road grew. So did betrayal. One host informed under threat that his daughter would be tortured. A guide killed a suspected informer who was innocent. Prisoners recanted, returned, lied and sometimes died rather than name a door. Soldiers desecrated bodies to prevent secret burial. No later song can divide the road cleanly into martyrs and cowards.

The first guide eventually arrived at a house whose owner had lost two sons because of the mark. She opened the door but refused to forgive him. He spent the night in the stable and left before dawn, placing no coin in her purse because hospitality was not a purchase and grief was not a debt he could pay.

She kept the mark after he left, but drew a line through it whenever a guest arrived. Welcome survived as her choice, not as the guide's inherited achievement.

### Why people still tell it

Its centre is a small decision about four unwanted travellers within an age crowded by famous witnesses. It matters because a custom of welcome survives without pretending its makers were pure or its victims obliged to reconcile.

### How tellers differ

Pilgrims call the guide a hidden Saint. Descendant hosts call him an ordinary sinner who once chose correctly. One church omits the deserter; another insists he later became a persecutor.

### What remains

An empty purse hangs beside certain pilgrim doors. A guest is received before it may be filled, and payment is placed inside only after departure.

### What remains open

No mark guarantees sanctuary, and no purse authenticates a Saint, relic or apostolic succession. The road touched the Redeemer generation but added no Companion.

## G-11 — The Children of the Closed Temple

*Formerly: Children of the Closed Temple.*

*Where it is told:* in western provincial cities where old sanctuaries were closed during Conversion.

### The tale

When soldiers sealed a hill temple, the lamp trimmers, singers, sweepers, cooks and orphan servants were driven into the square. Custodians who resisted were killed. Converts smashed images in the name of purification; old-cult mobs later burned the homes of converts who had once worked beside them.

Seven former workers returned at night for the things their families had made. They carried away a bronze bird, a black bowl, an ivory comb, a sealed drum and three ordinary tool chests. The porter who lifted the bird without its old cloth died before reaching the alley. The bowl proved hollow beneath its gilding. The comb was merely beautiful. The drum answered once when a child struck it and sent everyone in the room into the same terrifying dream.

The companions broke apart over what this meant. One was baptised and burned the cloth. One sold the bowl as a miracle. One returned the comb to an elderly priestess. Two used the tools to open a workshop for displaced children. A mob eventually found them, killed the priestess and destroyed the drum; or claimed to. Years later, three different cities displayed its skin.

At the companions' last meeting, they carried empty wooden frames through the streets. Each frame represented something lost: a false god, a craft, a friend, a home or a danger nobody should recover. They could not agree which was which.

The procession ended at the new workshop, where children used temple tools to repair ordinary stools. That small work prevented every old object from becoming either a demon trophy or a sacred claim.

### Why people still tell it

It gives conversion a workforce and a family cost. The tale allows genuine conversion, fraud, grief, dangerous objects and ordinary beauty to coexist without making one response fit every thing taken from the temple.

### How tellers differ

Church versions say the bronze bird killed through demonic malice. Old-cult descendants say it punished sacrilege. Workshop songs insist careless handling, not doctrine, explains only that death and nothing more.

### What remains

An annual procession carries empty frames. One is never touched, one is decorated by children and one is burned outside the walls.

### What remains open

The bird's nature, the drum's survival and the companions' final faiths remain open. No object's response proves the truth of an empire or an entire cult.

## G-12 — The Four Who Brought Water

*Formerly: Four Who Brought Water.*

*Where it is told:* in Covenant frontier country, among well diggers, former captive families and soldiers who swear public promises.

### The tale

A conquered town's only deep well collapsed during summer. Its governor promised freedom to any four prisoners who could reopen it. A thief, a physician, a deposed well keeper and a captured enemy captain descended together, each convinced the others would steal the reward.

They crawled through mud, old bones and poisonous air. The captain held a falling beam while the thief crossed beneath it. The physician cut the well keeper's crushed foot free. At the lowest chamber they found that the governor's own illegal quarrying had diverted the spring. They could restore water only by breaking the wall of his private cistern.

Water returned to the whole town. The governor freed the captain alone, claiming the others had supplied skill rather than victory. The four seized the public cup, climbed the well tower and refused to lower water until the promise was spoken again before citizens and soldiers. Thirst forced the governor to yield.

Years later his successor broke a related treaty with an allied household. Retaliation became massacre; to settle the delayed blood-price, descendants of the promise-breaker were executed. The four cups thereafter carried both memories: prisoners winning freedom and an oath preserved through the deaths of people who had not made it.

One survivor refused to drink from them and smashed his cup against the tower. Well keepers repaired rather than replaced it, leaving the fracture visible whenever later rulers praised the beauty of kept promises. The repaired cup always leaks.

### Why people still tell it

The descent is an adventure about mistrust becoming dependence. Its afterlife prevents the satisfying victory from teaching that every sworn debt can be made just merely by being paid.

### How tellers differ

Well-keeper families say all four survived. Military songs say the captain died under the beam. Some Covenant preachers omit the later executions; descendants of the executed begin there.

### What remains

Four mismatched cups are lowered together into a dry well before repair begins. None may be raised until every worker has drunk.

### What remains open

The original cups and town are unknown. This was not the source of all frontier well law, and no miracle excuses the later blood-price.

## G-20 — The Tongue Seal of the Kidnapped

*Formerly: Seal Borrowed by the Kidnapped.*

*Where it is told:* in one multilingual Long-Frontier port built around a Patron anchor and an older harbour people.

### The tale

Raiders sold captives at the port as numbered cargo. One woman, forced to sweep the customs hall, noticed that sailors, anchor servants and town judges used three different words for a person under protection. No single language agreed that the captives were only goods.

She stole a clay tongue seal used by visiting merchants and demanded a hearing in the harbour speech. The buyer called her stolen property; in the anchor-house tongue she answered that stolen things must be returned; in the town speech she named herself an unreceived guest. Dock workers laughed, then began repeating her words. Guards beat them. Someone cut the slave rope, and a riot spread from the fish steps to the anchor tower.

The Patron's human servants withdrew the harbour anchorage rather than let rebels possess it. A quay vanished from under fleeing families. Ships struck stone where safe water had been. Captives, guards and bystanders drowned together. The woman reached the hearing floor but broke the seal instead of asking the Patron to make her people another protected class.

Survivors carried the three phrases into other ports. They also carried the knowledge that clever speech had opened a chance, not delivered everyone. The woman never saw her youngest brother after the quay fell.

Years later, someone speaking his childhood dialect appeared in another harbour and claimed to be him. She fed the stranger and refused recognition until he named a joke no captor's manifest could have recorded. He could not.

### Why people still tell it

It makes recognition a dangerous public act. The kidnapped win the right to speak as people by exposing contradictions among their owners, but no word shields bodies from reprisal.

### How tellers differ

Merchant tales make the woman a trained advocate. Dock songs make her illiterate and the first phrase an insult. Patron loyalists insist the quay failed naturally; survivors name the deliberate withdrawal.

### What remains

Clay tongue tokens are given to strangers before public testimony and broken afterward so the borrowed voice cannot become a new ownership mark.

### What remains open

The Patron's identity and the woman's fate remain open. The seal never changed her human soul or made her the Patron's creature.

## G-24 — The Red Door and the Names Left Behind

*Formerly: Red Door Names; the Red-Passage Witness trace.*

*Where it is told:* along Underground prison-transfer roads and at one yearly memorial whose organisers still quarrel about rescue.

### The tale

A prisoner discovered that the red door between two holding yards could be lifted from its lower hinge. With a gaoler's frightened help, she opened a path used during the change of watch. The first escape saved nine people.

The route could not carry everyone. When the prison filled after a purge, rescuers chose those able to run and one child whose mother refused to leave. To keep the path secret, the witness killed a gaoler who had helped them but threatened to confess under torture. She barred the door behind the smaller group. Those abandoned heard the hinge lift and believed rescue had come. Soldiers executed them the next morning and left their bodies outside the wall.

The escapees later praised the witness as a deliverer. The rescued child's mother interrupted the first celebration and read the names of people left behind, including a sister who had surrendered her place. The witness did not defend herself. She unhinged the door again, laid it flat like a bier and asked that both the rescued and abandoned be read before anybody called the night a victory.

Later accounts say she died as a Saint, vanished, or returned to prison by choice. The tale ends before judgement.

The rescued child grew old and rejected every account that made survival proof of the witness's righteousness. She taught her grandchildren the hinge trick and the abandoned names together, so practical courage and accusation would travel in the same household.

### Why people still tell it

It preserves the courage of rescue without converting necessity, fear or murder into innocence. Every commemoration asks whether saving some gives the living permission to forget those they could not or would not save.

### How tellers differ

Escape families read the rescued first. Prison-descendant families read the abandoned first. Some omit the helpful gaoler because his death spoils both versions.

### What remains

An unhinged red door is carried flat once a year while both groups of names are read. Nobody passes through it.

### What remains open

The witness's final judgement and any later manifestation remain unproved. The door authenticates no relic and summons nobody.

## G-25 — The Ragged Gospel in the Hospital Apron

*Formerly: Second-Gospel Fragments.*

*Where it is told:* in Conversion-era hospital houses, among counter-church descendants and in villages that know only a medicinal recipe.

### The tale

A barefoot preacher entered a fever quarter carrying no recognised Gospel and speaking with such tenderness that abandoned families gathered around him. He washed the sick, persuaded wealthy converts to open storehouses and taught a broth that kept many children alive. People began calling his sayings the Second Gospel.

Praise changed him. He demanded that followers prove freedom from earthly attachment by surrendering children, marriages and property to his household. A grieving mother who asked for her daughter back was beaten as an enemy of charity. When one disciple challenged him, the preacher ordered the man sealed in a cold room to await a sign. The disciple died there.

The household split. Some destroyed the sermons and kept the hospital. Others carried the preacher's sandals as relics and accused the dissenters of murder. One nurse cut the useful broth recipe from a sermon and sewed it into her apron. She removed the preacher's name but left a line commanding that no patient owe service for food or medicine.

Centuries later, hospitals that despise the Second Gospel still use her broth during fever. A cult uses other fragments to demand families, labour and sexual obedience. Both inherit real words; neither fragment turns the preacher into what its readers need.

During outbreaks, nurses repeat the nameless line against service debt before serving the first bowl. Cultists call that mutilation of sacred teaching. Patients call it the sentence that lets them drink without fearing who will claim them afterward.

### Why people still tell it

The myth asks whether a true act of mercy can be retained without acquitting the person who later weaponised it. The answer remains practical: feed the child, free the patient, and do not call the recipe a halo.

### How tellers differ

Hospital versions credit the nurse with every good sentence. Cult versions call her the betrayer. Some church historians think several preachers were folded into one remembered figure.

### What remains

Old hospital aprons contain ragged recipe patches in incompatible hands. Users often know nothing of their disputed origin.

### What remains open

The preacher's name, death and final judgement remain open. No fragment is canonical Scripture or proof of sainthood.

## G-26 — The Empty Stool of the Slain Scout

*Formerly: the Shutwood Hunter trace.*

*Where it is told:* in late Long-Frontier route villages along a dense woodland belt.

### The tale

A hunter hired to find a path through Shutwood saw a bootprint turned toward the expedition's camp. Convinced a scout had sold the route to raiders, he followed and killed the man before asking a question. In the dead scout's boot he found a strip of bark marked with the warning that the eastern ford was poisoned.

The hunter hid the body and used the warning to lead the caravan safely west. People praised his woodcraft. Settlers gathered around the road he found, and his painted sign appeared on inns and watch posts. He accepted gifts meant for the founder who had saved them.

The scout's sister arrived years later carrying the matching half of the bark. She recognised her brother's cut bootlace nailed above the hunter's chair. The hunter confessed, but by then families feared that exposing him would destroy trust in the road. He offered blood-price. She refused it and demanded only that her brother's place remain present wherever the founder was praised.

The hunter continued guarding the route. Some say he died saving the sister's grandchildren; others say she never permitted him near them. He did not stop being useful, and usefulness did not make the first killing necessary.

On his last known journey he walked behind every scout and never interpreted a sign before the person who made it could speak. That changed his craft and saved lives, but the empty stool remained when his own chair was removed.

### Why people still tell it

The audience knows the founder is guilty before the settlement does. Momentum comes from waiting for the dead man's kin to enter the feast and change the meaning of every earlier success.

### How tellers differ

Hunter guilds say he mistook a real traitor's trail. Scout families say fear and pride were enough. Neither version removes the body beneath the road.

### What remains

Route hearings and founder feasts keep an empty stool with a cut bootlace across its seat. Nobody may sit there, even in winter.

### What remains open

No later act settles forgiveness or sainthood. The road is useful whether or not every supernatural story about its founder is false.

## G-27 — The Ninth Room of the Bastions

*Formerly: Sealed Ninth Chambers.*

*Where it is told:* among masons and garrison families in several unrelated early Long-Frontier fortress belts.

### The tale

A prisoner-mason was promised freedom if he raised a wall before the thaw. When exhausted workers revolted, he accepted the commander's bargain: one in ten would die, the rest would receive food and three days' rest. He chose the victims himself rather than risk the soldiers choosing more.

The wall stood. The commander then ordered a chamber built inside it for testing a device taken from a Giant ruin. Eight rooms had ordinary purposes. In the ninth, shackled workers were exposed to pressures the mason did not understand. Some died; some came out unable to recognise daylight; one returned speaking in the voice of a copied ancestor that had never been human.

The mason sealed the room with the bodies and told the garrison the experiment had failed harmlessly. His design was admired and copied. Later fortresses acquired ninth chambers even when builders did not know why. Some became storerooms. Some were used by ambitious rulers for new trials. In one bastion, apprentices found the original tally cut beneath plaster and refused to open the door despite a siege.

The enemy took the wall. The apprentices were killed, but the chamber remained shut. Masons thereafter argued whether their courage lay in preserving a danger or refusing to repeat their founder's crime.

The captured fortress changed rulers repeatedly. Each conqueror found the blank door and assumed the defeated side had hidden treasure. Each was restrained, for a time, by workers who knew that curiosity had once been given prisoners' bodies to spend.

### Why people still tell it

It gives admired engineering a concealed human price and asks when obedience under impossible pressure becomes authorship of an atrocity. The sealed room pulls the audience forward because opening it promises knowledge while repeating the very temptation that made it.

### How tellers differ

Fortress songs say the chosen tenth volunteered. Quarry songs name them as strikers. Some claim every ninth room is connected below ground; builders laugh at that until a wall knocks back.

### What remains

Otherwise unrelated bastions contain blank ninth doors sealed with bricks bearing mismatched workers' handprints.

### What remains open

The founder's later fate, the device and the speaking survivor remain open. The rooms do not form a single hidden network.

## G-28 — The Gauntlets Broken at the Hearth

*Formerly: Rival Broken-Gauntlet Relics.*

*Where it is told:* in late Long-Frontier forge towns and among households descended from one burned workshop.

### The tale

A smith with hands ruined by bondage built tools so light that children could carry them and armour that bent without breaking. When a refuge city asked for an emergency furnace, the smith promised to finish before enemy riders crossed the river.

His youngest apprentice heard the inner brick begin to sing—the warning that heat had entered a hidden seam. She rang the stop bell. The smith, terrified that delay would doom the city and drunk on his own reputation, ordered work to continue. The furnace split. Molten metal and falling stone killed apprentices, maimed neighbours and burned the homes the forge had been built to defend.

The smith survived. Admirers called the disaster a necessary sacrifice and offered him command of the rebuilding. Instead, injured families dragged his gauntlets to the communal hearth and broke them apart. They required him to sell his designs, restore each household and train smiths who could overrule a master when the stop bell sounded.

He agreed, then broke the bargain once, hiding metal for a private weapon. The families exposed him. Some say he finally cut off his own damaged hands; others say the “broken hands” were only his loss of authority. Many later houses claimed his gauntlets, but the restitution custom spread without needing any relic to be true.

The surviving apprentice became a master and rang the stop bell against her own favourite design years later. That smaller obedience is often the forge version's true ending.

### Why people still tell it

The tale turns genius from entitlement into debt. Its most important relationship is between a master who thinks urgency excuses him and an apprentice whose ignored warning becomes the voice every later workshop must honour.

### How tellers differ

Smith guilds praise his repentance. Burned-house descendants say restitution was imposed, not offered. Rival shrines display incompatible gauntlets and accuse each other of profiting from the dead.

### What remains

Forge hearths hang two broken gauntlets apart. When the stop bell sounds, the youngest qualified worker may quench the fire without permission.

### What remains open

No gauntlet authenticates the smith, a Saint or a later manifestation. His final judgement and the truth of his active-history legends remain open.

## G-29 — The Missing Link of the Chain-Bearer

*Formerly: Broken-Chain Tokens and the Missing Joining Link.*

*Where it is told:* along early Long-Frontier slave roads and among abolitionist camps, quarry descendants and recurrence cults.

### The tale

A child taken in a border raid grew to adulthood chained in a convoy that moved prisoners between quarries. He learned every joining rivet by touch. During a flood, he broke one link with a stone, freed the person beside him and passed the stone down the line until the whole convoy scattered.

The escapees made him their commander. He led raids on slave pens and killed guards who surrendered after recognising former masters among them. When followers began chaining captured slavers for public display, he broke those chains too and refused the crown they had forged from the iron. Freedom, he said, could be defended but not worn as somebody else's office.

After his death, devotees gathered pieces of the original convoy chain. Some used open links as promises to free captives. Others believed closing the chain around a new bearer would return the Chain-Bearer's power. They raided graves, murdered rival keepers and forged convincing pieces.

The final joining link had been carried away by a freed family who wanted no hero cult. Their youngest child dropped it into an unknown river, or used it to mend a cooking pot, or kept it. The recurrence cult still hunts for a piece that may no longer exist.

In the cooking-pot version, the family ate from the repaired vessel until the iron wore away. Nothing returned. Their freedom endured precisely because the famous fragment had become useful, ordinary and impossible to collect.

### Why people still tell it

The escape has the clean pleasure of liberation, but the later struggle asks whether a movement can honour a liberator without rebuilding the thing he broke. One missing piece keeps pursuit alive while denying anyone complete possession of the past.

### How tellers differ

Abolitionists say the bearer refused all permanent command. Military orders claim he accepted it reluctantly. Quarry songs remember surrendered guards killed in rage; heroic cycles often omit them.

### What remains

Broken-chain pendants are forged with an open gap. Closing the gap is taboo outside recurrence cults.

### What remains open

The true link, grave and later supernatural activity remain unknown. No completed chain can summon, contain or inherit the human person.

## G-30 — The Clerk Who Would Not Stop

*Formerly: “The Clerk's Ending.”*

*Where it is told:* in early Long-Frontier well districts and wherever rulers invoke an old emergency to govern a new season.

### The tale

During famine, a junior provision clerk discovered that wealthy storehouses were full while poorer wells had no grain. He seized wagons, broke private seals and fed entire districts before his superiors decided whether the order was lawful. Families called him the Hundred-Wells Clerk.

When rain returned, he renewed the seizures. He said hidden scarcity remained and anyone demanding normal rule must want children to starve. He punished merchants who withheld grain, then farmers who kept seed, then widows who refused billeting. His signature sent one old friend to forced labour and authorised soldiers to kill villagers guarding their harvest.

The clerk continued living plainly. That made opposition harder: he took no jewels and believed every extension necessary. At last the same well women who had first blessed his wagons entered his office, extinguished the counting candle and carried every chair into the street. He ordered their arrest. The guard captain, whose own mother stood among them, refused.

The clerk's power ended without a glorious battle. In some versions he accepted exile; in others he opened the prison before hanging himself. Later rulers invoked his famine courage and forgot the women until those they governed asked, “Where is the Clerk's ending?”

The well women never agreed whether he had become a tyrant or merely failed to notice the famine was over. Their disagreement kept the tale dangerous: future officials could resemble him while still doing genuine good at the beginning. That is precisely the warning.

### Why people still tell it

It traces how genuine rescue becomes oppression without requiring greed or secret possession. The forward pull lies in wondering whether the man who once broke rules to feed children can recognise the moment his own rule must end.

### How tellers differ

Officials say he prepared the ending himself. Well songs give the action entirely to women. Enemies blame every famine death on him; grateful families remember the children who lived.

### What remains

At the first new moon after an emergency, officials must snap a counting candle and say aloud why any exceptional power continues.

### What remains open

The procedure can be abused and proves no constitutional inheritance. The clerk's sainthood, damnation and final choice remain open.

## G-31 — The Bitter Cup of the Reaper

*Formerly: the Bitter Reaper's Cordial.*

*Where it is told:* in early Long-Frontier disease settlements, herb gardens and communities suspicious of miraculous healers.

### The tale

A field physician discovered that bark boiled with a bitter marsh herb brought down a deadly fever. Parents carried children for days to drink from his black cup. The cordial saved enough lives that villages gave him land, servants and authority over quarantine.

He began demanding service for each dose. Adults signed years of labour; orphans entered his household; parents who fled found their names posted at neighbouring wells. When a woman refused to surrender her recovered daughter, his guards dragged the mother away and took the child anyway. The physician insisted medicine could not continue unless beneficiaries repaid the living.

Two apprentices stole the recipe. Their first batch was too strong and killed several patients. The physician used the deaths to justify a monopoly and withheld cordial from the fugitives' village. One apprentice returned, knowing she would be tortured, and corrected the measure in public before the fever took her brother.

The settlements broke the physician's stores and copied the recipe onto kitchen walls, herb boxes and songs. They kept the bitter cup but poured its first measure onto the ground for those who died from cure, denial or coercion. The physician escaped, repented, was killed or became something worse, depending on the teller.

The stolen daughter eventually became a healer and removed her former master's name from every measure. She kept one scarred cup only to show apprentices that gratitude for survival never becomes consent to captivity. She never drank from it.

### Why people still tell it

The story matters to anyone who has depended on a person they fear. It refuses both easy lessons: the cordial genuinely heals, and the healer's usefulness does not entitle him to own the healed.

### How tellers differ

Medical guilds emphasise the apprentices' lethal error. Labour descendants name the stolen children. Cults say the Reaper will return when the exact black cup is filled.

### What remains

The cordial remains medically useful. Communities drink it from ordinary cups only after spilling one bitter mouthful onto earth.

### What remains open

The original cup and physician's final fate are unknown. The medicine neither proves a cult's claims nor carries its maker's soul.

## G-32 — The Letters That Must Not Be Opened

*Formerly: Sealed-Door Warnings.*

*Where it is told:* in late Long-Frontier monasteries and courier houses built beside roads that were once deliberately closed.

### The tale

A reader found a packet beneath a sealed roadside shrine. The first letter described a bridge collapsing the next day; it did. The second named a murder before the victim had left home. The third warned that reading the final page would complete a road from somewhere that should not reach the Material world.

The reader wanted the warnings without the danger. He ordered students to open one letter each in separate rooms, arguing that no single person would complete the sequence. One burned from within. One vanished, leaving wet footprints that stopped at the wall. Another returned unable to speak any name except the reader's.

The surviving students rebelled. They sealed each unread page in a different house and erased the route between them. The reader pursued the last courier and died at a locked door, or killed the courier and took his place; versions disagree. The houses inherited letters they dared neither destroy nor read.

For generations, keepers cooled the packets in wire cages where wind could touch them. Most became ordinary parchment. One cage has begun warming again, and children claim something knocks whenever two warning houses exchange guests.

The houses stopped marrying one another because weddings might accidentally rebuild the human path between pages. A recent pair has refused the prohibition. Their choice gives the old fear a new form: preserving a warning may now require sacrificing a life that has not yet begun. Their wedding date remains unset.

### Why people still tell it

Mystery supplies the pull, but the significance is the teacher's betrayal of students entrusted to him. The known temptation remains: an unread warning might save someone, and opening it might finish the path that killed the first readers.

### How tellers differ

Monastic tellers say the first predictions were a trap. Courier families insist they were true warnings corrupted by the reader's coercion. Popular tales add impossible messages that were never in the old packet.

### What remains

Sealed letters hang in open cages along several abandoned routes. Wind may touch them; hands may not.

### What remains open

The sender, route and present danger remain unfixed. No archive is complete, and opening a surviving packet is a future choice rather than predetermined history.

## G-33 — The Tools That Remember a Duty

*Formerly: Duty-Marked Refuge Tools.*

*Where it is told:* in late Long-Frontier refugee workshops, evacuation companies and households marked by a failed defence.

### The tale

A refugee smith made folding ladders, light hammers and self-bracing carts so people could rebuild while moving. A besieged city asked him to turn the same craft toward war. He built a weapon that threw burning metal beyond the wall.

The first discharge broke an enemy tower. Before the second, scouts reported captive civilians in the target quarter. The smith fired anyway because delay might lose the city. The tower fell onto prisoners, surrendered soldiers and families sheltering below. His own daughter, who had gone to negotiate an exchange, died there.

Workshop crews seized the remaining tools. They did not destroy them. On each handle they cut one duty: hospice, wall, water, grave, road, rescue or evacuation. A tool that had raised a war engine could henceforth lift roof beams from trapped people. The smith tried to gather them for another defence, but the bearers refused to return any object unless its marked duty required it.

After his death, stories claimed the tools rang when their maker approached. In truth—or in the humbler version—they rang when frightened workers struck the marks together before choosing whom to help. Collectors still steal them, hoping a complete set will call back the smith. No complete set ever existed.

One hospice owns a hammer marked for graves and uses it only to open trapped doors. Purists call that misuse. Refuge families answer that duties serve living need, not the collectors who want every handle frozen in an old story.

### Why people still tell it

The myth asks whether useful work can outlive a guilty maker without becoming his monument. Its tenderness rests in ordinary tools continuing to serve the people harmed by the most celebrated thing he built.

### How tellers differ

Military songs claim the second shot saved thousands. Refuge songs recite the known dead one household at a time. Relic dealers insist every marked hammer belonged to the smith's own hand.

### What remains

Duty-marked tools sometimes answer a particular obligation with a faint tone. They never ring in unison and cannot call, contain or command their maker.

### What remains open

The smith's final judgement and any active manifestation remain open. Later copies may be inspired objects, frauds or ordinary tools honoured by use.

## G-38 — The Houses Unhoused before the Gate Turns

*Formerly: Public Unhousing Rubric.*

*Where it is told:* in civic threshold rites whose oldest form may reach from the Age of Stars into later imperial use.

### The tale

A city seized a refuge house during war and copied its guest marks into a list of suspected enemies. Soldiers dragged families from beds, killed some in the square and sold others. The gate that had once opened for fugitives was fitted with murder holes and praised as the city's holy shield.

Defenders reported a great winged brightness above the gate during the next siege. Priests declared that Heaven had accepted the conversion of shelter into fortress. Yet when soldiers prepared to burn prisoners beneath it, the brightness withdrew. Hinges split, roofing tiles fell and the defenders were exposed to arrows. The prisoners escaped through the same passage that had trapped their families.

After the war, officials wanted the gate reconsecrated without returning stolen homes. Survivors refused. They climbed onto every participating house, stripped one roof tile and carried the tiles beyond the walls. Only after dependants were rehoused and the guest marks destroyed did they allow the old threshold to serve again.

The procession is remembered more clearly than the reported heavenly sign. Even tellers who deny any angel agree that the city learned protection could depart when rulers used refuge as bait.

One confiscated house was never restored because every lawful claimant had died in the purge. The city made it a night shelter governed by former guests. That imperfect solution, rather than the falling gate, is what descendants point to when asked whether repentance changed anything material.

### Why people still tell it

The story matters because a place beloved for welcome can become an instrument of murder while keeping its honourable name. Restoration requires visible loss and repair, not a cleaner inscription.

### How tellers differ

Church tellers call the brightness an angel under a brief commission. Secular versions call it lightning and rotten hinges. Victim families say the explanation matters less than who was under the falling roof.

### What remains

Before a disgraced sanctuary can be reused, roof tiles are carried out in public and replacement shelter must already stand.

### What remains open

No permanent gate guardian is established. The reported intervention proves neither the city's righteousness nor the enemy's.

## G-39 — First the Betrayed, Then the Witness

*Formerly: Name Before the Title.*

*Where it is told:* in Underground memorial households and early churches that disagree about confession after failure.

### The tale

A captive deacon broke under torture and named the dyer who had hidden him. Soldiers seized the dyer, tore down her workshop and killed her after she refused to name the families who used its cellar. The deacon survived by recanting publicly.

Years later he returned to the community. Some sheltered him; the dyer's mother would not speak to him. During a second persecution, officers demanded another name. This time he refused. He was beaten, imprisoned and executed. Admirers began calling him the Witness Who Returned and celebrated the courage of his final refusal.

At the first feast in his honour, the dyer's mother entered carrying a lamp made in her daughter's workshop. She set it before his and said, “If his courage is true, it began after her blood. Name her first.” Some tried to remove the lamp. Others put out the feast rather than submit. The old woman remained until dawn.

The next year two lamps were lit, the dyer's first. In some tellings the dead deacon appeared in a dream and commanded the order. In the harsher and older telling, no apparition was needed: the living understood repentance only when it stopped demanding that the victim disappear.

The mother never attended again. Her lamp came through a niece who thought the deacon's later courage mattered. Thus even the victim household carried disagreement, and no tidy reconciliation could be imposed by feast organisers. The empty chair beside it stayed hers.

### Why people still tell it

It makes room for genuine repentance and later courage without rewriting betrayal as a necessary step toward holiness. The dyer's mother is not asked to forgive, and the restored witness is not protected from the true order of his story.

### How tellers differ

One tradition names both people and calls the deacon a martyr. Another keeps only the lamps, fearing his cult. A fabricated Life claims the dyer blessed him before death; her household denies it.

### What remains

Two lamps are lit in a fixed order. The first must come from the betrayed person's household or remain dark.

### What remains open

The deacon's final communion and any dream remain matters for later review. The custom authenticates neither sainthood nor every surviving Life.

## G-40 — The Shelter That Outlived Its Heir

*Formerly: No-New-Heir Clause.*

*Where it is told:* in Legalization-era refuge houses and among servants who carried persecuted families between estates.

### The tale

An enslaved woman bore a landowner's child after years of coercion. When his lawful household learned of the boy, both mother and son were beaten and sent away. A wealthy widow sheltered them in a courtyard house and promised that anyone admitted there would remain safe until able to leave freely.

The widow died before making the promise public. Her nephew inherited the house and wanted the mother returned as property. He threw their bedding into the street and had the boy dragged from the kitchen. The household servants resisted. A cook broke the steward's arm with a water jar. Two grooms were killed while bringing a cart through the rear gate.

The servants carried the mother, child and several other dependants to another refuge. Then they returned to the empty courtyard, filled the widow's jar and passed it from hand to hand until neighbours joined them. The heir could possess the walls but could not make anyone serve inside them. Within a month he surrendered the house rather than live alone among locked rooms.

Other shelters adopted the servants' custom: the promise of refuge passed with the water, not the blood of the owner. The boy grew up under another name and refused every later claim that suffering had made him a sacred heir.

His mother became the first keeper chosen by sheltered households. She expelled a donor who demanded access to a servant, proving the water could bind generous founders as firmly as cruel heirs.

### Why people still tell it

The story locates institutional change in servants choosing a particular mother and child over inheritance. Shelter becomes communal because people risk bodies and livelihoods to keep it so.

### How tellers differ

House orders praise the widow as founder. Servant songs begin after her death and name the cook. Some noble versions make the heir possessed; the older tale requires only cruelty and entitlement.

### What remains

A water jar is passed from an outgoing keeper to the next. If no keeper is worthy, it passes to the sheltered households instead.

### What remains open

The family, property and child's descendants remain open. No bloodline inherits holiness, office or angelic protection.

## G-41 — The Silence Kept for Five

*Formerly: Five-Name Rest.*

*Where it is told:* among Conversion-era singers, descendants of temple custodians and choirs that inherited their melodies.

### The tale

A guild singer learned that five old-temple custodians were hiding sacred objects beneath a bathhouse. Hoping to gain a court place, he informed the governor. Soldiers tortured the five for the hiding place, killed them and burned their bodies with the bathhouse beams. The singer received their instruments and adapted their melodies for a victory hymn.

The hymn became famous. Its fifth measure, however, would not sit easily beneath the new words. Some said the borrowed strings went flat; others said apprentices deliberately spoiled the beat. Years later the singer confessed during a feast and tried to speak the custodians' names. Their families interrupted him: one name was wrong, and repentance would not turn him into keeper of the people he had betrayed.

He removed five beats from the hymn instead. One choir later filled the silence by naming the dead. Another kept it empty, arguing that the informer's surviving text could not own them. Their quarrel became bitter enough for blows, but neither restored the old triumph unchanged.

The singer died reconciled to some families and hated by others. The objects he sought were never found, or had never existed. The silence remained when the entire reason for the raid had become uncertain.

His finest student tried once to restore the missing measure for a royal performance. The audience applauded; the choir could not continue. The student removed her own verse afterward and spent the rest of her life teaching why beauty acquired through betrayal may need a wound in its form.

### Why people still tell it

The tale makes art carry the cost of the conditions under which it was acquired. Its meaning does not depend on the old cult being true or the informer's repentance being false.

### How tellers differ

Naming choirs say silence repeats erasure. Silent choirs say using the dead as lyrics repeats possession. Conversion polemic often changes the five into willing martyrs, though the earliest account calls them frightened custodians.

### What remains

Work songs and hymns pause for five beats. Some communities fill the rest with names; others stand without singing.

### What remains open

The objects, the singer's final judgement and the custodians' beliefs remain open. No melody proves a supernatural claimant.

## G-42 — The Supper of the Unchosen Roads

*Formerly: Pilgrim's Table.*

*Where it is told:* in Long-Frontier military schools, caravan camps and households descended from a burned settlement.

### The tale

On the eve of a siege, an old traveller laid seven pieces of bread before a commander. Each stood for a real road: negotiate, ransom captives, retreat, wait for dawn, attack the walls, strike the supply camp, or assault the river quarter at night. The traveller described the price of each but would not choose.

The commander selected the night assault. He believed speed would save his starving army. Soldiers entered the river quarter, set warehouses alight and found prisoners chained inside. Surrendering defenders were killed in the confusion and then deliberately killed so nobody could testify that they had yielded. The city fell; the captives and many inhabitants burned.

After victory, officers claimed the old traveller had commanded the chosen road. The traveller appeared at the victory supper and placed six untouched pieces of bread before them. “You had roads,” he said. The commander struck him and ordered his expulsion. The stranger's identity became the least important part of the account: Saint, angel, spy, conscience or invention, the alternatives had been understood.

Survivors repeated the supper, letting children rearrange the bread and argue what each choice might have cost. The blackened seventh piece is never eaten.

Commanders dislike the children's game because they choose foolish roads, invent eighth choices and sometimes refuse battle altogether. That disorder is part of the custom: alternatives must remain imaginable before authority narrows them into necessity. One child always eats the retreat road before orders are given.

### Why people still tell it

It refuses the comforting lie that atrocity becomes unavoidable because every alternative was dangerous. The commander remains responsible not because a better road was certain, but because he chose with knowledge and later lied.

### How tellers differ

Military versions make the traveller an experienced scout. Devotional versions make him a commissioned messenger. River families say no stranger came; captured officers themselves listed the choices before obeying.

### What remains

Seven pieces of bread are laid at a command supper. One is blackened, and the chooser must name what each unchosen road might have required.

### What remains open

The traveller's nature and the best military choice remain open. Counsel transfers neither guilt nor authority.

## G-43 — The Cart That Turned Back

*Formerly: Dependants' Annex.*

*Where it is told:* in Long-Frontier retreat traditions, veteran camps and families once omitted from a military withdrawal.

### The tale

An army abandoned a failing frontier town under orders to save its weapons and trained soldiers. The departure list left behind debt prisoners, camp followers, wounded foreigners and children whose guardians had died. Officers promised another convoy would come. Everyone knew it would not.

A repair crew driving the last spare-parts cart reached the ridge and heard a child beating a pot in the empty camp. The crew turned back. They threw out metal, powder and officers' chests to make room for people. A captain pursued and ordered them to restore military property; the crew bound him to the axle and continued loading.

Enemy riders arrived before the cart could leave. Rearguard volunteers held the narrow street and died there. Some wounded people suffocated beneath crowded blankets. An old debtor gave his place to two children and was killed after the cart moved. The rescue saved dozens, not everyone.

At the next fortress the crew was flogged for disobedience, then quietly copied by other retreat units. Descendants remember the moment the wheels reversed, not the arrival. Victory lies in turning toward people whom the official future had already counted as lost.

The rescued children later learned that weapons discarded for their weight had been needed at the next defence. Some veterans blamed them. The cart crew answered that a child is not guilty for occupying the space an officer had reserved for powder. Several later became defenders themselves and never forgot the accusation.

### Why people still tell it

It concentrates a large retreat into the sound of one child striking one cooking pot. The audience wants the cart to turn even knowing the choice will cost defenders and leave others behind.

### How tellers differ

Veteran songs claim the captain finally joined the defence. Family songs leave him tied to the axle. Some versions save the old debtor; the earliest pot song does not.

### What remains

Before a retreat, one cart wheel is rolled backward around the camp while dependants climb aboard first.

### What remains open

The crew's names and later punishments remain provisional. This is a later military echo, not the Exodus Last Cart or evidence of a recurring company.

## G-44 — Three Roads for One Cry

*Formerly: Three-Carrier Register.*

*Where it is told:* among Long-Frontier diaspora families, ransom routes and runners who carry news from prisons.

### The tale

A captive woman sent a plea naming the children held with her and the price demanded for each. A town archivist suppressed it because acknowledging the prison would expose a failed treaty. Her brother stole the message but knew one courier could be stopped.

He divided the plea among three strangers. A shepherd carried the names, a thief carried the place and a widow carried the demanded price. They took different roads and promised to meet at a kiln fire. The shepherd was killed by patrols. The thief traded the place-name to save himself, then doubled back to warn the prisoners. The widow arrived alone with a price nobody could use.

Families reconstructed the plea from what each route had touched: the shepherd's coloured cord, the thief's warning scratched on a trough, and the widow's memory of the children's order. Help reached the prison too late for several captives. The woman who wrote the message had died, but two named children survived because the rescuers knew whom to seek.

Afterward, no single runner carried a whole prison cry. The custom prevented easy suppression but sometimes slowed rescue. Every relay remembers that division can preserve truth and still cost lives.

The two surviving children met the widow years later. One thanked her for remembering the price; the other asked why she had not carried a name. She had no answer except the agreement made in panic at the first fire. The question altered the relay thereafter.

### Why people still tell it

The story turns transmission into a race whose stakes are particular children rather than an archive's completeness. It honours imperfect carriers without pretending delay was harmless.

### How tellers differ

Runner guilds say all three strangers volunteered. Prison families say the brother threatened them. Thief songs claim he died returning; town records call him the first betrayer.

### What remains

Three runners exchange red, grey and white cords at roadside fires. None knows the whole message until the recipients assemble it.

### What remains open

The prison and surviving children's later lives remain open. Three carriers corroborate a plea but do not create infallible testimony.

## G-45 — The Two Songs at the Wall

*Formerly: Two Death Rolls.*

*Where it is told:* in one Long-Frontier city where a fallen wall became both victory shrine and place of protest.

### The tale

When siege engines reached the eastern wall, the city called volunteers. Too few came. Officials opened debt prisons, chained prisoners in the breaches and threatened their dependants if they ran. Foreign labourers were promised citizenship after battle but not permitted to refuse service.

The wall held for three days. Free defenders died beside coerced debtors. One prisoner killed the officer holding his chain and then stayed because his daughter was inside the city. On the last morning the parapet collapsed over both groups. Relief arrived before the enemy crossed the rubble.

Rulers composed a single victory song saying every dead person had freely offered life for the city. Widows of the prisoners answered from the opposite end of the street. Their song named beatings, threats and the chains found beneath the stones. Volunteer families feared the second song dishonoured their beloved dead; coerced families answered that honour built on a lie dishonoured everyone.

The city eventually required both songs. They are never blended. As one choir finishes, the other begins from far away, so listeners must turn their bodies to hear the second account.

Some listeners always leave after the victory song. Others arrive only for the widows. Children who stay for both learn that one wall can have been worth defending, one defence can have been coercive, and the same dead can be loved without assigning them the same choice. The lesson requires both melodies and the uncomfortable turn between them.

### Why people still tell it

It lets people mourn a shared defence without erasing the difference between choice and coercion. The second song changes the emotional meaning of the first rather than disproving that the city was saved.

### How tellers differ

Official versions say chains were removed before combat. Excavated links and ankle injuries contest this. Some debt families sing the killer as liberator; officer descendants call him traitor.

### What remains

Paired wall songs are sung from opposite ends of one street. The audience turns between them; no choir receives the final note.

### What remains open

The exact casualty count and supernatural claims around the relief remain open. Death in defence authenticates no Saint or civic innocence.

## G-46 — The Child Who Owed No Wound

*Formerly: No Wound Inherits the Child.*

*Where it is told:* among Long-Frontier dynastic escape communities and households that receive children leaving violent families.

### The tale

A frontier dynasty marked each child over the heart so ancestral suffering would never be forgotten. The mark also decided work, marriage and punishment. A girl born after the wars was told she must avenge an uncle she had never met and inherit the office that had destroyed her mother.

Servants helped her flee beneath a scar-covering cloak. Two were caught and executed. Years later her elder cousin returned with an army, killed surrendered relatives and reclaimed the household in the name of healing its wound. He offered the girl honour if she would wear the mark publicly and legitimise him.

She refused both him and the enemies who wanted her as proof the dynasty should be exterminated. During the feast she opened the cloak, washed the painted imitation from her chest and said the dead could be remembered without being repeated. Children of servants, enemies and rulers followed her from the hall.

The returning cousin repented after his own son died in the purge he had authorised, or only after he lost power. Some households forgave him; others never did. The girl took another name and left no throne, order or sacred bloodline.

She later sent the cloak back without a message. The escape house gave it to another child rather than preserving it as her relic. That transfer changed the object from proof of one famous refusal into practical shelter for repeated, unequal departures. Its hem keeps acquiring repairs.

### Why people still tell it

Its significance lies in an ordinary human freedom: injury belongs to history but does not own a child's soul or future. Escape carries a permanent price because the servants who made it possible do not return.

### How tellers differ

Dynastic versions call her the true heir who renounced power. Escape-house songs say she was never an heir at all. Some cults claim the cloak contains ancestral pain; families use it merely to shelter the living.

### What remains

A scar-covering cloak is offered to any child leaving a violent house. It is returned only if the child freely chooses.

### What remains open

Names, dynasty and later lives remain open. A wound, office, legendary persona and human soul remain distinct.

# IV. Fall, Reconquest, Split, High Noon, Reformation and Present

## G-13 — The Thirty-First Crew

*Formerly: Thirty-First Crew.*

*Where it is told:* among refugees and sailors of the Fall, particularly families whose ancestors never received a berth.

### The tale

Thirty crews were named to carry people from a collapsing harbour. A thirty-first assembled from deserters, dock thieves, fisherwomen and one officer stripped of command. They had no ship, only the promise that one could be taken from a grain syndicate before the tide turned.

They reached the vessel as the syndicate guards were loading their own households. Fighting broke out on the gangway. The thirty-first killed guards, threw cargo into the sea and cut hands from ropes as people tried to climb aboard. When the deck sagged under too many bodies, the stripped officer ordered the line severed. Refugees fell between hull and quay. Some crew members obeyed; one dived after a child and never returned.

The stolen ship escaped the harbour and found more survivors on a rock shelf. Taking them would risk everyone. The crew sailed past, then turned under cover of night and made two dangerous trips in small boats. By dawn the rock shelf was empty, but the people cut from the first gangway were still dead.

Later rulers praised the thirty-first as proof that unauthorised courage saved the nation. Berthless families answered that the crew became heroic only after committing the same selection they condemned. The ship's bell was cracked before its first victory feast so no clean note could close the account.

The crew never agreed who struck the bell. One claimed the child rescued from the rock did it; another said a woman cut from the first rope broke it with a stone before drowning.

### Why people still tell it

The tale makes escape thrilling and morally damaged. It cares equally about the improvised crew becoming capable of rescue and the people they deliberately left to die in order to become that crew.

### How tellers differ

Naval songs say the severed line held enemy boarders. Refugee laments call every person on it unarmed. Some count thirty-one crews; others say “thirty-first” means simply unlisted.

### What remains

A cracked bell is sounded before refugee ships depart. The break produces two notes, one for those carried and one for those cut away.

### What remains open

The crew's membership and relation to the Thirty Sisters remain unresolved. The story does not explain the Sisters or authenticate a surviving ship.

## G-14 — The Six at the Cinder Inn

*Formerly: Six at the Cinder Inn.*

*Where it is told:* throughout Mountain Rebellion and Reconquest country, in mutually hostile royal, saintly, dissenter and refugee versions.

### The tale

Six fugitives shared a burned inn: a shepherd, a dispossessed clerk, a caravan guard, a shrine singer, a smuggler and a kitchen servant. They meant only to survive the night. By dawn they had robbed a tax convoy, freed prisoners and become the people whom frightened villages asked to lead.

War changed them unevenly. The shepherd became a ruler and used his office to take a subordinate's beloved, arranging the loyal man's death where battle would hide murder. The caravan guard killed rivals after surrender because he feared another rebellion. The singer protected refugees and later died in a manner claimed by rival churches. The clerk opposed the new taxes and became a dissenter. The smuggler grew rich supplying both sides. The servant vanished from the official account after opening a gate whose captives were then massacred.

Their children inherited feuds the inn companions could no longer contain. A ruler's household turned sexual betrayal into civil war. Commanders murdered one another at feasts. Widows rejected heroic compensation. Villages saved by the six were later stripped to pay their armies.

Yet the first winter also remained true: six hungry people shared one loaf, chose to free strangers and did not know they would become history. Every later version tries to decide which morning revealed their real selves. The tale refuses.

At performances, children choose which companion receives the final piece. Adults object because every choice becomes an accusation. That unease preserves the company as six changing people rather than six fixed virtues or sins.

### Why people still tell it

It carries the beloved shape of humble companions rising into history while preserving the crimes that make such ascent tragic. No collective guilt erases individual responsibility, and no later holiness launders another companion's murder.

### How tellers differ

Royal Lives separate the criminal acts from the ruler. Dissenter songs place every crime on him. Refugee versions remember the erased servant longest; wealthy houses insist the smuggler secretly funded liberation.

### What remains

Six chipped bowls circle an inn hearth. Each local performance fills them with different food, and one bowl is sometimes removed before the story ends.

### What remains open

Names, final fates, sainthood, damnation and any later survival in Egg White remain open. They were not one sacred roll and did not cause the whole Reconquest.

## G-15 — The Last Common Road

*Formerly: Last Common Road.*

*Where it is told:* on both sides of the Great Split, especially in towns whose graveyards, shrines or markets were divided by the new border.

### The tale

Two childhood friends maintained a road used by both crowns. One became a western abbot; the other an eastern city deacon. When the Split hardened, each was ordered to surrender common shrines and exclude the other's clergy. They kept meeting at a roadside oven to exchange prisoners, letters and bodies.

Partisans discovered the meetings. A western mob beat eastern pilgrims and seized a shrine. Eastern soldiers imprisoned western monks and desecrated a grave while searching for a relic. The friends each defended their own people first and believed the other had chosen institution over friendship. At the oven they exchanged accusations instead of bread.

When fighting reached the road, one friend opened a gate for refugee families from the enemy church. His own followers called it betrayal. The other arrived too late to prevent retaliatory killings and struck his friend in grief. An arrow or thrown stone killed someone between them—versions dispute whether it was one of the friends, a child, or the guard who had started the beating.

Afterward the road was divided, paved in two directions and given two names. Travellers kept using the old oven because neither route possessed another place to bake.

For years, each church accused the bakers of feeding schismatics. The bakers answered by mixing dough without asking who had milled the grain. More than one was beaten; one disappeared. The oven survived because hungry pilgrims defended it in turns while disagreeing about nearly everything preached beside it.

### Why people still tell it

It makes schism personal without reducing theology and history to a missed message. Friendship does not solve institutional conflict, but its breaking gives ordinary people a scale on which the division can hurt.

### How tellers differ

Each church says its servant opened the gate and the other struck first. Bakers say both friends survived and spent old age refusing to speak across one table.

### What remains

A roadside oven has two mouths feeding one chamber. Pilgrims bring different doughs and cannot retrieve a loaf without standing beside the other opening.

### What remains open

The victim's identity and the friends' final reconciliation remain unsettled. The tale neither explains the Bells nor declares either church's whole case false.

## G-16 — The Forty-First Ferry

*Formerly: Forty-First Ferry.*

*Where it is told:* along Grey Sweat funeral routes, among boat families, physicians, grave thieves and households denied lawful burial.

### The tale

During plague, authorities closed forty licensed ferries and ordered suspected bodies burned on the near bank. A widow whose husband had died in confinement bribed a thief to steal him before the burning. The thief brought the wrong body. A physician recognised marks showing the stranger had been executed, not killed by sickness.

They found a boatman willing to cross after dark. Others came: families with decomposing dead, prisoners carrying friends, a priest with no church permission and soldiers afraid their officers would throw them into unmarked pits. The boat made so many journeys that people called it the Forty-First Ferry.

The work was not clean or safe. Bodies split in the heat. Rowers sickened. One family attacked another for the last dry shroud. Guards executed suspected carriers on the bank. The widow discovered her husband had been buried secretly by a guard who loved him; she continued rowing for strangers she had not intended to save.

When authorities finally seized the boat, the physician burned it rather than let officials display it as proof that illegal burial had caused the plague. Boat nails were scattered among graveyards so no single shrine could own the crossing.

The widow kept no nail. She kept the stranger's first burial cloth, washed and repaired, and used it when her husband's hidden grave was finally found. By then she had buried so many others that private grief and public work could no longer be separated.

### Why people still tell it

The story begins with one woman's desire to bury one man and widens through repeated choices. It does not romanticise plague bodies or promise piety prevents infection.

### How tellers differ

Church Lives place a Saint aboard. Boat families name only exhausted criminals and kin. Official chronicles blame the ferry for new deaths; grave evidence shows executions continued after it burned.

### What remains

Small blackened boat nails are pressed into funeral candles. Most are later copies, but their meaning lies in burial carried when law refused it.

### What remains open

The original boat, saintly claims and plague cause remain unsettled. The Quiet Ninth is not explained.

## G-17 — The Company with No Charter

*Formerly: Company with No Charter.*

*Where it is told:* in 1360 AR taverns, caravan yards and neighbourhoods that have begun arguing whether the group exists at all.

### The tale

A failed apprentice found an injured stranger beneath a collapsed sign. Carrying him to safety drew the attention of a debt collector, a shrine keeper and two siblings searching for someone who had vanished. None trusted the others. Each expected to leave after one night.

The injured stranger possessed a token claimed by three institutions. The collector could have paid a family debt by surrendering it. The keeper believed it should be burned. The siblings recognised a mark used by the people who took their kin. While they argued, armed claimants arrived and set the lodging on fire.

The five escaped through a laundry yard with an old cook who refused to leave her neighbour's caged birds. They saved the birds, lost most of their money and discovered the token was not what any claimant had described. At dawn, an official offered protection if they signed a charter placing their finds, testimony and future service under one patron.

They refused to sign. That is as far as the shared story has reached. Some have already lied to one another. One may still sell the token. The vanished person may be dead. The company exists only because each has, for now, chosen not to walk away.

The cook fed them again without asking for a promise. At sunrise, the rescued birds returned to the burned roof instead of flying free. Nobody agreed whether that was loyalty, confusion or merely the nearest perch.

### Why people still tell it

This root plants forward hooks without converting them into foretold history. Its significance is intimate: debt, a missing sibling, injured trust, a neighbour's birds and the frightening first moment strangers become responsible for one another.

### How tellers differ

Because the events are recent, versions differ by gossip rather than tradition. Officials call them thieves. Children call them the Bird Company. None of the names has authority over what they become.

### What remains

A scorched bird cage hangs above the repaired lodging. Locals place unsigned scraps inside when they need help but do not know whom to trust.

### What remains open

Every member, choice, fate and post-1360 outcome remains governed by the Forward Rule. No token proves a Patron, Mask, Saint or conspiracy.

## G-21 — The House That Could Not Be Returned

*Formerly: The Unreturned Ledger.*

*Where it is told:* in Reconquest towns where returning families, occupation-born households and keepers of public works still share streets.

### The tale

A family returned after three generations carrying the key to a house their grandparents had lost during conquest. The occupation-born steward living there had repaired the roof, sheltered fever patients and kept the neighbourhood well open. He also collected taxes for the occupiers and denounced one rebel household.

The returning captain demanded the house emptied before sunset. The steward produced a maintenance book showing what his household had spent and which refugees had been lodged there. The captain burned it, saying no occupier's writing could outweigh the old key. Soldiers dragged mixed families into the snow and killed the steward when he resisted.

The captain's sister entered the house and found her grandmother's height marks beneath newer marks for the steward's children. She could not distinguish which scratches made the wall more theirs. She admitted the expelled families into her military storehouse, angering her brother and other returners who feared every concession would legitimise conquest.

The old house was eventually divided into rooms no claimant wanted alone. Its well continued serving both streets. The title dispute never acquired a clean ending; the story acquired one change instead—the key ceased to mean “give it back” and came to mean “you must answer for who is already inside.”

The captain never apologised for killing the steward. His sister nevertheless required his children to draw water beside the widow. Shared labour did not reconcile the households, but it prevented ownership from becoming permission to make one of them disappear.

### Why people still tell it

It gives occupation-era property complexity a beloved object and a murderous decision. Return, repair, collaboration and belonging all remain morally distinguishable without becoming separable by one document.

### How tellers differ

Returner songs call the steward an informer first and caretaker second. Well families reverse the order. Some say the sister married his widow; others make them lifelong enemies who shared only water.

### What remains

An old key is passed around the well before disputed houses change hands. Every present resident touches it before any door is opened.

### What remains open

The house and claimants remain provisional. The tale does not decide all Reconquest titles or make every returner guilty and every steward innocent.

## G-22 — The Three Nights While Years Passed

*Formerly: Three-Night Exception.*

*Where it is told:* along Grey Sweat refuge routes that once touched an Egg White adjacency, by families with contradictory ages in their household memory.

### The tale

A plague caravan accepted refuge through a white orchard gate after the road behind them filled with soldiers killing suspected carriers. The keeper promised shelter for three nights. Inside, fruit never ripened or fell, wounds closed slowly, and every sunset seemed to repeat the first.

On the third morning the gate reopened. Outside, nineteen years had passed. A mother returned younger than the daughter who had waited for her. A betrothed man found his beloved married, widowed and unwilling to resume a promise made to the person he had been. A child entered as an infant and came out still unable to speak while his brothers had become old men.

Not everyone emerged together. One woman refused the gate because her sick husband had begun breathing again inside. Her sister left and spent a lifetime returning to the orchard at each third dawn. Another refugee crossed twice and aged years between two footsteps. Families fought over marriage, inheritance and whether the returned were truly themselves. Their souls had not been copied or divided; ordinary time had simply failed to remain shared.

The younger mother opened a farewell gift she remembered sealing for her baby. The grown daughter no longer remembered what was inside. They began their relationship again without pretending the lost years could be restored.

They quarrelled over names, inheritance and the daughter's dead foster mother. Their first true intimacy came when both admitted jealousy of lives the other had lived without them. No altered clock could solve that ordinary wound.

### Why people still tell it

The wonder matters through estrangement, not spectacle. People return alive and unchanged in identity, yet home has acquired lives that cannot make room for them without pain.

### How tellers differ

Some call the orchard a Patron refuge; others a Menhir conjunction or fever dream. One family insists only three years passed, another ninety. All agree that resuming an old vow required present consent.

### What remains

Farewell gifts are sealed with a white thread and may be opened only by the person who remembers giving them, never merely by an heir with the same name.

### What remains open

The orchard, keeper and time ratio remain unresolved. It was not a collective afterlife, and no version creates a reusable rule for Egg White travel.

## G-23 — The Citizen Who Gave Away a Name

*Formerly: Nameless-Citizen Petition.*

*Where it is told:* as present-day harbour gossip in one Anchor-facing port where veterans have recently disappeared.

### The tale

An old naval veteran noticed that men who had served on one forgotten expedition were vanishing. Officials said they had left voluntarily. The veteran recognised the same grey carriage outside three homes and learned that its servants could find a person through civic rolls, medals and the public use of a full name.

He sought a false keeper who offered to hide him by taking his civic name. The bargain did not remove or divide his soul. It changed how the city's habits found him. Neighbours forgot why they knew his face. Rain washed the figures from his medals. His grandchildren remembered affection but argued whether he was uncle, father or an old family servant.

Unseen by officials, he entered the carriage house and rescued one captive. The price followed him home: his wife opened the door and treated him kindly as a stranger. He could tell her memories, but every proof sounded stolen. When he tried to reclaim the name, the keeper demanded the rescued captive in exchange.

The tale has reached only this point. The grey carriage still moves. The veteran still wears the coat. One grandchild has begun remembering the blank silhouette in dreams, while another intends to sell the coat to pay a debt.

The rescued captive refuses to explain why the carriage wanted them. Protecting that silence may be gratitude, fear or another betrayal. The veteran can no longer rely on public recognition to decide whom to trust.

### Why people still tell it

Its pull is a live identity mystery rather than a completed legend. The wound is being loved without being recognised, and the danger is that restoring public identity may sacrifice the person rescue made possible.

### How tellers differ

Dockworkers call the veteran a spy. His neighbours remember different family roles. The port authority denies any name can be removed and quietly confiscates drawings of the carriage.

### What remains

An old coat shows blank human shapes on its medals whenever rain darkens the cloth.

### What remains open

Every choice after 1360 remains open. The bargain grants civic obscurity, not soul erasure, and the false keeper's nature is unproved.

## G-34 — The Faces Beneath the Victors

*Formerly: Restored Victory Portraits.*

*Where it is told:* in Fall and Reconquest portrait halls, especially among mixed families painted out of official ancestry.

### The tale

A general commissioned a hall showing the liberation of seven towns. His closest companion, an artist who had fought beside him, painted what she saw: the general's missing fingers, soldiers married to occupation-born spouses, refugee children in enemy clothing and prisoners killed after surrender.

The general demanded clean triumph. The artist refused and threatened to reveal that he had ordered one massacre after the town gates opened. He loved her, feared her and strangled her during their final quarrel. His officers called the death illness. Court painters covered the wounds, lightened faces, removed mixed marriages and painted the general's hand whole.

The hall became a school of patriotism. Families whose ancestors had disappeared from it still came to point at empty spaces. Smoke from a later fire darkened the upper paint and revealed other eyes beneath. Restorers found the murdered artist's repeated signature hidden in Glass-court patterns under every scene, including one portrait painted after her death.

Some believed her spirit continued the work. Others found an apprentice who had copied the mark in revenge. Whatever the cause, the restored faces changed the hall from a procession of victors into a room where concealed witnesses looked back.

The general's descendants tried to close the hall. Mixed families occupied it overnight and cooked beneath the portraits, letting new smoke expose further paint. They did not need the murdered artist to be present for her refusal to remain active among the living.

### Why people still tell it

The tragedy is intimate: a liberator murders someone he loves to preserve the version of himself he wants history to see. The reappearing families make restoration an encounter with persons, not merely pigments.

### How tellers differ

Military Lives blame an enemy assassin. Artist guilds call the general murderer. Mixed households disagree whether all underfaces should be exposed when some living families fear renewed persecution.

### What remains

Smoke or warmth brings wounded hands and mixed households through the later paint of several victory portraits.

### What remains open

The signature's supernatural meaning and the general's later judgement remain open. It does not prove one Glass Court controlled the hall.

## G-35 — The Child's Cord at the Redoubt

*Formerly: the Last-Redoubt Warden trace.*

*Where it is told:* in Fall and Reconquest forts that measure evacuation routes from their smallest doors rather than their grandest gates.

### The tale

A fortress warden received orders to preserve a royal banner and the soldiers around it. As an enemy column approached, civilians crowded the lower gate. The warden closed it so the banner could be moved through the inner passage. Families outside were killed or taken while defenders watched from the wall.

Inside, a mason's child found the official measuring cord used to plan retreats. Its knots matched horses, wagons and armoured men; none measured a child carrying a baby through smoke. She retied the cord with the distance she herself could walk before needing rest and marked every doorway too narrow for two frightened people abreast.

During the next assault, the banner route jammed exactly where her knots predicted. The warden abandoned the symbol, opened a service gate and used the child's cord to move survivors. He lost his sword hand holding the passage and later confessed that the first closure, not the lost hand, defined his duty.

Shrines multiplied after his death, each claiming his severed hand as a relic. Fort builders quietly trusted the repaired cord more. It did not perform wonders. It simply measured the bodies earlier plans had ignored.

The child grew into an uncelebrated builder. When offered a shrine place, she asked for wider service doors instead. Forts that honoured the hand but ignored the measure later failed the same families in exactly the old way. Their relic rooms remained perfectly accessible while their nursery exits narrowed.

### Why people still tell it

The story changes defence from preserving the most prestigious object to making room for the weakest person who must escape. The child's practical act outlives heroic body relics because anyone can test it.

### How tellers differ

Military versions say the warden ordered the child to measure. Mason families say he first threatened punishment for damaging official equipment. Rival shrines cannot agree which hand was lost.

### What remains

Evacuation walls contain a knotted child's cord or its measured copy. Drills begin at the smallest inhabited room.

### What remains open

No hand relic or cord proves sainthood. The child, fort and warden's final fate remain provisional.

## G-36 — The Black Eaves and the Uncommitted Crime

*Formerly: the Black-Eaves prohibition and forecast market.*

*Where it is told:* in districts rebuilt after the Split where black-roofed houses became associated with illicit prediction.

### The tale

A judge received a forecast naming a cooper as the man who would kill three neighbours beneath black eaves. The cooper had committed no such crime. The judge arrested him anyway and ordered torture until he confessed what he had not yet done.

His brothers attacked the prison. A guard died, houses burned, and the cooper escaped maddened by pain. He found the prophet who had sold the forecast and killed him beneath the black roof, along with two neighbours who tried to intervene. The prediction came true because people treated it as settled fact, though not in the way any of them had expected.

The judge watched the three bodies carried out and prohibited conviction for “the future fact.” He did not free everyone held by his earlier forecasts, and the cooper's surviving family received no restoration. Prediction sellers moved into markets under the eaves, offering rulers outcomes that became more valuable when believed.

Years later, a child bought a tile scratched with her mother's supposed betrayal. She smashed it before reading the date. The market called that proof the forecast had frightened her away from truth; her family called it the first free act in the story.

The mother eventually did betray someone, but not the person or cause named on the broken tile. The family kept both facts: an untrue prophecy had no claim on her, and later freedom did not make every choice innocent. Choice remained hers in both acts.

### Why people still tell it

The tale asks how knowledge of a possible act alters the choices that make it real. It holds fast to the moral point that guilt belongs to acts freely done, not to an image of the future imposed beforehand.

### How tellers differ

Judges say the prohibition began in remorse. Cooper descendants say it was imposed after riots. Idol cults claim the first prophet wore a true Mask; surviving predictions prove only that somebody wanted them believed.

### What remains

Black roof tiles scratched with future crimes circulate illegally. Families sometimes smash them unread above the threshold.

### What remains open

No forecast transfers guilt, compels its fulfilment or proves a Mask. The present market and future choices remain open.

## G-37 — The Feast Followed by Ashen Names

*Formerly: the paired feast of the Ashen-Wings Saint.*

*Where it is told:* in High-Noon communities liberated from one slave system and among families of people used in the liberators' experiments.

### The tale

A rebel commander discovered that captives altered by an enemy engine could cross a killing field unharmed. He promised the prisoners freedom if they volunteered to guide the army. When too few agreed, his officers selected others and lied that the alteration could be reversed.

The experiment burned lungs, fused armour to skin and killed children placed near the engine as controls. Those who survived opened the field and made liberation possible. At victory, the commander hid them in a distant hospice so the public would see unscarred heroes. He feared the enemy would use the victims as propaganda and feared, more deeply, what their bodies said about his own choice.

The next liberation feast filled the streets with white bread and bright wings. At dawn the families of experimental victims carried ash into the square, spoke the names of the unwilling and mixed the feast ashes into black loaves. Soldiers tried to stop them. The commander ordered the soldiers aside and listened, or fled before the reading; versions divide there.

The public celebration continued. So did the unofficial second morning. Neither observance was allowed to consume the other. Freedom remained worth celebrating, and the people harmed in obtaining it remained people, not the necessary fuel of a legend.

Some altered survivors joined the first feast and refused the second. Others did the reverse. Their choices prevented later organisers from assigning all victims one political meaning merely because the commander had once assigned their bodies one military use.

### Why people still tell it

It protects liberation from cynicism and victims from patriotic erasure. The joyful first day gains moral weight because the second forces celebrants to meet the cost their victory story would rather hide.

### How tellers differ

Saintly Lives say the commander confessed and spent his life serving survivors. Victim families preserve versions in which he never apologised. Some identify him with the Ashen-Wings Saint; the feast cannot prove it.

### What remains

Bright liberation bread is eaten publicly. The next morning, black bread made with feast ash is shared while experimental victims are named.

### What remains open

The commander's judgement, saintly identity and later manifestation remain open. No victim's alteration changes the person's human soul.

## G-47 — Three Notes before the Spoils

*Formerly: Protected-Name Signal.*

*Where it is told:* on Fall and Reconquest surrender fields, by horn players, camp rescuers and families once counted among captured property.

### The tale

A city surrendered under terms protecting children, prisoners, healers and households of mixed descent. Before the ink dried, victorious soldiers rushed the lower streets for loot. Officers argued over which houses belonged to traitors while captives were dragged out by hair and clothing.

A young horn player climbed the breached wall and sounded three broken notes: the old field signal meaning, “Name the protected before dividing the spoil.” Rescuers began shouting children's names, then prisoners', then the names of occupation-born families promised safety. Soldiers cut the signaller's lips and smashed the horn. Two rescuers were mutilated; others were killed while standing across doors.

The call passed from instrument to instrument. Pots, bells and shouted notes answered from alleys. Enough people heard to hide families and force the commander to repeat the surrender terms before his army. The city was still plundered. Some protected people died. The commander later claimed the signal had been his order and erased the punishment of its first players.

Horn families kept the melody deliberately cracked so it could never sound like a triumph. It interrupts victory music even when commanders dislike the reminder.

At one later surrender, a nervous child played only two notes. Street defenders supplied the missing third by shouting the names they knew. The call worked because people accepted its duty, not because the original instrument or exact sound had returned. Nobody remembers whether the child finished the tune. The names were enough.

### Why people still tell it

The story has the urgency of a countdown: if names are spoken before the rush reaches each house, somebody may live. Its courage belongs to people who use a military sound against the appetite of their own victorious side.

### How tellers differ

Army chronicles call the mutilation enemy action. Street songs name the victorious unit. Devotional versions add a heavenly echo; the human players remain sufficient to the event.

### What remains

A cracked horn plays three incomplete notes before spoils, captured houses or evacuated beds are assigned.

### What remains open

No angelic commission or righteous Reconquest is established. The original horn, city and signaller's survival remain open.

## G-48 — The Threshing Floor beyond the Count

*Formerly: Floor Beyond the Count.*

*Where it is told:* in High-Noon harvest districts where a useful census became a route for soldiers and death.

### The tale

A ruler ordered every household counted so grain could be moved before drought. The count worked. Storehouses opened, roads were repaired and distant villages received seed. Then the ruler secretly marked households suspected of disloyalty on the same tally.

Soldiers followed the counters. They seized sons, broke doors and killed resisters. Families hid children beneath grain, but the marks led patrols to every barn. Soon sickness or a judgement no chronicler could name moved along the counting route as well, striking officers and peasants without distinction.

At the edge of one village, an old woman continued threshing after everyone else fled. The mortality reached her floor and stopped. Some say a blazing messenger stood between the falling grain and the soldiers. Others say the troops finally refused orders after seeing their own children sick. The old woman simply swept a circle clean and poured the household counters into the chaff.

The ruler ended the killings but blamed clerks for misusing his command. Survivors forced every later count to spill grain outside the total: a visible portion belonging to people whom no number could make available for seizure.

The old woman refused a court reward and asked instead for the marked doors to be repaired. Some had no survivors to reopen them. Their unclaimed grain was given away without adding the households to another list. The empty thresholds remained, making the cost visible after the food was gone. Nobody counted those doors again.

### Why people still tell it

The tale turns a mundane good—knowing how many mouths need food—into dread as the same knowledge guides violence. The humble stopping place matters more than identifying the wonder because a worker makes the limit visible.

### How tellers differ

Court histories call the deaths plague. Devotional versions name a commissioned angel. Harvest songs say exhausted soldiers saw the woman and disobeyed. None acquits the ruler's second use of the count.

### What remains

After communal threshing, one circle of grain is left uncounted and distributed by hand.

### What remains open

The stopping agency and ruler's repentance remain unsettled. The tale does not explain the Grey Sweat or establish a reusable heavenly instrument.

## G-49 — The Two Tags on One Dead Man

*Formerly: Double-Tag Docket.*

*Where it is told:* in Reformation workhouse towns and, in 1360, among two families facing the consequences of an old exchange.

### The tale

Two debt workers shared similar names. One had children who would be seized with him; the other had no dependants but was too sick to finish his term. They exchanged neck tags so the first family could flee while officials believed its father remained at work.

The sick man died under the borrowed name after overseers denied treatment for malingering. His body was sent to the escaped family, which buried him secretly rather than expose the fraud. His own sister received an empty coffin and spent years believing he had been sold elsewhere.

The escapee lived, raised his children and never told them another person lay beneath their family stone. Funeral wax eventually fused the two corroded tags. When descendants discovered them, one family demanded the grave and name restored. The other feared that confession would revive an old debt now held by a broker who had bought the workhouse claims.

The broker has threatened living dependants. One descendant wants to break the tags and preserve both families. Another believes continued silence repeats the original abandonment. No remedy has yet been chosen.

The dead man's sister is known only through a lullaby both families still sing with different names. If they meet before deciding the grave, the song may establish kinship—or prove merely that workhouse children shared music in the same dormitory. Either discovery would change who is entitled to mourn and who must answer for the years of silence.

### Why people still tell it

The clever swap first appears to save a family, then acquires the weight of a neglected dying man and a sister denied mourning. The present mystery matters because every truthful act may harm someone who did not make the bargain.

### How tellers differ

One household calls the dead worker a willing rescuer. His sister's descendants say he was too ill to consent freely. Workhouse records record only one name and therefore settle nothing.

### What remains

Two neck tags remain fused by funeral wax. Scraping them apart may destroy both inscriptions.

### What remains open

The present remedy and all post-1360 outcomes remain open. No supernatural guardian, inherited guilt or infallible record is established.

## G-65 — The Returners' Second Sack

*Formerly: The Returners' Second Sack.*

*Where it is told:* in one provincial Reconquest town, especially by hospital families and people whose winter graves lie beyond its old boundary.

### The tale

A claimant army returned carrying keys and titles from before occupation. The town opened after negotiation. Its stewards included collaborators who had enriched themselves, mixed families born during occupation, and workers who had kept wells, hospitals and grain mills alive for everyone.

The returners erased the distinctions. They executed stewards in the square, killed people who tried to hide them and expelled families into winter. A water-court book recording maintenance and refuge rights was burned. Soldiers issued backdated titles to their supporters and used occupation parchment because no other material remained.

The hospital stopped receiving water after the purge. A returning mason discovered that the conduits had been repaired years earlier by two of the executed households. He broke into the sealed channel and restored it with stones pulled from their confiscated homes. Each stone still bore a family mark on the hidden side.

Later victory histories called the winter deaths enemy propaganda. A collapsed ward exposed a grave of children wrapped in both occupation cloth and returner blankets. The hospital wall then became the town's second account: it literally stood through work done by people the first account had removed.

Returner descendants repaired the exposed grave beside expelled families. They quarrelled over prayers and did not reconcile their title claims, but none could again say the winter dead were invented by an enemy who had buried them in both peoples' cloth. The shared work lasted one winter and outlived no political quarrel.

### Why people still tell it

It does not condemn return or excuse collaboration. It shows how restoration becomes another sack when victors prefer categories to the lived differences among the people already there.

### How tellers differ

Claimant families say the executed men poisoned wells. Hospital descendants preserve patients treated by them. Some expelled households later fought the occupation elsewhere, complicating every clean lineage.

### What remains

The hospital wall contains stones with two household marks, one scraped from the public face and preserved toward the water channel.

### What remains open

The town, army and final title settlement remain open. The episode neither invalidates the wider Reconquest nor creates hereditary guilt.

## G-68 — The Three Knocks under the Mountain

*Formerly: Compact of the Knockers; Marks of Necessary Release.*

*Where it is told:* in one High-Noon Aversian mining belt under Shield-House extraction.

### The tale

Miners learned that three knocks from the dark meant a load-bearing seam was moving. Sometimes a small courtier beneath the mine carried the warning. Sometimes stone and timber made the sound without any person behind it. In exchange, workers left one share of ore and never cut the pale rib crossing the deepest shaft.

Officials turned the custom into production law. They claimed every missed quota had angered “the Knockers” and sent prisoners and strikers into dangerous workings as payment. To suppress stoppages, an overseer arranged a false middle knock meaning the passage was safe. He also paid a court interpreter to confirm it.

The crews descended. Gas and collapse killed prisoners, guards and free miners. When surviving interpreters accused the overseer, he executed them as people who had broken the court bargain. The mine answered that night with three knocks from behind a wall nobody had dug—or settling beams repeated the pattern until fear did the rest.

Workers abandoned the mine after daubing the untouched rib with meal. When it reopened, they accepted the first and third warning but refused ever to answer the middle knock. The silence became theirs, not the official's and not the courtier's.

New managers tried to replace the taboo with bells. Miners kept both, knowing bells can fail and customs can be manipulated. Their caution is not romantic superstition; it is the memory of officials who once counterfeited certainty and called obedience safety. Stone still gets the final word.

### Why people still tell it

The horror lies in being underground and unable to know whether a warning belongs to rock, a strange neighbour or the employer who intends to kill you. Worker solidarity changes an exploitative sign into a refusal.

### How tellers differ

Court-facing families say the interpreter betrayed both peoples. Officials blame superstition for panic. Miners name executed interpreters before managers at every reopening.

### What remains

Miners never answer the middle of three knocks. They leave the pale rib untouched and mark it with meal rather than blood.

### What remains open

No reusable Knocker species or hidden mine nation is created. Natural response, court person and Menhir pressure remain distinct.

## G-70 — The Empty Grave and the Two False Shrines

*Formerly: Empty Grave Archive.*

*Where it is told:* along quarry roads from the Underground into Legalization, by a family claimed by two hostile cults.

### The tale

A prisoner was tortured for the names of fellow labour organisers. One account says he recanted; another says the words were put in his mouth after he lost consciousness. He was executed beside the quarry. Officials denied his family the body and threw it into an unmarked lime trench.

The family dug a careful grave anyway. They lined it with stones, laid out his work shirt and gathered there without claiming miracle or martyrdom. After persecution eased, one cult built a shrine saying the prisoner had died as its Saint. A rival cult said his final curse had made him a powerful Sinner and built another shrine near the quarry.

Both demanded the empty grave. Relic hunters broke bones in the lime trench. Shrine custodians killed a cousin who refused to sell the work shirt. The second cult burned the family's house and blamed the first. Copies of the prisoner's face appeared on images that resembled neither surviving description.

The family removed every carving from the grave and left only unmarked stones. Pilgrims still come, but the empty place now refuses to say whether the dead man belonged to either supernatural story.

Children from the family sometimes rearrange the stones into a workman's bed and scatter them before sunset. Shrine keepers object that the act profanes holy ground. The family answers that the grave was made for mourning before either shrine discovered a use for it. They scatter every claimed shape.

### Why people still tell it

At its centre is a family denied the ordinary act of burying someone they loved. Competing cosmic claims become another form of taking the body from them.

### How tellers differ

One shrine says the grave emptied through ascension. The other says a rival stole the corpse. Family versions begin with the body already gone and distrust both.

### What remains

A carefully lined empty grave receives uncarved stones. Any inscribed offering is removed by morning.

### What remains open

The prisoner's last words, final judgement and body remain unknown. No shrine, image, Mask, office or relic is authenticated.

# V. Far-Distant and Otherworld Histories

## G-50 — The Three Deaths of Every Great House

*Formerly: Three Testimonies.*

*Where it is told:* in a far-distant Orphaned mortuary commonwealth where great Houses rose from the care of bodies after a civilisational collapse.

### The tale

When cities failed, mortuary workers knew how to wash the dead, separate sickness from hunger and identify families by scars, teeth and clothing. They fed survivors from funeral stores and returned enough names that scattered households could find one another. People gave them land and authority because they had kept the dead from becoming refuse.

The worker families became great Houses. They beautified their founders, hid prices charged to the desperate and treated control of burial as proof of fitness to rule. At the funeral of a beloved matriarch, the House recited her first death: the public Life in which she saved a district.

A rival then performed the second death, naming military failures and political enemies. Before the grave closed, a debtor woman demanded the third. She described her husband dying outside the matriarch's sealed grain court while House dogs ate funeral meat inside. Guards tried to remove her. The matriarch's grandson instead gave her his mourning mask.

From then on, every great funeral required three tellers: kin, rival and person who bore the cost of the dead one's greatness. The custom did not make the Houses just. They learned to hire agreeable debtors and stage managed hostility. Children therefore turned the rite into a satirical street play, changing masks whenever an adult version became too clean.

The Houses repeatedly banned the children's version. Every ban supplied a new villain and ensured the fourth ending spread into another market.

### Why people still tell it

The story honours lifesaving institutions while showing how gratitude hardens into oligarchy. A person's significance expands rather than collapses when the life is allowed to include people harmed by it.

### How tellers differ

House versions say the grandson invented the rite. Debtor songs say the crowd forced him to yield. Rural versions give the third mask to a child, animal or abandoned place when no victim survived.

### What remains

Three-mask funeral plays are performed by kin, rival and cost-bearer. Street children often steal all three masks and improvise a fourth ending.

### What remains open

The polity and Houses remain beyond known-world contact. The performances judge social memory, not a soul's final state.

## G-51 — The Cypress That Would Not Rot

*Formerly: Still Cypress Vaults.*

*Where it is told:* in the same far-distant ancestor polity, around a local conjunction with an existing Menhir pressure.

### The tale

Embalmers found that cypress laid in one valley did not decay. Bodies kept colour; flower oils remained fragrant; carved faces seemed only asleep. Great Houses bought the fields, planted more trees and promised their honoured dead would remain beautiful for generations.

Vaults spread across farmland. Poor graves were moved to wet margins so important bodies could lie near the still cypress. When crops failed, guards protected the return fields while living children ate bark. Starving families entered at night to dig roots and grain stored for funerals. House soldiers killed them among the unrotting dead.

A vault keeper hid surviving children inside a queen's chamber. For three days they ate sugared offerings from her table. On the fourth, the queen's perfect face blackened and collapsed. Rot moved from body to body wherever funeral abundance had been defended against the hungry. It spared some ordinary graves and ruined some without pattern.

The Houses called the decay sabotage. Farmers called it judgement. The vault keeper carried two cypress boards into the public square: one still fragrant, one black and soft. She asked which should roof a living child's house. No House could answer without revealing what it valued.

Farmers pulled down one empty vault and used its timber for winter shelters. The preserved boards made excellent roofs. That usefulness offended embalmers more than destruction would have, because it returned funeral wealth to weather, smoke and the needs of ordinary bodies. Children slept beneath them.

### Why people still tell it

The tale sets beauty and remembrance against bread without pretending preservation itself is evil. Its dread builds toward the moment the miracle ceases, and its heart is children hiding among richly fed corpses.

### How tellers differ

Priests say the conjunction withdrew from greed. Natural philosophers cite soil and oil. Menhir-facing songs say the land never promised permanent preservation, only a season that rulers refused to release.

### What remains

Funeral roofs use paired cypress boards. One is fragrant; the other is allowed to weather and is replaced only after a hungry household has been fed.

### What remains open

No new Menhir is created, and no wood preserves a human soul. The local pressure and exact valley remain unresolved pending distant geography.

## G-52 — The Ancestors Who Shared a Spelling Error

*Formerly: Court Beyond the Cypress.*

*Where it is told:* in a far-distant Egg White archive polity whose families purchase counsel in the voices of their dead.

### The tale

Great Houses deposited letters, gestures, memories and recorded judgements so trained keepers could model an ancestor's voice after death. At first the voices helped grieving families remember recipes, boundary stones and unfinished apologies. The keepers charged for longer conversations and gave the most convincing speakers ceremonial seats.

Soon the speakers advised marriages, condemned heirs and transferred property. A widow lost her house because the voice of her dead husband claimed he had always preferred his cousin. A soldier was ordered back to war by a “grandmother” who spoke in phrases recorded after the real woman died.

A child listening to several ancestral speakers noticed that each misspelled the same river name. The error came from a keeper's damaged index, not from any of the dead. She asked her supposed grandfather which hand she had burned in his kitchen. He answered from the archive and chose the wrong one.

The Houses did not abandon the speakers. Some were residues, some constructed patterns, some offices voiced by living keepers, some frauds, and a rare claimant may have been something stranger. Instead, families began asking the child's riddle before accepting counsel. The most powerful speakers declared the question impious.

The child's mother still paid to hear her own mother's voice. She knew the speaker failed the riddle and returned anyway, not for judgement but to listen to an old recipe. Doubt changed the relationship without instantly curing grief or desire. She no longer obeyed it.

### Why people still tell it

Its mystery grows from grief: people want one more conversation badly enough to let an imitation rule them. A tiny spelling error changes majestic ancestry into a vulnerable human system.

### How tellers differ

Keeper versions say the child exposed only a faulty batch. Reformers say every voice is false. Bereaved families preserve encounters no index can explain. The tale refuses to classify all speakers as one kind.

### What remains

Children ask an ancestor a household riddle whose answer was never deposited. The riddle changes with every family and is never written down.

### What remains open

Each speaker must be judged on its own nature. The Court is not a collective human afterlife, and no model inherits the original person's soul.

## G-53 — The Fire of the Unclosed Line

*Formerly: Unclosed Line.*

*Where it is told:* in one far-distant city and its dependent wards, among refugees whose skin bears white-ash signs.

### The tale

A mortuary school claimed it could keep valuable citizens available after death. Some subjects were unlawfully bound human souls. Others were copied habits wearing reconstructed bodies. Families were rarely told which had been returned.

Victims inside the school found ways to burn messages into the skin of attendants. When the truth reached the wards, rebels stormed the laboratories. They broke vessels and freed several bound persons. Then fury became purge. Surrendering technicians were killed. Dependants and children wearing school colours were dragged from houses. A nursery burned after someone shouted that copied infants were hidden inside.

The rebel council publicly destroyed the techniques. In secret, commanders stole them to question dead enemies and preserve their own leaders. A survivor recognised the same embalming marks beneath a liberator's new white-ash tattoo and exposed the theft. The city turned on itself again.

Refugees escaped carrying fragments of knowledge, guilt and people whose condition nobody could confidently name. They developed tattoos that concealed school marks from hunters. A mark might hide a freed soulbound person, a constructed pattern, an innocent child or a murderer. No colour settled the question.

One refugee camp split when a copied mother tried to claim a living child. The copy remembered birth perfectly; the child remembered years of fear after the original died. They separated without deciding whether resemblance created kinship, showing why no uprising slogan could replace patient judgement. Both survived the separation and carried incompatible grief away.

### Why people still tell it

The story begins as rescue from a genuine horror and becomes a warning that victimhood does not sanctify collective killing. Its most painful image is a technique condemned in public and coveted in private by the victors.

### How tellers differ

Rebel Lives deny the nursery deaths. School loyalists call every return authentic. Refugee families insist every claimant must be judged individually even when uncertainty is frightening.

### What remains

White-ash tattoos obscure old embalming signs. During mourning, wearers reveal only as much of the mark as they freely choose.

### What remains open

The purge was limited to one city and wards. It was not the Pale Hand, vampire origin or a civilisation-wide war; each person's identity and manner of survival remain separate.

## G-54 — The Four Seals for the Uncertain Dead

*Formerly: Keepers of the Last Door.*

*Where it is told:* in far-distant grave country where a human order investigates things found speaking from tombs.

### The tale

After the Unclosed Fire, graves sometimes opened onto living captives, copied personalities, predatory offices or bodies moved by something that had never been the buried person. Five travelling keepers developed slow tests. They listened for childhood memory, watched bodily need, examined the grave, asked what the speaker could refuse and waited for evidence from outside the tomb.

Their patience freed people wrongly sealed and exposed dangerous imitations. Rulers granted them soldiers and demanded the same certainty during foreign campaigns. Waiting became inconvenient. Cases showing four of five signs were destroyed before dawn. One keeper killed a terrified human survivor because a missing witness had not yet reached the camp.

When the witness arrived, the order's custodians turned against their own commanders. Several died opening cells. They broke one clay seal from every kit and left an empty hollow where certainty had been. The order survived but lost the right to make death immediate wherever doubt remained.

Its members still enter graves where something answers from within. They are neither saints nor heavenly officers. Their tools work imperfectly, and a compassionate mistake can release danger just as a fearful mistake can murder a person.

The order tells of one keeper who opened a tomb and found only an ordinary thief pretending to be dead. She laughed, fed him and was later killed by a true grave predator. The comic episode survives because caution without humanity had already made the order monstrous once.

### Why people still tell it

The tale combines dangerous field mystery with the terror of killing the person one intended to save. Doubt becomes a hard-won restraint rather than indecision dressed as wisdom.

### How tellers differ

Order Lives claim the fifth seal was voluntarily surrendered. Victim families say custodians smashed it after mutiny. Some rulers still issue complete five-seal kits outside the order.

### What remains

Investigated graves bear four broken clay birds and one empty hollow. A complete fifth bird is treated as a threat, not proof.

### What remains open

No test is infallible. The order's names, present size and encountered beings remain open, and every claimant must be judged separately.

## G-55 — The Armour Worn by Strangers

*Formerly: Mantle Houses.*

*Where it is told:* among far-distant mobile households whose defenders inherit altered armour and unfinished duties.

### The tale

A defender died holding a pass long enough for her household to escape. Her armour was too small for the next bearer, a broad-shouldered cook. The family cut its sides, added plates and asked him to finish the road she had begun.

He did. Victories brought land and honour, and custodians began calling him by the dead defender's name. They ordered him to marry her widower, repeat her gestures and avenge enemies he had never met. When he refused, they said the armour had rejected him. The next bearer accepted every demand and repeated an atrocity the first defender had once committed against a surrendered village.

Generations of wearers followed: some brave, some cruel, some children forced into plates, some strangers who wore the armour only for one journey. Custodians taught that the office made them one returning hero. Bearers knew better because every alteration pinched a different scar.

At last two claimants split the armour between them. One defended the household; the other went to compensate the village. Neither possessed a complete suit, but both fulfilled part of the inherited duty. The households began dancing the armour in several bodies instead of seating one person on the dead hero's chair.

The cook's descendants always wear the widened side plates. They joke that heroic destiny first arrived because the original suit pinched at supper. The laughter protects a bodily truth against custodians who prefer one ageless silhouette. Armour must fit the living.

### Why people still tell it

It celebrates inherited responsibility while defending the living bearer's identity. A person may finish another's work without becoming that person, inheriting guilt or surrendering marriage and desire to a costume.

### How tellers differ

Custodian epics insist one spirit continued through every wearer. Bearer songs list changes in height, voice and appetite. Village versions say restitution, not battle, was the only duty worth inheriting.

### What remains

Visibly resized armour is danced by several living claimants. No performer wears the whole suit or the dead defender's face.

### What remains open

No soul, saintly commission or metaphysical mantle transfers through the armour. The distant households and present bearers remain outside known-world contact.

# VI. Reclaimed from the Reserve

## G-71 — The Five Staffs and the Empty Granary

*Where it is told:* in post-Prophet Glass-camp traditions, later municipal emergency rites and northern caravan satire.

### The tale

After the Prophet died, the people still had captains, judges, witnesses, provisioners and household callers. Later songs gave each office one staff and pretended there had always been exactly five. In truth, offices changed hands and number while nobody could inherit the commission that had held them together.

Drought emptied the rear camps. A southern expedition promised new fields if it left before the last rains. The captain took grain before the provisioners could count the sick, children and old people who would remain. He believed delay would condemn everyone. His allies turned emergency shares into land allotments for the soldiers who marched.

Two rear camps starved. Families boiled harness and buried children without shrouds. The judges condemned the seizure after the grain had been eaten. They could not restore it, and the expedition council refused to remove a commander whose success seemed to prove him right.

At the last common meeting, five different staffs were thrown onto an empty granary floor. Southern households lifted the military staff and departed. Northern households took the provision staff and refused another levy. Nobody touched the Prophet's old walking place. The temporary expedition became a lasting separation because people had died under an authority that would not answer after necessity passed.

The expedition later found the grain country it promised. That success sharpened rather than settled the accusation. Descendants of the dead could see that their food had purchased a real future and still ask why the buyers alone inherited it.

### Why people still tell it

It makes a succession crisis hurt through hunger rather than titles. The commander may have saved future settlers, but the tale asks whether achievement can answer children who died because their food became somebody else's future.

### How tellers differ

Atherian versions make the march unavoidable. Jirahar versions make the rear camps the faithful remnant. Ahdari jokes add a sixth staff belonging to the cook whom every office ignored.

### What remains

Emergency staffs are laid on an empty granary floor before stored food may become military property. No tradition gives them identical names.

### What remains open

Five is mnemonic compression, not a fixed ancient constitution. No staff inherits the Prophet's commission or creates five tribes.

## G-72 — The Hostage Children of Oak and Glass

*Where it is told:* on one proto-Atherian–Folk border where fort households and an old great clan traded children to hold a truce.

### The tale

Repeated raids left the southern fort without winter meat and the Folk clan without safe access to its river fields. Their leaders exchanged children and adolescents as sureties. The children learned one another's songs, insults, kinship rules and weak gates. Some were treated as guests; others as servants who could be punished for battles fought far away.

Trade grew around them. A fostered youth returned to the fort and discovered officers preparing to seize the clan's stored cattle during a feast. He warned his foster-sister in the woods. The fort commander arrested him, staged a brief trial and executed him as a traitor despite the truce.

The clan retaliated without distinguishing responsibility. Warriors killed settlers in the fields and captives in their hall. Surviving fosterlings ran between both sides, opening one oak palisade and one glass-bright fort door so children could escape. They prevented total destruction and were thereafter suspected by everyone: too southern in the woods, too Folk behind the wall.

Their descendants required that no hostage be punished unless witnesses from both kin-groups stood present. The custom did not undo the murder or massacre. It made the people who had belonged uneasily to both sides into guardians against either side telling the whole story alone.

Some former hostages refused the duty and moved away. Others married one another and were accused of founding a secret third nation. They had founded households, which was politically troublesome enough without making them a hidden people.

### Why people still tell it

The developing relationships among the foster children pull the tale forward. What matters is not diplomacy in the abstract, but young people used as security who acquire real loyalties their rulers cannot control.

### How tellers differ

Fort songs say the warning endangered civilians. Folk songs say it exposed planned theft. Mixed families remember one fosterling marrying across the border; purists on both sides omit that ending.

### What remains

Oak and green glass beads are tied together at hostage exchanges and border fosterings. Breaking either bead ends the rite.

### What remains open

The fort, clan and children's later lives remain open. This was one border truce, not the source of all Folk–Atherian mixture.

## G-73 — The Oar-King without a Country

*Where it is told:* in the heroic-maritime Atherian world, especially at one harbour that both crowns and dockworkers claim he founded.

### The tale

An enslaved rower survived when a payless fleet's officers abandoned a damaged ship in foreign water. He cut the remaining chains, rationed bilge-soaked grain and brought the crew through a storm by following birds no navigator trusted. Refugees, criminals, pilots and defeated soldiers joined him from port to port.

He began as the man who shared every bench. Victories made him captain; need made him judge; conquest made him king. His fleet captured an inhabited harbour whose rulers had refused them winter refuge. Soldiers killed defenders, sold captives and took houses. The Oar-King protected his followers from revenge but did not protect local families from his followers.

His oldest companion demanded they leave after winter as promised. The king arranged a boat race, rammed the companion's vessel beyond rescue and called the death an accident. He then carried the dead man's oar before his new crown, claiming friendship had blessed the city.

The harbour prospered. Captive pilots made it a centre of navigation; older inhabitants survived beneath new names; the rowers' children became citizens. Dockside singers continued naming those sold during the founding generation. Each year the magistrate carries an oar in public while an unofficial lament follows one street behind.

Once, a magistrate turned and tried to silence the lament. The bearers lowered the ceremonial oar and refused to continue. Since then the procession can advance only while the song remains audible, binding civic pride to the victims it cannot repay.

### Why people still tell it

It gives the irresistible ascent from slave to king without pretending humble suffering prevents later abuse. The audience can love the rower who saves a crew and condemn the ruler who murders the friend able to remember him before the crown.

### How tellers differ

Civic epics make the harbour empty or tyrannical before conquest. Older-family songs name markets, wells and graves already present. Royal lists turn the companion's murder into honourable single combat.

### What remains

A ceremonial oar precedes one magistrate. Dockworkers trail it with a lament whose verses name captives rather than kings.

### What remains open

The ruler, harbour and authentic oar remain provisional. He was not Aurelius Thalion, founder of all sea-kings or leader of another signature company.

## G-74 — The Raid That Returned One Tool

*Where it is told:* in a pre-imperial Arkenan–Saronikan island corridor and in court-side families descended from captured raiders.

### The tale

Blockade and failed harvest trapped an island league behind reefs that shifted their safe approach with current and season. A nearby court stronghold possessed a sounding-weight that could find one path through. Its rulers offered passage for a toll and return of the tool. The sea-king demanded permanent possession.

When refused, he sent coerced pilots and hungry oarsmen through a brief opening. Raiders killed defenders inside the populated stronghold and stole the weight. The court closed every route except the one the stolen tool revealed. One ship escaped. Others struck reefs that should not have been there, drowned, or reached the far side with crews altered by the crossing. Prisoners remained among the court households and raised children there.

The returning ship carried grain to the threatened harbour. People hailed the theft as civilization's victory. The sea-king displayed the weight but never again entered the route himself. Each safe voyage thereafter depended on a stronghold deprived of the instrument that had protected its own people.

Old songs steadily increased the number of survivors. Court-side burial customs did the opposite: they preserved human names the songs declared drowned. When a later captain tried to copy the weight's marks, the false tool confidently led three boats onto stone.

The harbour blamed the court for that wreck. Pilot families blamed the captain's desire to own a shortcut without its relationship or price. The argument changed the weight from a trophy into an object nobody could safely use alone.

### Why people still tell it

It offers a dangerous otherworld raid, a real treasure and a diminished return while keeping the moral cost visible. Saving a harbour does not make killing defenders and stealing their safety righteous.

### How tellers differ

Island epics call the stronghold empty and the tool withheld from starving humans. Court descendants remember negotiations and named defenders. Pilot families blame the king; royal songs blame treacherous currents.

### What remains

A disputed sounding-weight answers one current sequence near one reef. Its wear and custody marks suggest use before the raid, but it proves only its own response.

### What remains open

The tool, sea-king, stronghold and restitution remain unsettled. It grants no general sea mastery and commands neither Hecatara nor Thalassor.

## G-75 — The Empty Oar of the Winter Tithe

*Formerly: The Elfland Tithe.*

*Where it is told:* on one western Kedravan fishing coast from the late Underground into early Legalization.

### The tale

A shore-threshold court once bargained with an offshore winter-road court. In return for rowers and watchers, the offshore court kept one crossing open when ordinary water turned inward. At first the shore rulers sent members of their own household.

They soon found substitutes. Fosterlings, fishers and provincial prisoners disappeared into embodied service. The road's tokens counted completed watches but could not tell consent from coercion. Underground officials used the route to remove believers, debtors and inconvenient witnesses without corpse or public execution.

A fisher entered the winter road to recover her taken brother. She was told that freeing him would leave an empty oar and somebody else would be seized unless the obligation itself was broken. She transformed herself through cold, labour and disguise, recognised her brother despite changed skin and brought him home. On the next tide, the court took her daughter.

The fisher returned to end the compact rather than attempt another exchange. Some say she drowned destroying the route mark; others say the road remains because shore rulers stopped her. Later church polemic made every missing believer a martyr and every nonbeliever a willing pagan servant. Families kept the harsher refrain: one rescue is not freedom when the empty place still demands a body.

Her brother spent the rest of his life rowing ordinary rescue boats. He never claimed the sacrifice had been worthwhile. Each saved stranger deepened both his gratitude and the knowledge that his sister or niece had paid for the chance.

### Why people still tell it

The adventure is a rescue whose apparent victory changes meaning at the moment another child disappears. Love for one captive becomes opposition to the system that makes captives interchangeable.

### How tellers differ

Court ballads praise the compact's saved ships. Church Lives claim the fisher acted under a Saint's guidance. Coast families disagree whether her daughter ever returned and refuse to let either claim erase the forced service.

### What remains

Winter boats carry one deliberately empty rowing place. Nobody may be named as its substitute, and an empty oar is laid across it.

### What remains open

The courts, family and road's present state remain open. The creditor is not Hell, the servants keep complete human souls, and rescue summons no Saint.

# How to Use the Garden

This file is the story-facing nursery. Read a root first as a tale: what happens, what changes, what makes the next beat worth hearing and why the loss or gain matters. Use the separate [[Historical Seed Garden - Mythic Voice, Variety, and Localisation Review|control ledger]] for source hard edges, metaphysical distinctions, sacred limits, collision rulings and canon readiness.

The seventy legacy roots keep permanent IDs `G-01` through `G-70`. Five reclaimed roots begin at `G-71`. Titles may change again; the block IDs beneath each root are the stable link targets.

## Canon harvest gate

Before any root enters chronology or a culture's canon, ask:

1. Does the tale still work if every supernatural interpretation is false?
2. Is its broad region culturally prepared to carry it?
3. Does each beat cause, alter or reveal the next?
4. Is the source's guilt, taboo or irreversible cost still present?
5. Does the vivid remainder matter because of the tale rather than merely proving it?
6. Is the root distinct from its nearest neighbour in action, emotional centre and consequence?
7. Are human souls separate from offices, Masks, Patrons, residues, copies and legendary personas?
8. Are Saints and angels bounded by commission rather than inherited or summoned?
9. Does the root preserve protected Weirds and the Forward Rule?
10. Is the desired promotion genuinely small enough for the receiving canon note?
