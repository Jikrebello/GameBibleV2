# Historical Spine of the Known World

The known world's history extends from Giant dominion and the Great Unbinding through the Glass migration, the formation of later peoples, Atherian rule and the successor states of 1360 AR. This survey follows the principal changes that connect those ages: what broke, why it broke, and what each age left the next to carry. Wonders and horrors belong to that chain as much as harvests and wars. A voice in a tomb, a lamp that would not kindle and a river that will not freeze have each changed who ruled, as surely as a drought or a drowned heir.

## 1. Age of Chains

[[Age of Chains]] is the age of Giant dominion. The Five Imperial Horizons and dozens of smaller crowns, estates, leagues and subject cities enslave humanity while leaving behind ruins, forbidden sciences, sacrifice systems, stolen engineering and the memory of power impersonating divinity.

The age ended in hunger. As Giant blood thinned, the old works demanded more crews, captives, grain and at last children, and revolt began where the takers came. Dike crews were marched off to tomb-works while the river walls went unmended; granaries were emptied into vaults; in the northern dry belt the sky roads of Vathra failed and the valleys beneath them stretched. Survivors compressed a century of being eaten into a few great disasters, a Naga lord who drank the Serpent's Spine into his coils and a Blood Sun that burst, and their grandchildren remembered the dikes nobody mended.

**Inherited wound:** later empires are haunted by the fear that monument, law and sacred language may become Giant tyranny in human form, and later peoples fear anything kept past its time.

## 2. Age of Exodus

[[Age of Exodus]] begins when the Great Prophet's movement becomes the centre of the [[The Great Unbinding|Great Unbinding]]. The plagues strike the works through which bodies were taken, and the principal liberated remnant goes into the [[Great Glass Desert]]. Other human communities survive outside that host. During [[The Glass Sojourn]], approximately a century of provision, law, labour, worship, language, marriage and burial forms the mixed Ark host into one people.

When the host is sent south the provision stops at the edge of the Glass, company by company. The springs that had run in its channels come late, then low, then not at all, and the garden beds beside them yellow. The Prophet dies at the outward threshold as the last direct witness to the Giant world. Every people tells his death differently. Jirahar road-singers say he walked back to the rear camps where the bread had already stopped; Covenant jurists, that he opened a well he had not paid for and was held to his own law; Atherian temple drama, that he was taken up among the stars; Folk satirists, that his captains killed him for forbidding them other people's fields. All four tellings keep one line: *no one is owned.* The later southern sanctuary fails in a quarrel over the seed the drought-struck rear camps were eating.

**Inherited wound:** a real unity formed under deliverance cannot survive land, succession and possession. Ark descendants inherit its divided witness; Orphaned peoples inherit the fallen world it escaped.

## 3. Age of Scattering

[[Age of Scattering]] contains three overlapping processes. First, southern war-settlement leagues separate gradually from a continuing Near-Glass camp continuum and become proto-Atherian through settlement, conquest and mixture. Generations later, water, distance, marriage, trade, raiding and reciprocal Tarkhan contact divide the Near-Glass continuum into proto-Jirahar and proto-Ahl-e-Ahd formations. Meanwhile Folk, Ättar, Tarkhan and Nsizwa develop through independent Orphaned reassemblies among communities that survived outside the Ark. Sestaran harbour populations and Vesari emerge later as Braided peoples; later Eclessians braid Atherian provincial, Folk, Markish, Vesari and other regional layers.

Proto-Atherian expansion enters inhabited country. It produces forts, roads and connected recovery networks through local knowledge and labour, but also massacre, slavery, hostage-taking, displacement and land seizure. Settlers who had never learned the farming year took farmers with the fields, and many southern companies turned to stars that still answered: *the Lord who stopped feeding us; the stars that still answer.* Folk clans destroy or absorb some forts; harbour communities bargain, resist or braid with others. No victory inherits the Prophet's commission or proves divine title.

In the northern dry belt, Tarkhan communities develop western camel-and-well circuits and eastern hunting and herding circuits long before Atheria falls. At Narash, in the first winters after Vathra's fall, the crown-mother Meya had sealed the Hundred Wells by a bargain at a dead anchor field; every crown-mother since has walked their round at accession, and Narashi rule still runs through mothers. Covenant seizure of the Nine Wells in 558 AR, in the worst year of the dry-country drought that outlasted the Thin Floods, creates a frontier wound; the Atherian collapse later supplies the horses of the First Catch without creating Tarkhan civilisation from nothing.

**Inherited wound:** no Ark-derived people keeps the whole Exodus intact, no Orphaned people escapes the wreckage of Giant rule, and later Braided peoples inherit several memories that cannot be made seamless.

## 4. Age of Hearths and First Kings

[[Age of Hearths and First Kings]] is the age of villages, hill forts, river towns, first kings, ancestor houses, Menhir pacts, oasis law and local priesthoods.

In the southern ranges, established Ättar halls face the [[War of the Empty Halls]] around 1600 BR. Their firstborn covenant with Mother Moon creates the four pact-kindreds long after the Exodus, while related Folk societies retain different clan and court traditions. On the Tidewood–Gale-Crown watershed, halls that kept their seed, stock and dead sealed past the thaw were broken open by a High Hunt riding the seasonal roads, and the release cuts made in the Withheld Season became southern custom.

Late in the age the long bronze roads of the north broke. Failed floods, drought, seizure and unpaid soldiers did most of the damage, but caravans also vanished on the old processional roads, where Ishkatar's waking servants collected salvage metal still marked with their dead owners' titles. Trunk roads failed first. Iron, which road-traders said no dead owner ever claimed, became the northern road metal.

**Inherited wound:** kingship protects people from chaos but also reintroduces domination after liberation.

## 5. Age of City-States

[[Age of City-States]] creates the first urban worlds: Atherian civic rivals, Sestaran ports, Jirahar tower towns, Ahdari and related proto-Ahl-e-Ahd oasis polities, roads, archives, walls, war engines and early elemental scripting.

Around 1160 BR the harbour council of [[The Refusal at Kelarion|Kelarion]] kept its chain closed against a storm-driven fleet of refugees and pilgrims. The harbour drowned soon afterwards, sailors still speak of the King beneath the Breakwater, and the Roll of Refused Hulls kept by its pilots became the oldest refuge custom of the sea. Late in the eleventh century BR a besieged inner-gulf city enthroned an old protector behind four doors: a hill gate, a debt door, a victory road and a black pool. It won the war, then each door asked its own price, and the city lost its fields and most of its people.

In the Sacred Basin the dead were landlords. After two failed floods the last Yasharet king could not pay what his crown had pledged to a dead merchant's house; her factor, Aramen of Sakhret, fed the unpaid garrisons from her warehouses, took Qemaret around 1076 BR and had the king walled into her vault as returned cargo. He married the walled-up king's sister, and his heirs spent two centuries paying the dead with an empire the Yasharet had begun on borrowed grain. Hesren III then dug the seal of a buried Giant estate out of the vaults at Meheret and set its gilded procession, the Single Radiance, on the tombs to take back their old metal, and he gave the cleared fields to his soldiers. The estate wasted the son on whose neck he had hung the seal. That son took the embalmers' rite and was sealed into the estate-house with the metal and the tallies of every cleared field, so that stewards collected rent in a dead prince's name, from his father first. The boat households that starved that rent crowned Zaheret on a grain barge at Tenaret in 814.

At Qiryath, guardians who carried the festival offering beside the altar's cool stones came to rule like kings: a wood-carrier who caught a fevered guardian's basket on the sanctuary stair was cheered into the office. Around 940 BR two parties fought for the basket on the stair, and it reached the altar in the hands of a dying lamp-filler; his widow asked the custodians aloud whom the stones had answered. They answered the offering, and from then on its bearer was chosen by lot.

The eastern [[First Qiryath and the Arqeshite Exile|Arqeshite River Empire]] destroys the First Qiryath in 742 BR. Its king had demanded that his image stand beside the altar, because an Arqeshite image had stood on the sanctuary stair once before, on a morning when soldiers had killed two people at a custodian's door for his witness-staff, and the offering had burned without smoke. In the siege's first winter the council declared the city's foreign residents unhoused and let them starve within the walls, and the altar's sign, an offering that burns without smoke and leaves the stones cool, was withheld; the exiles judged their disputes on witnesses alone. House Ubar falls in 690. A succession war had drained it, its last king cut the rations of the capital's dredging gangs and deportee quarters to feed his soldiers, and the gangs opened the gates to the armies of [[Eryndor III and the Ortheian Eastern Conquest|Eryndor III]] and Arezan Tiravan. Both armies sacked the city and set the gangs back to work under guard. Arezan's restoration decree permits the Second Qiryath to arise under Vasharan supremacy; Vashar pays for the first courses of the new sanctuary, and the city finishes it. The offering made there in the Vasharan king's name smoked, and the sign returned only around 654, under an offering carried by the mixed households of the rebuilt city. Conquest, deportation and contested return entered Jirahar identity centuries before Atheria unified.

**Inherited wound:** cities preserve memory, but they also create debt, class, legal violence and appetite for empire.

## 6. Age of Stars and Atherian Synthesis

[[Age of Stars]] is the late pre-imperial Atherian synthesis. Star cults, civic law, archives, roads, astronomy and military discipline turn rival cities into a civilisation that believes it is destined to organise the world.

Ortheia briefly subjects much of the city world to royal command. Its king Valeron II is killed by his own infantry in 706 at a pay table that came up short, and his son Eryndor III leaves that army at home, where it later sacks Marenthe, the city whose withheld silver had emptied the table. Eryndor crosses the eastern sea with newer men, feeds them in Qahur on grain lent by a dead financier's house against the right to embalm him, and helps destroy Arqesh. The gilded images of the river kings, which had learned his voice, take a dead companion's instead and mock him out of his own hall. In the farther east a speaking wood whose price he refused closes the road behind him, and he cuts his way out with a wound that will not heal. He dies in 684 aboard ship below Arqesh, waiting for a wind to take him home, and a voice in his tomb at Delkos, claiming that he still reigns, breaks the conquests among rival Successors. Caleran recovers the Celestial Compact from the soldiers' captain who holds Ortheia and becomes an overseas republic during the Gateway Wars. Vashar, Delkos, Qahur and the eastern ports develop through independent successor histories. Vashar's attempt on the Atherian coast ends at Ilyessa in 493: the king had already lost one fleet at anchor there, hired the pilots, called the sea his and anchored again, the bread in his anchored fleet rotted in sound hulls, and his own subject crews rowed for an Atherian refuge fire. He died of the flux in his camp in the winter of 492–491, and his army went home by nations. The League of the Eastern Oar that took the crews in was raising its chains against its own members within a generation.

Caleran rules the Compact through its calendar, its rolls and the money for its fleet. Its wars with Qarzeth are fought over a god's bread. The Gateway's Provider feeds its city from its subject towns' stores, and an invader his own council had disowned feeds Caleran's wavering allies on the same bread until their sealed jars come up short. Caleran answers with a sacrifice of its own, killing on its festival ground the Gateway children handed to it as a pledge of peace, and with an office its holder cannot lay down, the Warden of the Red Field. The invader is killed in 601 at the store doors of a town he had fed for eleven years. Qarzeth's assembly at last surrenders the books in which the children's names are entered, and the god's granaries stay shut until the hungry city takes the lists up again. Qarzeth falls in 520, its spared towns salt the sanctuary with their own hands and swear an oath over a shared meal that outlives both republics.

**Inherited wound:** Atheria turns older fragments into law and beauty, but also converts spiritual loss into civic grandeur, and its case against Qarzeth's altars was always made from its own festival ground.

## 7. Atherian Unity and the Star Pantheon

The Atherian city world and republic create roads, cities, law, archives and a civic identity capable of admitting outsiders on unequal terms. Provincial wealth and unequal service strengthen personal military followings. In the republic's last century its own sky answered people other than its senate: in 488 BR the Sun at Sarven showed Eshren Damar's veterans the old lines of the public land, and the augurs answered by claiming the sole right to seek or record a sign. In 466 a harbour that closed its chain on Caleran's order shut out its own grain ships until its wardens cut it. [[Teren Casvilar]] accepts the Red Field in 383 against a Folk chief who walks into a river ford and is never found. Unable to close it or be recalled, he has first his banker and then the senate counted as his contestants, and gains supremacy through civil war. In 370 BR seven men enter their names as his challengers, one of them as his vanished enemy's son, and kill him on the muster ground before the contest opens. The ground is fouled, and the priests rule that nobody can close it while any of the seven lives. For twenty years no commander dares disband an army. The one great battle, at Darsil in 369, is won without one of the seven on the field, and the two companies paid off after it carry the war home to their own valley. Four men govern from the grain store above the muster ground, because armies that cannot go home must still eat; they write their enemies into the register of challengers and have them killed there, until an old speaker written in by his own partners walks down with a practice sword and the soldiers will not finish him, and the war spreads. [[The Wars of the Atherian Succession]] become a hunt for the seven through every refuge of the inner sea, from the mobile sea-city of Marelith, burned with one of them aboard, to the spared towns' table at Sarqen, where the last of them sits under the towns' oath for twelve years. The queen's son kills him at that table, and the oath that crowned her puts her house out and seats an abandoned rower in her place. She lives because the same oath forbids anyone to take a stranger from the table, and [[Aurelius Thalion]], who never commanded in a battle and sent painted boards of his face to war in his place, has to eat there, three seats from her and beside a son the towns have kept safe for him, and swear the towns' oath before he establishes imperial supremacy around 350 BR.

[[Historical Atlas of the Atherian Age of Glory]] follows the empire to 0 AR through the three ways an emperor was made: painted on the board the armies swore to, seated at the spared towns' table at Sarqen, and stood under an arch. Cities and professional armies allow the empire to outlive individual houses. After an untested hall kills the founder's heir, every public work must be loaded before the Red Star until its weakest stone cracks, and the official who opens it stands beneath it. In 247 BR the accession portrait at Caleran, once the painted board Thalion's soldiers swore to in his absence, answers in the Emperor's voice, and for two winters a magistrate governs in its name while clerks in its sight count grain that is not there. The warden-general who covers it refuses to be painted in its place, and its veiling sends the imperial cult's appetite into provincial copies, before which eastern citizens and Raveth's debtors swear. In the fever year of 151 the carters of Sarqen seat the next emperor at their table, and in 61 an emperor who will not stand under drowns beneath his own untested banks. Under the late Halreths a Vasharan civil war lets Atheria take Qiryath by charter, so the world into which Isfir is born already holds the governor, augur and referral that his trial will use. The Star Pantheon presents that authority as cosmic civic order; later writers call the hunger in the imperial portraits the Emperor after the Emperor.

**Inherited wound:** everyone wants Atheria's legitimacy, roads, ruins and archives.

## 8. The Redeemer Event

Isfir the Redeemer is born in 33 BR in a Jirahar tower town, inside imperial history and under its charters. His ministry confronts both imperial theology and legalistic religious expectation. At Second Qiryath the High Augur urges the sentence, the governor passes it, and soldiers execute him outside the city. His death, resurrection, ascension and gift of the Flame reorder the spiritual meaning of history.

From the hour of his binding to the third morning, no augur college in the empire recorded an answer from the Stars. The colleges sealed those days. At Qiryath the sacrifices went on burning, but no authenticated sign of acceptance returned; the Jirahar call it the Silence.

**Inherited wound:** the question of who truly understands the Redeemer becomes a political and metaphysical fault line.

## 9. Conversion and Persecution

For nearly three centuries the Redeemer's congregations met as an underground church. When the Companion Tyros, once an astrologer-priest, made the three blank days a public question, junior augurs followed him with copies of their books. The Great Purge of c. 36–45 AR hunted those copies, burned Jareth in the Celestial Forum and made it a crime to publish a wonder the colleges had not entered, so believers learned to record their signs as weather and good sense. When grave-lamps that burned without oil fed mutinies in the unpaid eastern garrisons, Tervan Marcellan's edict of 245 made every household relight its grave-lamps from the Stars' fire and burn them green on the colleges' oil, and for three years night watches walked the burial grounds hunting yellow flames. Congregations relit from flames that had never burned green, above all Amaryah's, hidden in a neighbour's bread oven at Veridun Minor, and quarrelled for a generation over who might be given fire.

After Qiryath's fall the port councils made a supply officer emperor, and for a century the empire was run from the store-house at Caleran, longest by his daughter Ysane the Paymistress, while generals held the roads. The line ended in 189 at Sarqen, where runaways of a slave rising sat down at the spared towns' unranked table, no governor could be sworn for the grain province, and the emperor Marek Vessan swore the towns' oath among them and stayed as their guest. Provincial commands then issued orders in an absent emperor's name, the augur colleges learned to sell the opening of campaign seasons, and a frontier army whose season had been voided acclaimed Cassian. In the winter of 290 forty prisoners at Ironvale died in an unlit furnace rather than renounce the Redeemer, and when it was opened a flame stood on the dead. In the winter of 292 the White Flame stood on the frozen Aurin at the Stone Bridge of Velos while Cassian's vanguard was crossing the ice by night. The ice went out, no battle was fought, the court's army crossed the bridge to him, and the Aurin at the bridge has never frozen since. His settlement restored property through provincial courts and gave the four sees a way to hear disputes across provinces. The treasury's grants gave the emperor influence over which congregations could afford to build and serve publicly. Cassian himself was baptised in the unfrozen reach in 316, by the eldest son he had forced into orders. [[Atheria between Qiryath and Cassian - Emperors Wars and Revolts|Atheria between Qiryath and Cassian]] · [[Cassian and the Four Sees - The Settlement of 292 to 316 AR|Cassian and the Four Sees]]

The Stars had their last public day under Astrion. In 350 a loaded bronze standard cracked before a crowd at Caleran where the engineers could see the flaw, and the eastern army marched under that Answered Standard for a generation, though no second answer came. The next year, on a desert retreat, he was crushed beneath an untried timber bridge he had insisted on standing under while the wagons of wounded crossed. He lived. Caleran, told that he was dead, made Lucar Aster emperor and kept him, and for twenty years Astrion was carried under his standard as emperor of the east. In the south Lucar's chaplains took as idols the ford-tokens of the Folk guides who had always led the army across the river-courts' fords. The guideless forts watched allied villages burn in the hard winters. They crossed by the fords' older toll, a person delivered to the far bank who could not return, and paid it in children taken from the allied households of the fort districts; stewards sold some of the children. The fort-district households began to slip away across the fords on hidden tokens, towards their children. In 377 Lucar's army followed the last of them into the Kellovar ford and drowned; Lucar, carried dry on a stolen token, was three days a guest at the fire of the house it belonged to, and his own soldiers killed him when he rode back. Vestian, who read the drowning as judgement on a divided empire, meant to hold every sign in one hand. In 383 he dissolved the augur colleges, after the last High Augur's petition to the Red Star went unanswered before the crowd, and gave the bishops the power to say which signs were real; he beat the eastern command and in 385 made the faith compulsory. The lamps of his Profession failed to kindle. His court blamed hidden rites and the Jirahar, banned household worship and took the lamp out of the oath. Gateway congregations named the failure the Second Silence, and their sworn accounts burned with the hearing house at Raveth. Their champion, Bishop Qedrel of Qabir, who would certify no relic for the estates buying their way out of the land tax, was condemned at Halmenth in 411. Halmenth's own ferrymen rowed him out to a Jirahar Sea Fleet ship that night. The regency struck his cities off its roll and left them alone because they still paid their land tax, and his communion survives as the Velian Church.

The Redeemer's faith overtakes the Star Pantheon. Martyrs become saints. The empire baptises itself into a new order, but conversion also produces coercion, persecution, archive rewriting and violence against rejected or inconvenient communities.

**Inherited wound:** sanctity and institutional power become intertwined from the beginning, and the power to say which signs are real passes to whoever holds the registers.

## 10. Jirahar Destructions and Exile

After the Silence, militants in Qiryath taught that the sign would return when foreign rule ended. In the spring of 61 AR the emperor's daily offering lay unburnt on the altar for nine mornings before the pilgrims. The governor's augur entered it as a fraud, and the soldiers sent up the sanctuary stair to burn a bull in pitch killed the altar's custodians and two hundred others. For three years no army came against the city, whose Keeper went on offering for the emperor every morning. The legions went round it to Migdal Shahar, a deep tower on the Dawn Road, took its gatehouse, dug down for two years and dropped the gallery roofs on the refuge halls. The tower's Way-Gate led to an older tower across the Vasharan frontier, whose bench would take the households and no one who had borne arms. In the spring of 64 the keeper Yaltah bat Ozer opened it on those terms against her captain's refusal and sent the households through for nine days while the fighters held the stairs; she broke the gate's scroll-keys as the soldiers came, and the legions killed the fighters, the keepers and the old who would not leave their dead, and sold the living. Gate benches still cite Shahar when they weigh another tower's danger against its people, and none would open for Qiryath's. Second Qiryath fell in 67 AR, dried out by the breaking of its cisterns. Its Keeper had sent the sanctuary's vessels through the city's gate, which has not opened since; the legate's augur burned the emperor's offering on the altar and the sanctuary with it, and the triumph at Caleran carried empty litters. Qiryath's charter government ended, imperial officers governed the surviving settlement directly, and the treasury charged every Jirahar household for the missing vessels. Many displaced Jirahar sought shelter with older communities beyond direct Atherian rule. Three centuries later Vestian's persecutions of 377–398 AR, sharpened when the lamps of his compulsory Profession failed to kindle in 385, found their households by the rolls of that debt and destroyed or confiscated most remaining eastern towers. About 396 Qiryath itself was cleared under a charter that forbids rebuilding *before the Mesakha*, words Vestian wrote in his own hand: the Church reads them as never, and the Jirahar as a promise. Survival depends on households, law, records and the terms communities can negotiate with their hosts.

**Inherited wound:** the Jirahar remember the Church as persecutor, while Church cultures remember the Jirahar through suspicion, prophecy anxiety and inherited guilt.

## 11. Ahl-e-Ahd Rise

The Ahl-e-Ahd develop from desert oath and water-right peoples into a contract civilisation with legal metaphysics, bound entities, golem labour and military enforcement. Their rise begins in a broken well-country. In the Ubar Toll War of 514–523 Atheria and Vashar each voided the other's well-awards in the dry country and granted the same wells again, so that every well had two lawful holders, and the dry country fell into feud. Mujalib grew up in it, received his revelation in 541 beside a well with two masters and spent eight years sealing the shares of disputed wells, which nobody afterwards dared foul. From 546 the Basin's flood failed in five summers of seven, and an ox plague killed the settled world's teams while sparing the desert's camels. Through the famine the 4,444 bound devils of the Covenant dug the oasis and channels at Al-Qahur while children starved in the canal towns below, and the grain went to those who sealed their names into the Covenant. Vashar's relay stations stood empty, Covenant jurists declared its last king forsworn in 555, and the horse-lords of Ruzhan sealed for bread. Every name on the roll was owed bread in a failed year. Mujalib took no ruler's title; in 563 he gave his authority away under three Seals, of Water, Names and the Roads, and he lived until 596.

From 568 the First Unsealing took the Atherian north with armies that marched behind their lawyers. The roll had outgrown the Basin's bread, and the Covenant's bound carriers would lift only grain that had been given. Breach-courts asked each town to produce the witness to its charter, sworn long ago before augur colleges Vestian had dissolved. A town that took terms entered its people on the roll, gave a share of each harvest and received a bound keeper at its well, and half the north was served, not fought. The one pitched battle, at the Dry Ford in 575, was an Atherian victory, and the winning army broke up that summer at wells that drew only for the roll. The conquest stopped where hermit-bishops had read the charters again before living witnesses and the Fire had come on the wax. The conquerors were already two peoples. When Mujalib died in 596 the college produced a Fourth Seal naming Zuhayr ibn Qays, who became the first caliph, and the Prophet's daughter Rumaitha produced another naming her son Hilal; the marshal Qatada halted his army until the two could be tried. In 603 Hilal, travelling to arbitration under Qatada's safe conduct, was pushed off his named road by a flood. At the station well of Bir Qassim the bound keeper drew water for his escort and his drovers and brought the bucket up empty for the fifty-eight named in the instrument, and a court ruled their protection void. On the sixth night Hilal read the safe conduct aloud at the well-mouth, clause by clause. At dawn the escort refused to disarm the household and carried those who could ride to the strait, and Qatada rode after it. Sixteen died of thirst beside a full well, Rumaitha among them, and the caliph's column killed the dying and the drovers who had given them water. Hilal lived, veiled, at Qazmarid. The Sealed Blood mourn that reading every year with the answer *And yet the well was dry*, and their villages still draw their water by hand. At Tamsar in 611 a breach-court's ruling failed against such a town and a Caleran reliquary burned yellow before both armies; neither side gave battle, and the peace of 612 held every disputed charter *neither void nor in force*. The Velian cities, under the Banu Mizan, put their charters into fireproof chests.

**Inherited wound:** contract order becomes both a real civilisational strength and a spiritual danger. The conquered north remembers that it was read its own charters, and the Velian chests are still shut. The Covenant itself has been two houses since Bir Qassim, and one of them trusts no bound keeper with its water.

## 12. The Fall of Atheria

From Vestian's death the palace's rites failed in plain sight while frontier hermits and hospitals went on kindling. A Thalmyric refuge chronicler named it the Guttering: *in the palace the lamps guttered, and in the passes they burned.* The court entered failed rites as postponed and hired Sestaran warlocks to throw Shell-fire for it. When Tavoran II's accession lamp flared in 681, an unpaid warlock later sold a brass fire-cup and his account of the night to a Covenant factor, and Covenant jurists kept a file of counterfeit fire for two hundred years. The frontier itself held through kindled marches, each sworn in the Fire and renewed by an emperor kneeling in person before a frontier monk, so that an emperor who would not ride could not call his marches. In 653 the eastern tax fleet sank, the eastern bishops kept the revenue, and from then on Aurelium tested relics honestly because the truth cost it nothing; in 711 Bishop Oswin recorded the Velos Standard silent and held the eastern oaths in trust for the emperor who proved lawful.

In 874 Corvenas II broke the peace to "receive" the Velian cities. Before Oronda in 876 the Velos Standard failed to kindle in front of both armies, the crusade collapsed, and in 884 Qahir ibn Ruzbeh won the ruling that Atheria had voided the peace of 612. A second ruling, that the Clause binding the 4,444 to take nothing not given binds parties and not powers, let his foundries cage beasts from Hell and the names of the condemned. Qahir had risen by paying his army from the labour of nine thousand unransomed crusade prisoners entered under his own name. His successor Idris was thirty-one and sonless, and every oath in the army named him and nobody after him, so his jurists kept him at Iqashar and he never saw the city taken in his name. In 905 his breach-court train, under Qahir's old engine-master Barzin, read Caleran its own charter district by district before the great engines came to the river dyke that kept the Aurin out of the lower city, and the Depth-Core frames waded the flood to lean on the one length its builders had made to break in their test, which the Red Star was said to have marked. Corvenas III, who could have saved the city only by passing tests in public, had held rites inside the Ash Hall that could fail in private. On the last day he carried the Velos Standard onto the dyke and called on the Fire before both armies. Nothing came, the quarters still holding took terms that afternoon, the engines broke the dyke at dusk and the river drowned the poor quarters no court had served, and Caleran fell. The Covenant's court would accept from him only his person and his relics. He lived three years as their captive keeper and died among them in 908, and only then could the refuge raise a standard of its own. Idris's ruling on the Eternal Ember came by courier: a deposit for the final audit, kept where it stood, so the basilica stayed open around it under guard for fifty-two years.

**Inherited wound:** no successor state can prove itself without arguing over the fall, and the two churches draw opposite lessons from the Guttering.

## 13. Thalmyric Founding and the Long Reconquest

Mountain resistance, holy fire, refuge government and imperial memory become Thalmyria across [[The Long Reconquest (908-1052 AR)|five generations of reconquest]]. In the winter of 906 a Covenant column came up the valley against Veridun, and the refuge council ordered the store-cave flour burned. The hearth-keepers of Veridun Minor baked it instead, all night; the smoke of the ovens lay over the valley for the five days the bread lasted, the besieging levies dreamed of their mothers' hearths and walked home, and Thalmyria keeps the Veil as its supreme feast of the Mother. At Caelmount in 917 the valley's cave-wardens shut the vents that kept Mount Askre's dead air out of their granary caves, and the Covenant column that lay down in them died there through the winter. The valley could never store bread in those caves again. Caedros Aetherion, who led the mountain league, had promised to feed it until it had granaries; he took Virelos and its granaries in 921 to keep the promise and was crowned there the next year. Caleran returns in 957, but the core provinces are not secured until 1052; the Velian States and Zanthir remain outside imperial rule. The enemy was coming apart as well: the Qahirid army's oaths lapsed when Idris's heir refused to take them up and Idris died (929–931), and from 951 Tarkhan riders on blessed Atherian horses held Iqashar's army on its northern wells. The amir of the occupied south tried in 957 to carry the Eternal Ember off to his ships. Its Covenant keepers, whose names were pledged that it would never be moved, held it against him, and eleven died at the lamp; the dockers burned his ships, and the keepers opened the forum only on terms that protected the garrison families he had shut out. He was taken alive, and Thalmyria kept him at Veridun signing the releases of the garrisons sworn to him; it took most of the amirates that refused at their canal heads, where a field registered at an imperial water-court got its share and an amir kept his walls above a dry valley. The Reconquest found the Eternal Ember still burning, with a Covenant ledger of its fuel and custody, and Veridun paid the bill.

Occupation resettlement means return is never restoration to an empty land. Garrison families, bound locals, transplanted subjects, surviving villages and refugee descendants possess competing claims.

The restored empire put the Fire back into endowed office, and its lamps guttered as they had before the Fall. In 982 a bought chaplain's lamp stayed dark while a town burned, and its survivors began asking every lamp in the empire to burn for them and breaking the ones that would not. A soldier-emperor, Tavoran III, rode the Breaking for the Church's estates and hooded the Great Lamp of Veridun in bronze to keep it from the question; households hid their own lamps in wells and ovens. The Breaking ended without a decree when the Breakers' own hero became Patriarch and taught them there was no test. Tavoran's son gathered the surviving lamps into crown houses to protect them, made the houses compulsory after one lamp burned the officer who forced it, and found his own lamp dead on the first shelf when a drought brought the hidden lamps up out of the wells and the villages broke the seals (1049). Families walked to Caleran to beg a light, and in 1052 the whole empire was relit from the Ember the Covenant had kept; that night the hood came off the Great Lamp after sixty-six years, and it was burning.

Caedros III's frontier districts, settled on land the Two Winters emptied, won the Southern Deeds war by kindling in defence of their homes. Caedros spent the enemy's ransom on roads and aqueducts and borrowed more, and he left his succession to the Fire to bar his own son, who had cut down surrendered men at Vresna. At his bier in 1159 no claimant's lamp kindled; the son's flared with bought fire and the court acclaimed him on it, a district widow's lamp took the Flame, and after three years of war the district armies made her warrant theirs. The throne passed to the districts and the debts to their levies, and those burdens made the frontier open to the Covenant's offer of land without service. At Qarath's Well (1214) a thirsty army drank from a sworn well whose curb promised a guest's peace until the next sunset, and sickened at dawn when it took up arms against its host. At sunset the well named its own price, a well for every cup, and the Sultan-Caliph himself could not lower it. The emperor, who had drunk last with his men, would not give up the aqueduct claimed for his cup; his chancellor pressed his seal to a decree taking the drinkers' farms; he left his own palace for a monastery, and the Covenant held the Throne to the debt anyway. In 1225 he poured out a cup of water untasted before the altar at Virelos and gave his claim to Nikandros, the commander who had read the curb aloud and been overruled. Every emperor since has poured out the cup.

**Inherited wound:** reconquest becomes spiritual identity, while the cities fill with people who possess ancestral homes they cannot lawfully or safely recover, and the frontier learns that its loyalty can be bought with water and land. Every household lamp in Thalmyria now descends from a fire the enemy kept, and since 1215 no emperor will drink as a Covenant guest or sign a Covenant instrument.

## 14. Eclessian Fragmentation

In the east a march banner that kindled for a miller's widow made a king, and the bishops who had held the provinces' oaths in trust since 711 credited the sign to him. Adalmar's register war on the Folk ended at a lake that stood up. The Split of 1015 began as a demand that Veridun lift the hood from its Great Lamp; the Patriarch nailed it shut, and two years later the Sanctum crowned Adalmar in the mud of the ford where the banner had kindled. No heir of his could pass the examination that had made him. Examinations became the price of crowns, and at Drossfeld (1041) the lot drawn to choose an emperor came up blank and froze the realms where they stood. In 1061 the cold hearth-lamp of Strazgorod's burned hall, carried to Veridun to be relit from the Ember, lit itself in its fire-keeper's hands in the cathedral. The Patriarch kept it as the Fire's chosen dwelling; Vesari law makes whoever keeps a house's fire its lord, and Virelia's prince took his fire from the Sanctum's altar sooner than be Veridun's man. In the Two Winters the last Adalmarid king refused to carry the unworn Crown of Embers to its examination, and it went into the Sanctum's vault (1104). Eclessia survives as a church-bound but politically fragmented world: kingdoms, duchies, cantons, reform currents, relic politics and competing claims to true doctrine.

**Inherited wound:** unity is desired, feared and constantly deferred.

## 15. The Sweat, the Black Wind and the Held Proofs

The Grey Sweat (1232–1247) killed priests faster than their parishioners, the dead went unsung, and the question of who sends the dead became the question of the age. In 1240 a Primarch died unsung on the night of his election; his twin buried him in the conclave garden, reigned twenty months in his ring and let any baptised person sing a funeral where no priest lived, until sacristans saw a figure with the Primarch's face at the garden wall and the garden was dug. Thousands of parishes no longer knew whether their dead had been sent. Eclessia answered with Sending Houses and assurance letters. An ascetic Primarch, elected on a banker's credit, taught that the seal itself did the sending (1283) and gave the letters away as alms by the hundred thousand, so that parishes stopped singing for their poor and the letters outran the singers, until a Sending House chaplain, Ansgar of Brenholt, counted them in the margins of his psalter. He drowned with forty-one miners in 1297, and their widows buried them by having his margins read aloud in place of the rite they were queuing to buy. A synod put the dead man's psalter to the fire, voided the test when it would not burn (1299) and charged the country a second fee for its funerals. The miners of the upper Weiss swore in the river and fought out of their pits for twenty-three years. The old Church's council answered early (1319–1324), repudiated the seal and ordered every unsung letter sung; the levy for that debt turned the Duke of Ironvale, who burned his duchy's letters (1329), and a princes' war followed over who should sing the dead and who should pay. It ended when Ironvale's guild ministry banked its furnaces until the princes talked, commissioners sat up in disputed churchyards to see which lay quiet, and the Korrheim Accord gave every yard to the rite that had kept its dead sent, sworn with hands in the same water (1343). Thalmyria refused the Sanctum's offer of a crown and Eclessian lances at the price of a trial of its Great Lamp (1227), recovered half its lost districts when plague-widowed Covenant garrisons marched home, and was saved and remade by a frontier commander it adopted (1328). The Covenant lost its witnesses. When the sultan's son died of the Sweat at a frontier fort with only his Guard escort to see it, his father named the Guard a lawful witness so that the prince's name could be released (1243), and its bench has made every sovereign since. On the steppe a captive farrier's blessing renewed the horse-grace (1249), and Qarayel's Black Wind made the settled powers pay until the grace thinned. A Jirahar claimant, Meshalem, led half the tower towns back to the Empty City on roads the Black Wind had emptied; the Covenant voided the charters of every family that left, and before the whole camp he twice went down the stair to the silent gate and twice came back without touching it (1291). On the night Qarayel died, by the watchers' account, his stallion carried the body out of the camp with no one at its head and the sacred mares followed; the herds came home on the eleventh day without him, in four bands, and were named as four Hordes (1304). The Medori held the registers of the dead and lent to everyone. When a pamphlet named Fiorenzia's unsung dead, the wax faces the city kept of them began to speak; the senate burned the faces against a friar's warning, a banker's son-in-law opened the vault and gave the city its names, and the citizens made his house hereditary dukes (1346–1349). Castara crossed the Boiling Sea on a day the storms stopped (1328).

**Inherited wound:** every power holds a proof it dares not test, and owes someone for its dead.

## 16. Present-Day Fractured Inheritance War

The current board is not merely geopolitical. It is a struggle over who inherits Atheria, who owns the Redeemer's public meaning, who controls relics and archives, who commands lawful violence, and whose account of history becomes reality.

**Current question:** can restoration happen without another empire of coercion?
