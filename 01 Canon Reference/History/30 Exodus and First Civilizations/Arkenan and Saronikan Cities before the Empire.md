# Arkenan and Saronikan Cities before the Empire

The islands of later Arkena and the harbours of later Saronika formed an eastern maritime centre of Atherian civilisation. Their ports, workshops, schools and civic households developed alongside Caleran and the Aurin towns. These societies contributed ships, learning and wealth to the later republic while preserving strong local identities.

Arkena and Saronika are used here as familiar geographic names. Their ancient cities had separate governments, and neither the modern military province nor the later maritime kingdom existed during this period. The five principal names associated with Arkena—Verion, Pyrassa, Irenai, Delkos and Kyrame—refer to settlements whose institutions acquired different forms over the centuries.

## Islands, fields and water

Sheltered harbours supported fishing and exchange, while cultivated slopes supplied oil, wine and some grain. The balance varied from island to island. Cisterns and maintained terraces made settlement possible beyond the immediate waterfront. A neglected retaining wall could wash soil into the harbour; a damaged cistern could force a household to abandon fields despite a profitable season at sea.

Naverion and Erenost grew as shore markets with access to food, labour and repair materials. The Elmarai Isles supplied anchorages and communities accustomed to movement between islands. Arkenan ports linked these places to other Thalassian producers. Caleran lay on a different, north-western coast: contact with it required longer maritime journeys or transfers through inland routes.

Early residents included descendants of local Orphaned survivors and maritime settlers. Some households preserved older fishing and agricultural practices while adopting Atherian speech. Others acquired land through armed occupation. Craftspeople, freed captives and marriage partners moved between ports, making civic affiliation an increasingly important distinction within mixed populations.

## The earlier heroic coast

The maritime communities inherited memories of the smaller Giant powers surviving the Unbinding. [[The Siege and the Returnings|High Harbour's siege and the returnings]], around 1980–1930 BR, belonged to their approaches before the city world described here had developed. A mixed coalition destroyed the stronghold, and its subsequent voyages produced stories of supernatural pursuit, changed bodies, captivity and settlement among strangers.

Later island and shore tellers preserved different accounts of the victors. Some honoured captains; others followed the shipmistress and escaped captives. The cycle's tide mistress, wind envoy and mailed apparition acquired familiar divine names in later songs. Those traditions entered the maritime religious world alongside local practices and older Orphaned memories.

## The Erenost grain seizure

Around 1550–1500 BR, Erenost supplied an armed fleet commanded by **Kassilon**, a captain supported by several ship-owning households. He had secured profitable escorts and defended vessels against raiders. When his patrons prepared a larger campaign, they treated the harbour's stored food as an advance on the expected spoils.

Kassilon's men seized grain intended for resident households and the crews staying ashore for repairs. Shipwrights refused to finish vessels whose provisions had been taken. The captain arrested leading workers and compelled captives to continue the work under guard. Several prisoners died during the hurried repairs, and families fled to relatives along the coast.

Merchants at Naverion and island suppliers withheld further deliveries, fearing similar seizures. Kassilon sailed with a reduced fleet. The campaign failed to produce enough wealth to pay his supporters, and his own ship households withdrew their backing. He lost command and disappeared from the harbour's public life; later accounts disagree about his death.

Erenost's resident houses subsequently took joint responsibility for designated food stores and the timing of large departures. Prominent owners benefited most, but yard workers gained an established occasion to press for supplies before work began. The episode remained distinct from the Oar-King legend: Kassilon's surviving reputation concerned the misuse of an existing command, rather than an ascent from slavery to kingship.

## Pyrassa's grain voyages

Pyrassa's prosperity depended partly on imported grain. Around 1220–1180 BR, a succession of poor harvests exposed the weakness of leaving each household to compete for cargo when prices were already rising. **Doreva**, who managed a store and several shares in trading vessels, organised purchases before the sailing season and spread the cargo among different ships.

Her arrangements depended on work ashore. Families repaired storage bins, moved older grain first and paid people to inspect arrivals for damp. One shipment spoiled despite these precautions. Doreva's partners absorbed part of the loss, while households that had already sold animals to buy food remained poorer. The experience encouraged the city to maintain reserves over more than one season.

The voyages enlarged the influence of suppliers and store owners, including women whose inherited property gave them a place in the venture. Dock workers and cultivators contributed labour with less control over the returns. Annual meals at the stores nevertheless became occasions at which people celebrated the arrival of grain and remembered relatives who had spent the season away.

Doreva's name survived in the practice of opening older stores before displaying the newest cargo at a public feast. It commemorated the work of keeping food usable. Later writers sometimes turned her into a queen; the surviving tradition of her business and partnerships gives a more specific account of her importance.

## Workshops, schools and civic competition

The island cities supported occupations which drew customers from beyond their own populations. Bronze workers produced fittings, tools and decorated vessels. Teachers attracted pupils interested in navigation, public speaking and the interpretation of inherited observations. Families supplied accommodation and food to visiting students, giving education a place in the urban economy.

At Irenai, the teacher **Mesthra**, active around 960–910 BR, compared observations brought by pilots with the seasonal reckonings used by temple households. Differences reflected viewing conditions, local practice and copying as well as disagreement about sacred meaning. Her teaching required pupils to describe where and when an observation had been made before interpreting it.

Mesthra's household school remained dependent on patrons. A donor who wanted a favourable departure day could resent a teacher who explained why the evidence was uncertain. Some pupils entered temple service; others taught navigation or carried methods to another city. The school contributed to the habits of comparison from which later academies developed, without producing one universally accepted account of the stars.

Delkos prospered through repair work and transfer of cargo. Kyrame's workshops and surrounding cultivated districts served a more local market as well as visiting vessels. Verion grew as a place where shipping interests and public assemblies could meet. Rival festivals brought performers between these towns, and households competed to entertain guests with food, music and well-made vessels. These pleasures also created livelihoods which survived changes in rulers.

## Sea powers and the stolen sounding-weight

Voyagers made offerings to powers associated with weather, routes and the coast. Accounts of successful passage circulated alongside stories of disappearance and bodily transformation. Different communities attributed similar events to different beings; a widely sung divine name could cover several local explanations.

The raid remembered in [[01 Canon Reference/History/80 Myths and Legendary Histories/G-74 - The Raid That Returned One Tool.md|The Raid That Returned One Tool]] belongs to the Arkenan–Saronikan corridor around 1120–980 BR. A sea-king attacked a populated court stronghold for a sounding-weight that revealed one safe reef approach. Defenders died, and most raiders drowned, were captured or were transformed. The returned tool helped a hungry harbour. That use did not restore the dead or free the people left behind.

The city's name and the king's identity remain uncertain. Neither Kassilon, Doreva nor Mesthra is identified with the raiders or their descendants. [[01 Canon Reference/History/30 Exodus and First Civilizations/The Refusal at Kelarion.md|Kelarion's refusal]] belongs to a separate coastal history.

## Relations with the Caleran republic

Caleran's religious and political arrangements circulated through travellers, marriages and commercial agreements. Island communities could adopt a festival reckoning without joining a particular military league. The twelve-city Compact's existence therefore did not make every Atherian-speaking port a member. Later republican expansion produced additional and unequal relationships with coastal cities.

Valeron II's Ortheian hegemony reached selected ports through ship assessments, garrisons and alliances with leading households. Eryndor III drew vessels, crews and money from this eastern maritime world for the invasion of Arqesh. Delkos became especially important after the conqueror's death in 684, when his fleet commander Serevan diverted the royal funeral there and established a Successor kingdom among the islands and eastern harbours.

Serevan's court joined Atherian military practice to Arqeshite, Vashari and local maritime traditions. Later wars reduced the kingdom, while its officer houses, coin standards and naval titles spread through other ports. One such Delkosi house produced Nerethan, the commander who arrived in the Gateway country in 499 BR.

Some harbours supplied ships or paid for protection while preserving local councils. Others lost the ability to determine their own military contributions after defeat. Republican patrons invested in cargo and workshops, and island families sought citizenship or useful marriage connections. Naval service carried men farther from home, increasing both earnings and the danger that a household would lose its principal worker.

The [[The Gateway Wars|Gateway Wars]], approximately 650–520 BR, made those obligations more demanding. Inner-sea ports supplied the fleets and repair facilities through which Caleran fought its maritime rival. Repeated losses required replacement crews and equipment. Allied cities could use that dependence to negotiate, although relief secured by prominent shipowners did not necessarily reach the workers or families serving beneath them.

By the later republican centuries, the island world was closely involved in competition among commanders. A port's support might depend on an exemption from requisition or a promise to repair harbour works. Rival commanders could divide merchants from crews and one prominent family from another. Civic institutions survived within these disputes and became part of the world inherited by the empire.

## Later inheritance

Ancient quays, cisterns, workshops and observatories were repeatedly adapted. Imperial government, Conversion, occupation and Reconquest added further layers. The modern cities preserve different combinations of those histories. Their councils and academies have ancient antecedents, but their present powers were also shaped by much later settlements.
