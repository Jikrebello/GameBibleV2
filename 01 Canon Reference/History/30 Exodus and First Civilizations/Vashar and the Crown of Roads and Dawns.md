# Vashar and the Crown of Roads and Dawns

**Vashar** was a highland country east of the Ubar whose valleys, pasture roads and sanctuary towns were united by House Tiravan during the fall of Arqesh. Arezan I and his successors created the **Crown of Roads and Dawns**, an empire governed through royal relatives, road commanders, subject rulers and established city institutions. It protected the return to Qiryath, imposed tribute across former Arqeshite lands and remained an independent peer when Atheria unified in 350 BR. A Vasharan civil war, the War of the Two Dawns, let Atheria take Qiryath in the last century before the Redeemer, and the two crowns fought and bargained over the roads for six hundred years more. Vashar ended in 555–560 AR, when famine emptied the relay stations of the Dawn Road and Covenant jurists declared the last King at Dawn forsworn.

## Vashar and its peoples

**Vashar** is the name for the high country east of the Ubar riverlands. Long cultivated valleys lie between dry ridges and colder upland pastures. Snowmelt feeds the western tributaries. Roads rise through narrow passes towards horse country, mining districts and farther lands beyond the known world's dependable surveys.

The **Vashari** developed from several Orphaned populations whose ancestors survived the Giant collapse outside the Glass refuge. Their villages, fortified estates and sanctuary towns existed before House Tiravan united them. Related languages, marriage customs and teachings concerning truthful order linked these communities. Political unity arrived much later through alliances, conquest and the royal service created by House Tiravan.

Three regions supplied the later imperial core. The **Kavresh valley** produced grain, fruit and the principal royal house. The high pastures of **Ruzhan** bred horses and supported mobile aristocratic households. The **Daryam approaches** contained copper, iron and old sanctuaries in which household fires were tended across generations. Villages commonly combined farming with seasonal movement. A household might cultivate valley land, send adolescents to summer pasture and maintain a relative in a market town.

The Vashari encountered Arqesh through trade, warfare and hostage exchange. Highland timber, stone, metal and horses descended to the canal cities. River grain and finished cloth travelled uphill. Arqeshite rulers tried to control the passes through allied lords and garrisons. Some Vashari houses prospered as intermediaries. Others lost children to hostage courts or land to collaborators.

## Before the imperial crown, c. 1300–760 BR

The oldest Vashari political unit was the **hearth domain**. Several villages and pasture households pledged service to a fortified landholding family or an important sanctuary in return for protection, access to winter stores and representation in disputes. The domain's ruler judged water, grazing, compensation for injury and military service with an assembly of household leaders. Larger coalitions formed for war and commonly dissolved when their leaders quarrelled over spoils or marriage alliances.

The sanctuary town of **Daryam** became influential during the Breaking of the Bronze Roads. Its keepers maintained a sheltered market and a fire used to light household lamps after winter. Keeping it alight through the closed season represented the community's obligation to preserve life and deal truthfully.

Teachers associated with Daryam called truthful speech, kept promises and the defence of cultivated land **the Right Order**. They called deliberate deceit and the abuse of a promise **the Lie**, treating it as human corruption. Royal teachers later identified loyalty to the king with the Right Order. This allowed a ruler who had broken faith to condemn resistance as falsehood.

The oldest attributed teacher is **Ashara of the Three Questions**, placed uncertainly around 1190 BR. Her surviving sayings ask whether a gift remains generous when refusal brings punishment, whether a ruler speaks truth when his messenger cannot contradict him and whether a pure fire excuses stolen fuel. Several later sects claim her.

House Tiravan first appears among the Kavresh valley rulers around 900 BR. Its estates controlled fruit terraces, a ford and the road to Ruzhan. Marriage with pasture houses supplied cavalry. Marriages with Daryam keepers supplied religious prestige. The house alternated between service to Arqesh and resistance against it.

## Arezan Tiravan and the coalition against Arqesh

**Arezan Tiravan** was born around 742 BR, the year Arqesh destroyed First Qiryath. His father **Vahrad Tiravan** had sent him to Durqesh as a political hostage after a failed rising. Arezan learned Arqeshite, trained with royal cavalry and formed relationships with provincial officers. His sister **Mithara** remained in Kavresh and managed the family estates during their father's illness.

Arezan returned after Vahrad's execution in 716 BR. He inherited a divided household. One uncle wanted submission; Ruzhan allies demanded war; tenant villages feared another Arqeshite levy more than they trusted the Tiravans. Mithara secured the valley by restoring the grazing and inheritance agreements her father had suspended.

The war that followed was fought along Mithara's stations. Arezan was seldom in Kavresh after 706, and his sister needed to know within days where he was and what he lacked. She bound each hearth domain along the pass roads to keep a house with fodder, a lit hearth and four fresh animals, a day's ride from the next, and counted the service as that domain's levy. Riders changing horses at these houses carried her letters from Kavresh to Durqesh in five days. The line was later carried on to Arqesh and the coast and called the **Dawn Road**. Its keepers were hearth-domain families long before they were royal servants, and the captains Arezan placed in the gate cities along it became the first road commanders.

The Arqeshite succession war beginning around 706 BR gave Arezan an opportunity to leave provincial service. Mardun Ubar, governor of Durqesh and Arezan's relative by marriage, sought Vashari support against Kassur III. Arezan accepted weapons and passage through Durqesh while negotiating independently with Nemeru, Qahuri rebels, Tarkhan leaders and subject governors. Northern allies supplied camel scouts, foot archers and guides; isolated elite houses also possessed a few riding and chariot horses. When Mardun attempted to withdraw from their agreement, his own guards killed him and opened the city.

Arezan presented the coalition as a restoration of roads and truthful rule. Its members pursued different aims. Nemeru wanted freedom from Arqeshite tax officers. Tarkhan leaders wanted hostages released, pasture restored and royal animal stations broken. Qahuri crowns wanted their eastern approaches returned. Vashari houses sought land, office and vengeance.

The western invasion of **Eryndor III of Ortheia** changed the scale of the war. Eryndor had inherited an Atherian-speaking upland kingdom, a hegemony imposed upon several city leagues and a standing army that had killed his father, which he left at home. After crossing the eastern sea in 702 BR, he defeated Arqeshite coastal armies and left his fleet to blockade the fortified harbour of Terys. He entered divided Qahur in 695 BR, where the crowns shut their granaries and he fed his army on grain borrowed from a dead financier's house.

Arezan and Eryndor met in 694 BR. They agreed to destroy House Ubar while retaining separate commands. Arezan controlled the highland roads, provincial alliances and eastern approaches; Eryndor brought the larger siege army, fleet and treasury. The agreement was reinforced when Eryndor married **Parena Tiravan**, the adult daughter of Mithara. The marriage gave Eryndor a place within the Tiravan household and gave Arezan influence at the western king's court. Parena accompanied the royal household east and became an intermediary for Vashari officers, Ortheian commanders and Arqeshite families seeking terms. In 691, in the camp before Arqesh, she bore the western king a daughter, **Mehrava**.

The final campaigns included massacre and enslavement by both victorious armies. Arezan punished some killings when they deprived him of useful officials and ignored others committed by necessary allies. In 690 BR the dredging gangs and deportee quarters of Arqesh, their rations cut to feed Kassur's soldiers, rose and opened the gates. Arezan's forces seized the eastern gates and water stations while Eryndor's soldiers crossed a closed channel in hulls launched by the deported Terys families of the Nemeru yards. The two armies plundered districts assigned to one another after the surrender.

Kassur III escaped with several sons and the portable royal images. In 688 BR the images began to answer in Eryndor's voice, and their bearers carried them across to him by night; Eryndor kept them as his own regalia. Kassur, his guard scattered and two of his sons dead, rode to Arezan's outposts at Durqesh and surrendered to the former hostage he had known in the royal cavalry there. Eryndor demanded him. Arezan refused, and the two kings had their first open quarrel. At Durqesh Kassur sealed the Gate House's water books over to Arezan, which is why the Ubar canal officials served Vashar without a second conquest. Arezan kept the last Ubar king as a guarded guest in an orchard house at Kavresh until his death around 676, and retained royal children and former Ubar officers in guarded households of his own. Vashari houses had recognised Arezan as king in 704 BR, fourteen years before Arqesh fell; he reigned until 671 BR.

## The Crown of Roads and Dawns

Arezan adopted the title **Keeper of the Roads and King at Dawn**. Later historians call the state he established the **Vasharan Empire** or **Crown of Roads and Dawns**. During Eryndor's lifetime, however, Arqesh and the conquered provinces had no single master. Eryndor occupied the High Palace and treasury. Arezan held the Gate House, eastern roads and much of the surviving provincial administration. Canal officials continued working because neither army could feed the city without them.

Eryndor left Arqesh in 687, after Kassur's images began mocking him in a dead companion's voice, and Arezan let him through the highlands. He came back wounded in 685 and died in 684 BR aboard the flagship of the allied fleet in the roads of Nemeru, waiting for a wind to take him home. His commanders acknowledged no heir and divided the army and treasury among themselves. Arezan used the crisis to secure the highlands, eastern roads and most of the Ubar basin.

The empire reused Arqeshite institutions. Canal governors retained their staffs. Local languages remained in administration. Subject rulers kept courts where their cooperation reduced the cost of occupation. Vashari relatives and allied houses received the highest military and provincial offices.

In 684 BR, during the first division of Eryndor's commands, Arezan issued the **Decree of Roads and Houses**. It authorised several displaced peoples to restore sanctuaries, recover approved sacred objects and return to property recognised by Vasharan officials. The decree served the king's interest by weakening Arqeshite military households and reopening neglected roads. It also allowed families to leave captivity and rebuild communal life.

Jirahar teachers later described the return decree as an act of providence. Vasharan inscriptions claimed that the Right Order had chosen Arezan to correct the Lie of Arqesh. The Jirahar accounts praised the release and return of captive households while continuing to record the killings committed by Arezan's coalition. Vasharan court histories used the decree to support broader claims about his right to rule.

The **Wars of the Inheritance**, fought from 684 to about 650 BR, divided Eryndor's conquests. Arezan held Vashar and most of the Ubar basin. **Demeran Kavos**, speaker of the companies Eryndor had left at home, took Ortheia and the western army. **Serevan of Delkos**, commander of the allied fleet, established a mixed court among the islands and eastern harbours. **Neresh of Qemaret**, a Qahuri-born officer, held southern garrisons and crown towns through marriage and military settlement. Each claimed to act in the dead king's name. The Terys crews of the flagship had sailed Eryndor's embalmed body and Kassur's images to Serevan's harbour at Delkos, and within months a voice in the tomb there declared that the king still reigned and had no heir, and sealed grants that promised the same provinces twice.

Parena rode up the Dawn Road to Kavresh, where Mithara had kept Mehrava since the western king marched east. Demeran killed the half-brother whom the upland assembly had acclaimed in Ortheia and sent agents after mother and child; Arezan's road guards took them at Durqesh and hanged them in the gate. Vasharan accounts treated the attempt as proof of western treachery. Ortheian histories called Mehrava a camp child never shown to an upland assembly, and cited the voice at Delkos, which had declared the king heirless; Vashari households answered that he had named her before two armies under the walls of Arqesh. Mithara kept mother and daughter in her valley household and arranged marriages for two surviving members of Parena's household. Their descendants carried rival accounts of Eryndor's final days into Vashari and maritime courts. Around 670, in the last year of her brother's life, Mithara married Mehrava to his younger son **Vahrad**.

## Daryav I and the offering that smoked, 671–638 BR

Arezan's eldest surviving son, **Daryav I Tiravan**, inherited a coalition held together by his father's friendships and his aunt's stations. The captains in the gate cities were by now called **road commanders**. Each collected tribute, raised soldiers, supplied the relay houses on his stretch of road and watched the provinces for the royal family. Village assemblies, city councils and subject rulers continued to govern local affairs beneath him. Messengers could cross the core of the empire rapidly; merchants travelled more slowly and paid at bridges and stations. The road brought markets and news to some towns while exposing them to tax collectors and requisition.

Daryav inherited his father's wager on Qiryath and paid only part of the stake. Arezan's grants had raised the sanctuary's platform and lower courses. The inheritance wars took the road gangs to the coast, the building grant lapsed under Daryav, and the city finished the walls itself. He confirmed the sanctuary estate and sent the consecration offering in his own name all the same. Qiryath's sign, an offering that burned without smoke and left the stones cool, was the one answer in the east that anyone could touch, and it had been withheld since the Arqeshite siege. A return under Vasharan protection would show every province that heaven confirmed the Right Order against the Lie of Arqesh; without one, the crown had still bought a road, a market and a city of interpreters. At the consecration around 660 BR the offering made in the king's name smoked.

Every delegation on the Reqa shelf saw it, and the news went down to the river on Daryav's own road within the week. Nemeru had kept its gates shut against three armies in 690 and had never thought itself conquered. It rose within the year with its river fleet, and canal towns along the lower Ubar followed. Daryav needed three summers to put the rising down. He executed its military leaders after they had surrendered and deported the boatmen's neighbourhoods; officials who had delivered their stores before the siege were pardoned. Some six years after the consecration the stones went cool under an offering carried by mixed households of returners and Arqeshite settlers. Vasharan inscriptions credited the crown, and the Jirahar wrote down which offering had been answered.

Road houses supplied interpreters, maintained selected stations and carried recognised correspondence. The king's officials also used Jirahar household knowledge to assess communities that had previously escaped provincial counting.

After Nemeru Daryav built the **Terrace of Mirrors** at Kavresh, a stepped platform facing east. Each road command furnished a disc of polished bronze, set so that the first light of the dawn audience fell from every province onto the king's seat. A command in revolt had its mirror turned to the wall. Over the door of every relay house the king had one sentence cut: *the king keeps the road*. Tribute records, garrison letters and the lists of the Nemeru deportees record what the light and the sentence left out.

## Religion and royal legitimacy

Vasharan kings adopted the language of Right Order and tended light. A royal flame was carried from Daryam to a new reign's first public audience. It represented household continuity and the king's promise to preserve the roads. Provincial rulers could retain their own worship while acknowledging the royal ceremony.

Three traditions later claimed that a sacred flame had accompanied Arezan's conquest. Daryam remembered a household lamp carried on a mule. An Arqeshite relief showed a royal brazier taken from Kassur's palace. Qiryath remembered a lamp lit when the return decree was read. Later writers fused these separate objects into one travelling relic called the **Threefold Fire**.

In the years after the consecration offering smoked, a radiance like that of the royal ceremony began to appear in the terrace mirrors, in polished armour and in dreams of a king surrounded by kneeling nations. Court diviners called it the **Witness of Dawn** and proclaimed it the sign Qiryath had withheld from the king. Jirahar interpreters at court dated its first appearance to the year of the smoking offering and called it a sign made to order. Its visions encouraged rulers to treat obedience as proof that their subjects recognised the truth.

Some priests resisted. **Mazhara of Daryam**, active under Daryav, taught that a king's lie remained a lie when it was carved over every door, and she refused to call the radiance a sign. After she condemned the killing of surrendered rebels at Nemeru, Daryav made her keeper of the royal flame. Her family's orchards passed to the flame's estate, and their bonded workers now cut fuel for the king's fire. Her sayings were carved on the Kavresh terrace with the hard lines removed. Her students kept the uncarved text, including her refusal to call the Witness of Dawn a sign, and they noted that the royal fire now burned wood cut by her own household's bondsmen: Ashara's third question, come home. Court histories cite the carved sayings as proof of her loyalty.

## Tiraz I and the cut embankment, 638–629 BR

Daryav's son **Tiraz I** believed the mirrors. He carried a travelling set of them west to hold dawn audience in the provinces, replaced two governors who had sent their discs late and put royal surveyors into canal districts whose customary water and pasture rights nobody at Kavresh understood. Villages hid their stores, and herders drove their animals beyond the stations. In 629 BR the king camped with his surveyors and tax clerks on low ground below a canal district west of the Ubar. The district cut its own embankment in the night. Tiraz drowned in his tent with most of the camp, and the mirrors were dug out of the mud downstream. The district lost its fields for three seasons and its elders to the executioner.

## Kavran I and the Far-Road line, 629–594 BR

Tiraz left infant sons. The keepers of Daryam, Mazhara's students among them, carried the royal flame past the infants to **Kavran I Tiravan**, the grown son of Vahrad and Mehrava, and the Ruzhan houses rode in behind it. Kavran was the Far-Road King's grandson, and his branch never let the west forget it: Vasharan inscriptions called the king heir of the Far Road whenever the claim was useful. Tiraz's sons grew up in guarded households, and their descendants supplied pretenders for three centuries.

Kavran inherited a stable core and a contentious western coast. The coast contained mixed cities whose inhabitants spoke local, Vashari, Arqeshite and Atherian languages, and their ships connected the empire to the inner sea. Royal governors supported friendly rulers and demanded ships, taxes and soldiers. Kavran withdrew the surveyors, confirmed the customary water rights his cousin had died trying to measure and took his revenue from the harbours. The Caleran republic was then occupied by the Gateway Wars and sent no unified army against him. Atherian merchants, mercenaries and individual cities nevertheless appeared along the Vasharan coast, where they traded, served rival rulers and learned the passages later used by fleets.

## Mithara I and the repaired empire, 594–552 BR

Kavran died after a short illness. His niece **Mithara I Tiravan**, named for Arezan's sister and Parena's mother, was an experienced estate manager and provincial negotiator. She secured the throne against two adult cousins. One was blinded; the other accepted a highland command and later died during a suspicious hunt.

Mithara renegotiated ship levies, restored estates damaged by western resistance and regularised the crown's claims over the road stations. Her treaties allowed Atherian merchants into selected ports while forbidding those cities to shelter rebels.

She also confirmed Qiryath's internal courts after a governor attempted to appoint the chief sanctuary custodian. The confirmation required a larger annual payment and access to Jirahar route information. Later Qiryath tradition praises her restraint while remembering the price paid for it.

Mithara's treaties, tax agreements and marriages reduced the number of provinces that had to be held by a royal army. Provincial families entered the royal service, while Vashari officials married Arqeshite, coastal and Qahuri houses. Children of these unions governed cities and estates, although the oldest families at Kavresh continued to treat many of them as social inferiors.

## Tiraz II and the grain stations, 552–528 BR

Mithara's son **Tiraz II** spent her savings on the western road. He built permanent grain stations between Arqesh, the Qahuri approaches and the coast, made Avesh the seat of a single western road command and gave it to the Aresh house, which had kept his mother's ports quiet. The stations fed garrisons through bad years. They also gave the governor of Avesh the keys to the bread of every army that marched west.

## Xarun I and the eastern sea wars, 528–491 BR

**Xarun I Tiravan**, his son, inherited as Caleran gathered for its final war against Qarzeth. Within a decade the destruction of Qarzeth released Caleran ships and commanders from the western Gateway struggle. The city of **Tasar** rose in 514 BR after Governor **Mazdan Aresh** intervened in the choice of a new harbour magistrate and increased the city's naval service, and Mazdan was killed there after he surrendered. The magistrate **Liora of Tasar** asked the Atherian leagues for aid and was refused. Tasar fought with hired crews.

The Aresh house recovered the city in 511 BR, when Tasar's poorest harbour quarter opened a gate to the besiegers for bread. Leaders were executed and inhabitants enslaved, and the rebel quarters were sent up the Dawn Road to keep the relay stations, sea families feeding the king's remounts in the snow of the high passes. Fugitive hulls rowed for the Atherian port of Velathene, whose harbour wardens took them in. Xarun demanded the fugitives and was refused.

In 506 BR he sent a fleet to seize the pilot families of Ilyessa, who had guided the fugitives through the Narrows. The fleet anchored in the lee of the islands to hold them, and through one calm summer its sound hulls settled at their moorings until more than half had gone down. The court blamed Ilyessan sorcery and executed the admiral. Xarun concluded that the pilots commanded the water, and that a king who commanded the pilots would command it too. He hired them, took their children to Avesh as hostages and prepared a larger war. His inscriptions at Avesh called him heir of the Far Road through Mehrava, and claimed the Atherian cities as the hegemony Eryndor had held.

The invasion began in 497 BR. Subject fleets, coastal allies and road-command armies advanced by several routes. They won battles, burned resistant settlements and compelled neutral cities to choose sides. The royal fleet passed the **Narrows of Ilyessa** in 495 on the terms Ilyessan pilots kept with the sea there: keep moving, and never call the water yours. In 493 Xarun took the title Keeper of the Roads by Land and by Water and anchored his granary fleet in the Ilyessa lee to feed the siege of Velathene. Through the summer the grain spoiled in sealed jars in sound hulls, the ships settled at their moorings without a leak, and the subject crews rowed by night for Velathene's refuge fire. In the autumn the Atherian league fleet, much of it rowed by those deserters, took the anchored transports.

The army before Velathene starved, and in the winter of 492–491 the flux that was killing it killed the king. His son **Orvaz**, eighteen years old, was acclaimed at the bier by the household regiments while the road commanders broke camp by nations and raced one another home. The Dawn Road ran through Avesh. Its governor, **Ardash Aresh**, brother of the murdered Mazdan, kept the grain stations shut. When the hungry lower town and the army's stragglers stormed the granary gates, his house troops burned lower Avesh to clear the approaches. Orvaz swore the western command to the Aresh house by right, at the gate, in return for bread and remounts, and reached Kavresh before his uncles. The rest of the army came home in 491 BR as it could.

Vasharan tradition remembers the campaign as the defence of order against oath-breaking cities, undone by Ilyessan sorcery and an Aveshi price. Atherian tradition makes every city a willing defender of freedom and the sea a fighter on its side. Several Atherian cities submitted, supplied Xarun or waited to join the winning side. Subject peoples within the Vasharan army pursued their own interests, and at Ilyessa they decided the war.

## Orvaz I and the bought road, 491–439 BR

**Orvaz I Tiravan** never led an army west. He used money and grain. Atherian maritime alliances expanded after his father's defeat. They collected ship money, dismantled the walls of cities attempting to leave and installed friendly councils. Vasharan governors supported exiles from those same cities. Western governors recruited local troops and foreign mercenaries; families changed allegiance to preserve ships, estates or political enemies.

Orvaz financed rival Atherian leagues, hosted exiled rulers and offered naval timber to coalitions willing to recognise Vasharan coastal claims. Provincial governors acquired semi-hereditary influence, although the crown formally appointed them.

He never forgave the gate at Avesh. Around 462 BR the Aresh governor joined three other commands in a revolt. Orvaz promised pardon, separated the coalition through private agreements and then executed two surrendered leaders whose safe conduct had been publicly proclaimed, the Aresh governor first. He kept his oath to the house and gave the command to the dead man's nephew. Courtiers reported him saying that the Aresh had taught him what a hungry man's oath was worth. **Rathava of Daryam**, called the Truth-Teller, condemned him in the palace garden. Orvaz had Rathava's estate labourers brought into the garden in ropes and told him they would be sold unless he unsaid his words. Rathava unsaid them before the court, and the labourers walked home. His school split over that afternoon. One party held that the Truth-Teller had told the single lie that mattered; the other that he had kept an older promise, to the people of his house. Royal historians recorded the recantation as proof that truth served the king.

In his old age, after a run of good harvests, the harvest audience at Kavresh began to hail Orvaz as the one who made the grain rise, and courtiers said the Witness of Dawn filled the terrace mirrors whenever they did. Orvaz accepted the praise. He ordered each road command to send children to be raised in the king's dawn-house as servants of the royal fire. At the harvest audience of 444 BR the heralds gave the signal for the new praise, and the terrace made no sound. Ten thousand people stood with their mouths open. They could speak to one another, curse and pray, and they could not say the words. Orvaz had the heralds flogged. On the third morning he had the dawn-house children brought out to cry the praise, and they could not say it either. He sent them home. Palace physicians wrote of a sickness of the throat that struck one terrace for one sentence. Jirahar interpreters present that day said a messenger had walked along the mirrors. Daryam's keepers wrote nothing. Nobody who stood on the terrace that morning ever spoke the praise again, and for a generation, courtiers said, the radiance did not appear in the palace mirrors. Orvaz reigned five more years, announced at his own audiences by heralds who stammered.

## Tiraz III, Orvaz II and the Caleran crisis, 439–350 BR

**Tiraz III Tiravan** secured the throne after fighting among the royal kin and inherited a wealthy but decentralised empire. With the mirrors dark and the harvest audiences cut short, his western governors feared the king less and dealt increasingly with individual Atherian commanders. Marovan Veyr's campaigns between 414 and 397 BR secured winter harbours, suppressed raiding coalitions and established clients near the Vasharan sphere. Some local rulers played Veyr and the road-command governor against one another.

Tiraz died in 409 BR and left a grandson of nine, **Orvaz II**. In the same years the radiance came back into the terrace mirrors. The keepers of Daryam would not carry the royal flame to a child's audience until his grandmother's council swore that the mirrors would never again be called a sign, and the council took ten years to swear it. A king without the flame could summon no highland levy, and the crown could not contest Veyr's settlements. The regents recognised several of them temporarily in exchange for Atherian withdrawal from Avesh's inland estates.

During the Caleran conflict between Casvilar and Veyr, Orvaz supplied money, pack animals and limited sanctuary to Veyr's clients. He did not commit the central army. The indecisive battle near Velos in 374 BR, and the Oar ports' refusal to pay for Veyr's ships, therefore damaged Vasharan influence without destroying the empire. Veyr fled to sanctuary at Ilamar in 373 and died there of fever in 371.

Thalion's rise after 370 BR confronted Orvaz with a more coherent Atherian government. In 358 Darion Velsar's thrust into the Avesh approaches broke on Vasharan relay riders, reserves moved along the Dawn Road and early snow, and Orvaz came to the settlement of 350 BR the stronger for it. Under its terms Thalion secures the Atherian-speaking ports and several western clients. Orvaz retains Avesh, the Ubar riverlands and the Vashari core. The agreement leaves two large states facing one another across the coastal marches, with neither able to dictate terms throughout the eastern sea.

## The Dawn Road, Raveth and the War of the Two Dawns, after 350 BR

The settlement of 350 left the Dawn Road as Vashar's greatest military advantage. Its relay riders and remount stations had beaten Velsar's columns in the snow, and Atherian commanders said for two centuries that the eastern frontier belonged to whoever had the faster road. Orent I's Arcelon road, built c. 190–182 BR, was Atheria's answer. It pulled the trade of the Raveth river towns west. Those towns had paid canal duty to the Avesh command and sent men each spring to dig silt out of the flood channels. Around 160 BR their councils negotiated entry into the Atherian empire, and Avesh lost a province without a battle.

Late in the reign of the Atherian emperor Cassen I (r. 101–76 BR), the Vasharan crown passed in dispute. Two Tiravan claimants held dawn audiences in the same week. The keepers of Daryam carried the royal flame to the claimant backed by the Ruzhan horse houses and left the one who held Kavresh to rise in lamplight. Daryam's preachers said the Witness of Dawn showed him nations kneeling in his mirrors, and the charge stuck. Atherian writers called the struggle the War of the Two Dawns. It lasted years. The Avesh road command chose the losing side. The victor purged it, pulled the western garrisons east to fight in Ruzhan and, needing silver, sold confirmations of offices across the western sanctuaries. Tribute from the Qahuri crowns lapsed in the same years.

Qiryath was the war's costliest sale. After the sanctuary's Keeper died, a river-quarter house long in Vasharan road service bought the confirmation of his office, but the city's interim Keeper had already made the pilgrimage offering and received the sign. The tower houses called in the Atherian governor at Arcelon, who came to the next pilgrimage with an augur of the Red Star. The purchaser refused the augur's trial by sign. The interim Keeper offered, and his fire smoked like any fire. The governor ruled for him all the same, on the sanctuary's written record of the earlier sign, and a Qoreth scribe drafted the charter under which Atheria held Qiryath. Early in Velen I's reign the victor, still fighting in Ruzhan, accepted the charter by treaty in return for the **Dawn Portion**, a yearly share of Qiryath's pilgrim dues paid to Avesh. Vasharan scribes wrote "lease"; Atherian scribes wrote "cession". The families who would not serve under the charter came east by the Dawn Road and settled in tower communities under Vasharan rule. Those communities later received the refugees of 65 AR.

## Wars with imperial Atheria

Vashar remained independent at the time of the Redeemer. The Dawn Portion ended when Atheria destroyed the sanctuary in 65 AR, and Vasharan claims to Qiryath's revenue joined the grievances behind the war of c. 94–103 AR. Under Daven Talar, Atherian armies advanced toward Avesh in that war. Vasharan forces recovered the exposed posts, and an agreement over tolls and passage ended the campaign. In the war of c. 214–225 AR, Vasharan commanders seized road stations and encouraged border towns to direct tolls east. Both crowns accepted a negotiated evacuation and exchange of captives. The Atherian eastern garrisons went unpaid for years afterwards, and in their mutinies a cult of grave-lamps that burned without oil took hold. During Cassian's reign, the eastern towns again arranged prisoner exchanges and safe conduct for trade along designated routes. When Vestian's persecutions emptied the eastern towers in the 380s and 390s, some Jirahar households reached Vashar under safe conduct, received by kin whose families had come east after 65 AR. Vashar retained its highland core and the Ubar through these wars. [[Atheria between Qiryath and Cassian - Emperors Wars and Revolts|Atheria between Qiryath and Cassian]]

## The end of the Crown, 514–560 AR

In 500 AR the Crown of Roads and Dawns still held the Ubar riverlands and the roads to the grain of the Sacred Basin, and its road commanders had become hereditary lords of their stations. Vashar took its toll on every bale that crossed the Ubar. The Atherian emperor **Teomar Saldane** wanted it. The **Ubar Toll War** of 514–523 was fought for relay stations, bridges and wells. Atherian columns took the western stations of the Avesh corridor and then found that their treasury could not pay to garrison them; Vashar's western commanders, beaten and unpaid, stopped answering their king. Both crowns borrowed against future tolls, and both stopped the stipends that had kept the Ahdari well-houses of the dry country as their clients. The well-country fell into feud. Wells were fouled, guest-law broke down, and caravans paid twice and arrived with half their camels.

Then the rain failed. In the **Thin Floods** of 546–552 the highland snowmelt came thin and the Basin's flood failed in five summers out of seven. From 548 an ox murrain called **the yoke-sickness** spread up from the Basin through the Vasharan valleys. Plough oxen coughed, lay down in the furrows and did not rise; road mules died in their traces at the relay stations. Camels were largely spared, and the desert clans who followed Mujalib kept their mobility while the settled world lost its traction. In the Basin the bound devils of the new Covenant dug channels and an oasis at Al-Qahur through the famine, and the grain went to those who sealed their names into the Covenant.

Vashar had carried one promise over the door of every relay house since Daryav's day: the king keeps the road. By 554 couriers on the Dawn Road found station after station with cold hearths, empty stalls and doors banging in the wind; the horses had been eaten and the keepers had walked home. Covenant preachers rode the empty road reading Mazhara of Daryam's old saying back to the road commanders under those lintels: a king's lie remains a lie when it is carved over every door. In 555 Covenant jurists declared the last King at Dawn forsworn. Road commanders and subject rulers, many unpaid since the Toll War, sealed with the Covenant on terms that kept their estates and their horses. Among them were the horse-lords of **Ruzhan**, who entered Covenant service as road and cavalry officers; three centuries later one of their houses produced Qahir ibn Ruzbeh, founder of the Qahirid dynasty. A few highland valleys fought and were reduced by 560. The last King at Dawn was pensioned at the oasis the bound devils had dug, and died there.

Vashari villages and speech outlived the crown inside the Covenant. In 931 a Vashari-born engine feeder at Iqashar, Gulnar, stopped a walking engine with the words that gave the Qahirid throne its name. [[The Long Frontier and the First Unsealing]] · [[Historical Spine of the Covenant]]

## Government and society

The Great King ruled through the royal household, great Vashari houses, appointed road commanders, local rulers, temple estates and city assemblies. Authority varied by province. Arqesh required canal officials. Qiryath retained communal courts. Coastal cities defended civic privileges. Highland estates relied upon personal allegiance and mounted service.

Taxes arrived as grain, animals, silver, cloth, weapons or labour. Subject troops usually served under their own commanders. Royal inspectors travelled the roads and could hear accusations against governors, although access depended upon influence and distance.

Women of the royal and great houses managed estates, patronage and dynastic marriages. Mithara's reign established a royal precedent for a woman ruling in her own name, although most provincial offices remained in male hands. Women worked throughout the agricultural, textile, trading and religious economy; their control of property and choice in marriage differed sharply between regions and social ranks.

Slavery and bonded labour supported estates, mines, roads and households. War captives could be sold far from home. Some royal projects paid skilled crews through ration and wage systems. The same worksite could contain free specialists, estate dependants, conscripted villagers and enslaved prisoners.

## The eastern heroic inheritance

Vashari singers perform histories of great houses, flawed kings, physicians, truth-tellers and wars between kin. Most surviving cycles were composed centuries after the periods they describe. They preserve family claims, local grievances and heroic memory.

The songs of **Razana Tiravan** remember a physician and negotiator of the royal house who refused the throne when it was offered to her and supported a rival household whose tenants she had treated during famine. Daryam singers pair her with Mazhara and Rathava as the three who told kings the truth and paid for it in different coin.
