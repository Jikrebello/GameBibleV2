# Age of City-States

The **Age of City-States**, approximately 1400–800 BR, is the first widely urban period after the Exodus. Walls, markets, towers, ports, granaries and public shrines concentrated people and specialised work. City governments maintained roads and water, supported professional soldiers and recorded obligations that had once depended upon household memory. These institutions protected larger populations while giving rulers new means to collect debt, labour and tribute.

The period had several regional centres. Atherian cities developed civic law, star observatories and formal roads. Jirahar tower towns served as places of teaching, caravan exchange and refuge. Arqeshite river cities joined levees, royal estates and deportation armies into an early mortal empire. Western oasis polities fortified wells and guest protection. Central-sea harbours grew through migration, intermarriage, shipbuilding and mercenary service, while Folk and Ättar settlements pursued their own histories along the southern forests and cold coasts.

## Walls change the scale

Between c. 1400 and 800 BR, cities concentrated specialists, stores and coercion. Potters no longer needed to grow all their own grain. Rulers could keep soldiers between harvests. A foreign worker could find employment without possessing local kin, although debt or captivity might be the price. Wells, sewers, warehouses, courts and shrines made urban life possible; disease, fire and class violence made it precarious.

The Atherian city world formed one part of this wider change. Jirahar tower towns preserved their own institutions. Oasis coalitions fortified water and guest-law. Harbour populations grew through migration and intermarriage. Folk and Ättar towns traded with these places on terms shaped by their own rulers, kinships and seasonal needs.

## Rival citizenships

Civic belonging could be inherited, purchased through service or denied to families resident for generations. Cities needed outsiders and feared giving them a share. Foreign workers repaired fountains which could later be used to accuse them of poisoning the water. In one harbour tradition an interpreter proved that a supposed confession described a broken pump; the discovery saved several workers but could not undo the execution already performed.

The [[G-07 - The Key Bent in a Murderer's Hand|Bent-Key cycle]] begins in bodily danger: elders sexually coerce a woman and frame her for a capital crime; fugitives steal evidence; a companion personally kills and beheads a besieging commander. The city's new right of appeal then becomes a protection that can also be invoked against the guilty people who helped to save it.

## The sea and the stolen sounding-weight

In the pre-imperial Arkenan–Saronikan corridor, c. 1120–980 BR, a sea-king attacked a populated court stronghold to obtain a sounding-weight which disclosed one safe reef adjacency. Defenders were killed. Most raiders drowned, were captured or suffered physical transformation. The few who returned helped a hungry harbour, then let later songs make that relief justify the raid.

[[G-74 - The Raid That Returned One Tool|The Raid That Returned One Tool]] survives on both sides of the crossing. Court-side descendants remember the captives whom the victorious city forgot. The weight's usefulness establishes neither ownership nor heavenly favour. This episode is separate from [[The Refusal at Kelarion]], whose storm, denied refuge and sealed Dragon belong to their own event.

## Hunger acquires new forms

City wealth made the Idols' cultural faces more elaborate. A royal image could absorb the biography of a dead champion; a creditor could turn a stored hoard into claims upon families who had never seen it. In the empty-signature marriage stories, refusal brought beating, sale or coerced sexual access. Human rulers, households and enforcers maintained these arrangements, and an existing Idol fed through their conduct.

## What lasts

City walls were repeatedly dismantled into younger houses. Founding myths outlived the languages of their first tellers. Amnesty fires, bent keys, foreign quarter feasts and compulsory silences preserve disputes which official civic genealogies conceal.

Cities continued to develop after 800 BR. In the west, the [[Age of Stars]] names an Atherian synthesis of civic and religious traditions. Eastern powers followed separate courses: the Arqeshite river empire reached its height and destroyed the First Qiryath in 742 BR, during the same centuries in which Caleran enlarged its compact.
