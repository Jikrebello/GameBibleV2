# Age of City-States

The Age of City-States is the first recognizably urban age after the Exodus. Walls, markets, towers, ports, archives, roads, and siegecraft become the grammar of power.

## What Changes

People no longer organize only around hearth, clan, and wandering memory. They begin organizing around:

- walls,
- charters,
- courts,
- markets,
- temples,
- archives,
- docks,
- roads,
- granaries,
- war colleges,
- tolls,
- public rituals.

## Regional Expressions

- **Atherian cities:** develop civic law, star observatories, formal roads, public records, legions, and imperial rivalry.
- **Harbours later connected to Sestara:** grow through migration, intermarriage, shipbuilding, exchange and mercenary service. Neither a single Sestaran people nor the later Concord yet exists.
- **Jirahar tower towns:** preserve law and prophecy while functioning as caravan courts, memory fortresses, and negotiable sanctuaries.
- **Arqeshite river cities:** join levees, royal estates, deportation armies, and water control into an early mortal empire. Its destruction of First Qiryath proves that humans can reproduce Giant methods without Giant blood.
- **Ahl-e-Ahd oasis polities:** turn wells, guest-law, caravan security, and oath enforcement into durable desert authority.
- **Southern frontier forts:** separate Folk clans, border traders and early Atherian settlers begin long patterns of forest war and winter defence. Eclessian identity belongs to a much later Braided history.

## Magic and Engineering

This is the age when practical elemental scripting begins to move from craft-secret to institution. See [[Origins of Warlock Rune Magic]].

## Inherited Wound

Cities preserve memory better than camps, but they also create class, debt, law courts, slave markets, and hunger for empire.

Coastal traditions also remember an unnamed harbor drowned after its rulers refused storm-driven ships. Surviving public accounts disagree on whether this was punishment, weather, or political allegory. The complete history remains outside public canon.

## Atlas narrative

The following account distinguishes established history from the received stories named within it.

## Walls change the scale

Between c. 1400 and 800 BR, cities concentrated specialists, stores and coercion. Potters no longer needed to grow all their own grain. Rulers could keep soldiers between harvests. A foreign worker could find employment without possessing local kin, although debt or captivity might be the price. Wells, sewers, warehouses, courts and shrines made urban life possible; disease, fire and class violence made it precarious.

The Atherian city world was only one part of the change. Jirahar tower towns preserved their own institutions. Oasis coalitions fortified water and guest-law. Harbour populations grew through migration and intermarriage into the ancestors of later Sestaran communities. Folk and Ättar towns traded with these places without becoming incomplete Atherians.

## Rival citizenships

Civic belonging could be inherited, purchased through service or denied to families resident for generations. Cities needed outsiders and feared giving them a share. Foreign workers repaired fountains which could later be used to accuse them of poisoning the water. In one harbour tradition an interpreter proved that a supposed confession described a broken pump; the discovery saved several workers but could not undo the execution already performed.

The [[G-07 - The Key Bent in a Murderer's Hand|Bent-Key cycle]] begins in bodily danger: elders sexually coerce a woman and frame her for a capital crime; fugitives steal evidence; a companion personally kills and beheads a besieging commander. The city that survives must decide whether its new right of appeal can be used against the people who saved it. It is not a fable about a flawlessly neutral procedure.

## The sea and the stolen sounding-weight

In the pre-imperial Arkenan–Saronikan corridor, c. 1120–980 BR, a sea-king attacked a populated court stronghold to obtain a sounding-weight which disclosed one safe reef adjacency. Defenders were killed. Most raiders drowned, were captured or suffered physical transformation. The few who returned helped a hungry harbour, then let later songs make that relief justify the raid.

[[G-74 - The Raid That Returned One Tool|The Raid That Returned One Tool]] survives on both sides of the crossing. Court-side descendants remember the captives whom the victorious city forgot. The weight's usefulness establishes neither ownership nor heavenly favour. This episode is separate from [[The Refusal at Kelarion]], whose storm, denied refuge and sealed Dragon belong to their own event.

## Hunger acquires new forms

City wealth made the Idols' cultural faces more elaborate. A royal image could absorb the biography of a dead champion; a creditor could turn a stored hoard into claims upon families who had never seen it. In the empty-signature marriage stories, refusal meant beating, sale or coerced sexual access rather than merely losing an abstract right. These arrangements required human rulers, households and enforcers. The existing Idol fed through them; humanity did not create it.

## What lasts

City walls were repeatedly dismantled into younger houses. Founding myths outlived the languages of their first tellers. Amnesty fires, bent keys, foreign quarter feasts and compulsory silences preserve disputes which official civic genealogies conceal.

The period's end is not the end of cities. The [[Age of Stars]] names a later Atherian synthesis. Eastern powers, including the Arqeshite river world, continued their own rise and destruction; the sack of the First Qiryath in 742 BR belongs to that plural history, not to an Atherian universal calendar of progress.
