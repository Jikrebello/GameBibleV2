# Southern Settlement and Maritime Atheria

The earliest Atherian societies developed among southern leagues leaving the region of the Glass refuge and settling in inhabited country. Between about 2130 and 1400 BR, their descendants formed river forts, maritime households, colonies and trading communities. These societies supplied much of the population, craft and political experience from which the later Atherian city-states grew.

## Leaving the common camps, c. 2130–2050 BR

The Prophet's death left several offices with responsibility for parts of the Glass People. Commanders could organise defence, provisioners distribute stores and teachers preserve testimony. Disagreement arose when decisions about movement and permanent settlement required cooperation among those groups. A leader who could assemble an expedition could also take supplies needed by households remaining behind.

The southern departure developed over generations. Captains sought defensible crossings; craftsmen wanted workshops and customers; younger households sought fields they could pass to children. Others remained near the Glass, where familiar routes, graves and camp institutions supported a different life. Relatives continued to marry and trade across the growing separation. Their loyalties changed through repeated journeys and local commitments.

Those moving south brought shared measures, apprenticeship practices and experience of collective provisioning. Their religious inheritance included the Prophet's liberation and the years in which the Lord sustained the refuge. No surviving office inherited the Prophet's commission. Later claims that conquest fulfilled that commission grew within the settler societies themselves.

## Forts in occupied country, c. 2050–1900 BR

The southern leagues encountered settlements descended from people who had survived Giant rule locally. River villages maintained fields and boats; hill towns controlled workshops and routes; separate Folk clans occupied forests and farmland. Some inhabitants had preserved considerable knowledge of old works. Others had abandoned sites they could no longer supply or defend.

Settler forts acquired food through different arrangements. A household might provide grain in exchange for protection against a rival, then find that the garrison demanded the same quantity after a poor harvest. Captains seized land, enslaved inhabitants and took children as hostages. Their opponents destroyed some forts, absorbed survivors or used incoming fighters in local quarrels. Marriages and adoption brought newcomers into established households alongside these more violent forms of incorporation.

At the Aurin mouth, defended landings placed shipping households in contact with the [[Peoples of the Ancient Aurin|Enathi, Serathi and Vardeni]]. These peoples had developed after the collapse of [[Arsuvan - The Giant Kingdom of the Aurin|Arsuvan]]. Enathi river knowledge, Serathi craft and upland supplies helped support the newcomers. The same relationships became sources of resentment when temporary military requirements turned into permanent ownership.

The traditions of [[01 Canon Reference/History/80 Myths and Legendary Histories/G-57 - The Gate Opened during Supper.md|the First Southern Gate]] and [[01 Canon Reference/History/80 Myths and Legendary Histories/G-72 - The Hostage Children of Oak and Glass.md|the Oak-and-Glass hostages]] belong to this wider settlement world. Their different places and participants preserve particular encounters rather than one common founding event.

## A ship-owning household

By about 1900 BR, maritime households joined the resources needed to build, provision and crew vessels. A prominent family might own a hull while relatives supplied timber, preserved food or sailcloth. Its dependants included hired workers, debtors, captives and people connected through marriage. Leadership required maintaining these relationships between voyages as well as commanding at sea.

A successful voyage could support a feast, repair an old vessel and finance another expedition. Failure could leave the household without seed or food for the coming season. Crews therefore watched how a captain divided the proceeds. Experienced pilots could bargain for better terms or take their knowledge elsewhere; enslaved rowers had far fewer choices. Families ashore often bore losses before investors accepted a reduced return.

Shipbuilding encouraged the movement of craftspeople. Carpenters learned how different woods behaved in wet conditions. Rope-makers, smiths and sail workers adapted techniques to materials available in a new harbour. Apprentices carried songs, games and food preferences with them. A port's maritime character developed in its yards and households as much as in the voyages commemorated by its rulers.

## Trade, colonisation and armed followings

Vessels carried grain, salt, timber, metal, pottery and enslaved people. A captain might enter one harbour as a trader, threaten another and settle relatives in a third. Sea-kings relied on shore communities for repair timber and winter food. Their following could disperse when they failed to provide either.

Harbour houses used these needs to negotiate. They required marriage, payment or hostages before allowing a powerful fleet to remain. Some acquired protection; others discovered that an invited force had become an occupying one. The later Braided harbour tradition of [[01 Canon Reference/History/80 Myths and Legendary Histories/G-58 - The Harbour Founded Twice.md|the Harbour Founded Twice]] preserves the difficult relationship between inherited conquest and a genuinely shared community.

The [[01 Canon Reference/History/80 Myths and Legendary Histories/G-73 - The Oar-King without a Country.md|Oar-King cycle]] tells of a man rising from slavery through the rescue of abandoned rowers, then murdering a companion after gaining power. It remains a distinct tradition within this maritime world. Its events do not belong to Avren's later royal household or to every successful fleet commander.

## Connected production and unequal recovery

Furnace leagues linked ore basins to charcoal districts and harbours. Craft knowledge inherited from Giant workshops became useful where households could obtain fuel, food and transport. Bronze, iron, stone tools and salvaged equipment coexisted. Communities maintained what they could repair with the labour and materials available to them.

The [[01 Canon Reference/History/80 Myths and Legendary Histories/Additional Mortal Lives and Frontier Traditions.md#^atlas-tf-06|Furnace League tradition]] remembers pooled production sustained partly by captive labour. A ruler diverted food towards an expansion fleet; workers resisted the levy, and two towns were lost in reprisal. The broader industrial networks also suffered commercial failures when coastal buyers stopped accepting ore. Escaping workers carried techniques elsewhere, while abandoned furnaces marked the places where exchange had broken down.

Aurin producers participated through river and pack routes. Nerava transferred goods between boats and animals; Thesra supplied craft and learning; Olveth supplied metal and pack stock. Their traffic towards the north-western coast was distinct from the island networks of the eastern Thalassian. Intermediaries, overland approaches and longer coastal journeys connected these regions; the Aurin did not empty into the inner sea.

## Religion among travelling households

Religious change accompanied settlement and exchange. Families preserved the Prophet's story while giving increased attention to powers associated with particular voyages, harvests or victories. In some recitals the Lord became a distant creator. Local offerings and borrowed rites occupied more of public life, while singers recast the Prophet as a heroic liberator favoured by the stars.

Inland teachers contributed observations and sacred explanations inherited from the Giant period. Maritime tales supplied wandering divine figures, monstrous encounters and rivalries. These interpretations circulated for centuries before the royal [[The Common Sky and the Formation of Astratheon|Common Sky settlement]] at Caleran organised a shared public cult. Other cities made their own accommodations.

## From maritime households to cities

Between about 1600 and 1400 BR, several exchange networks weakened through warfare, extraction and broken supply. A defeated fleet could leave distant workshops unpaid and demand grain from a harbour already short of food. Some landings were abandoned; others survived by replacing patrons or broadening cooperation among resident families.

Settlements with dependable water, cultivated hinterlands and useful anchorages could sustain larger populations. Households invested in quays, store buildings, walls and public spaces which outlasted individual voyages. Councils acquired responsibilities for work that one family could no longer undertake alone. These developments prepared the urban world described in [[The First Atherian City-States]].
