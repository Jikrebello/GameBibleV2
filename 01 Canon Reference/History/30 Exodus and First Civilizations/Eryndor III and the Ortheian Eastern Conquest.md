# Eryndor III and the Ortheian Eastern Conquest

**Eryndor III of Ortheia**, commonly called the **Far-Road King**, was an upland Atherian ruler whose campaigns transformed the eastern world between 706 and 684 BR. He came to the throne when his father's soldiers killed his father at their pay table. He left those soldiers at home, crossed the eastern inner sea with newer men, fed his army on grain borrowed from a dead woman and joined Arezan Tiravan against the Arqeshite River Empire. He held its capital until its gilded images began calling him by his camp name, marched east to be rid of them, and lost the road behind him to a wood. He died in 684 BR aboard a ship in the roads of Nemeru, waiting for a wind to take him home, in the middle of a Qahuri funerary rite meant to keep him. For nearly thirty years afterwards a voice in his tomb at Delkos claimed to be the living king, and the rivals who divided his conquests fought partly over what it promised them.

The failure of Eryndor's dominion taught later Caleran writers that conquest and government were separate achievements.

## Ortheia and Valeron II

Ortheia lay in the colder mountain country between the older Atherian city world and the lands later associated with Thalmyria. Its valleys produced grain and fruit; its pastures supported horses and pack animals; its forests and mines supplied timber, iron and silver. Ortheian speech, marriage and sanctuary practice belonged to the broad Atherian world, although the kingdom retained its own royal houses, upland assemblies and military traditions.

Valeron II inherited a crown threatened by neighbouring valleys and stronger cities. He drew the sons and daughters of subordinate houses into his household, maintained a permanent infantry core around the seasonal levies and employed engineers and clerks trained in the city world. When harvest contributions failed, the silver of the upland mines paid the soldiers. Around 730 BR his smiths began cutting that silver into weighed pieces stamped with the royal mark, so that a company's pay could be counted into soldiers' hands at a camp table. These were the first struck coins of the known world. Marriage, bribery, hostage education and war extended Valeron's influence into the twelve-city Celestial Compact between about 735 and 706 BR.

His war had a grievance to stand on. After Arqesh destroyed First Qiryath in 742 BR, Arqeshite governors on the eastern coast seized Compact ships lying in their harbours and held the crews against ransom. Between about 740 and 730 they marched the Atherian merchant quarter of **Belyra** inland to the shipyards and estates of the river cities. Wives and parents of the taken crews petitioned at Compact festivals for a generation. Valeron used their grievance to win the cities' mandate for a common eastern war.

The cities retained their councils and festivals under this arrangement. They supplied money, ships, grain and soldiers for the campaign, much of the money struck as Ortheian silver. Some joined because Valeron restrained their local wars and promised protection from Arqesh. Others paid because Ortheian troops stood outside their walls. Caleran tried to preserve the authority of its assembly while meeting the king's demands.

## The pay table at Pelatha, 706 BR

The silver that paid the army also killed its maker. In 707 BR the council of Marenthe withheld its contribution, arguing that Valeron's levy exceeded what the cities had voted for the eastern war. That winter the pay table at the muster camp of **Pelatha** came up short. Soldiers who had been promised their count in coin watched the clerks stop counting. Someone shouted that the king was keeping back their silver, and in the press around the table men of Valeron's own standing infantry killed him.

Who began the shouting was never settled. Ortheian songs blame Marenthan agents in the camp. Caleran writers say that a king who paid in promises was killed by the first soldiers able to count. Marenthan lists say the army had been robbing the cities for years and turned at last on the man who taught it how. Ortheian soldiers kept a saying from that night for centuries: *count it in front of us.*

Eryndor secured the crown against adult relatives and commanders who had served his father. He hanged eleven men at the pay table and paid the rest what the mints held. Why he kept the war is the oldest argument about him. Ortheian songs say he went east to bring home the deported families of Belyra. Caleran writers say an army that had already killed one king over its pay would otherwise have chosen another. Marenthan lists say the war was made so that the cities would go on paying. Vashari stories say Arezan invited him.

## The companies that stayed, and Marenthe, 705–698 BR

The king and the companies that had stood round the table could not trust each other after the hangings. The companies chose a speaker, **Demeran Kavos**, a file-leader who could read a pay roll. Marenthan lists place him at the table at Pelatha; Ortheian records put him on the mine road that night. Through him they made their terms. The companies would hold the uplands, the home garrisons and the mints. The season's arrears still owed them were written against Marenthe, and they were to collect from the city what its council had withheld. The king would take the war east without them.

In 705 BR the companies camped under Marenthe's walls. The council paid a first instalment in silver and temple plate and promised the rest by years. Eryndor left the royal seal with an elder half-brother, a son of Valeron's first marriage whom the assemblies had passed over, and the garrisons with Demeran. The two watched each other for eighteen years, and Ortheia stayed quiet.

Marenthe paid for six years. In 699 BR word came from the east that the king had been three summers on the coast without taking Belyra, and the council wagered that he would never come home. It stopped paying. The companies blockaded the city through the winter, and no neighbour sent relief. In 698 the council opened its gates and offered its treasury, and the treasury was short. Ortheian officers said the council had shipped its silver to Caleran before the gates closed; Caleran never answered the charge.

The soldiers took the difference from the city. They killed defenders and civilians, burned the town and sold the survivors at the camp market, where clerks wrote each captive's price against a soldier's arrears. Its fields went to the companies that had stood around Valeron's pay table. A letter from the king, written in camp on the eastern coast and read out over the ashes, confirmed them in the land. Exiles carried the city's cult objects, workshop skills and grievance to Caleran and the coast. Their lists say the king paid his father's killers with Marenthe's children and did it by letter.

The sack kept the league paying through a long war. Cities later celebrated the eastern war as liberation from Arqesh while omitting the Atherian city sold to pay for it. Marenthan descendants preserved lists of the dead and sold, and refused to honour Eryndor at festivals established after his victories.

After Eryndor's death Caleran bought ships from his unpaid commanders, landed its own sailors on Marenthe's fields, drove off the Ortheian grantees and seated the settlement in the Compact as Marenthe (c. 680–672 BR). The exiles' lists did not win the seat back.

## Crossing the eastern sea

Eryndor sailed in 702 BR with the horse of the great houses, new foot raised from the valley levies and sworn to him alone, Compact contingents, siege engineers and an allied fleet under **Serevan of Delkos**. The companies of Pelatha watched him go from the uplands. The cities had accepted the war mandate under different degrees of pressure. Their contribution lists remained a source of argument for generations because they showed which councils gave willingly, which delayed and which supplied troops only after royal seizure of their grain.

The army defeated Arqeshite field forces along the western approaches. Coastal cities then made separate choices. Some opened their harbours; others changed sides after neighbouring defeats; several fought because Arqeshite hostages or local rivalries gave them no safe neutrality. The war went slowly. The city levies went home at every harvest, the new foot had never stood a siege, and the king spent five years doing on the coast what his father's companies might have done in two.

## Belyra, 697–692 BR

Belyra, the harbour from which the merchant quarter had been marched away, was the city the war had been voted for. It barred Eryndor from its offshore sanctuary and fleet anchorage, and he had no army that could storm a fortified island. In 697 BR he left Serevan's ships to close the harbour and marched south to find grain. He never saw Belyra again.

The blockade lasted five years. In 692 the Arqeshite governor and the households that had grown rich on the deportations loaded the war squadron by night and ran for the river ports. To close the fairway behind them they scuttled the harbour's old hulks in its mouth, with the bonded divers and haulers of the port shut below the hatches so that nobody could tell the blockaders which way the squadron had gone. The town opened its gates the next morning.

Serevan's crews, many of them kin to sailors ransomed or lost in the seizures, came ashore looking for the families that had profited. They massacred inhabitants, enslaved survivors and took the remaining ships. Ortheian songs call the killing reprisal for the deported families and put the king at its head. Belyran survivors said that the guilty had sailed, that the crews killed the people who stayed, and that the king in whose name it was done was far up the Ubar and learned of it by letter. The sanctuary island was abandoned. Belyra supplied ships for the remaining campaign and graves for families scattered across the eastern ports.

## Qahur, the loan from the dead and the Tiravan alliance

Eryndor entered divided Qahur in 695 BR because his army required grain, river craft and guides. The Qahuri crowns responded separately. One sold him boats and guides against a share of Arqeshite plunder; another supported Arqesh; a third withheld commitment until the military result became clearer. None offered him a title, and none would open a granary to a king who had marched away from Belyra.

The grain came from the dead. At Qemaret in the lower Basin the store courts of the **House of the Returning Cargo** opened to the army. Their owner, the ship investor Khaseret, had been dead for more than four centuries and still lent through her stewards (see [[The White Measure and the Second Houses]]). She asked the old Basin price for a great loan: the right of her house's masters to prepare the borrower's body when he died, and garrisons of the borrower's own men at her store courts until the grain was repaid. Eryndor was thirty, and he laughed and pledged it. Ortheian court singers later made the loan a gift and left the pledge out. Among the Qahuri who joined his army was **Neresh of Qemaret**, an officer from the lower Basin who knew the embalming houses of his city. Qemaret tradition makes him the House's man in the camp, and he commanded the garrisons at the store courts.

In 694 BR Eryndor allied with **Arezan Tiravan**, leader of the Vashari coalition fighting Arqesh from the east. Arezan commanded highland houses, mounted troops and relationships with rebel governors and forced workers. Eryndor supplied the larger siege army, allied fleet and money. Their treaty treated them as independent kings and divided campaigning responsibilities and captured revenue between their commands.

Eryndor married **Parena Tiravan**, adult daughter of Arezan's sister Mithara. Parena acted between the two courts, received Vashari officers and Arqeshite petitioners, and protected parts of her household from rival commanders. In 691 BR, in the camp before Arqesh, she bore a daughter, **Mehrava**, and the king named the child before both armies.

Arqesh fell in 690 BR, and neither king took it. Sixteen years of succession war had emptied the granaries, and Kassur III fed the palace army by cutting the rations of the dredging gangs and deportee quarters that kept the canals. The gangs rose and opened the gates. In the Nemeru shipyards the Belyra families launched unfinished hulls and carried Eryndor's soldiers across a channel the defenders believed closed, while Arezan seized the eastern roads and water stations and provincial governors withheld boats and food. Both armies killed inhabitants and plundered districts during the capture, and both put the gangs that had opened the city back to work under guard.

## The images that changed voice

The last Ubar king, Kassur III, escaped up the eastern channel with several sons, his household guard and the portable royal images, the gilded likenesses before which subject rulers had bowed. Arqeshite courtiers attributed flattering dreams and a weight in the air at the bowing ceremonies to a presence they called the **Crowned Countenance**, and on occasion an image had answered in the reigning king's voice.

In the reed country in 688 BR the images began to answer in another voice, with an upland accent. Their hereditary bearers heard Eryndor speaking out of Kassur's gilded mouths, decided that the images had chosen, and carried them across to the Ortheian lines by night. Kassur's guard scattered, and two of his sons were killed in the confusion. Kassur rode east with a handful of men to Arezan's outposts at Durqesh and surrendered to a man he had known as a hostage in the royal cavalry there.

Eryndor demanded him, and Arezan refused, the allies' first open quarrel. At Durqesh Kassur sealed the water books of the Gate House over to Arezan, and the canal officials of the Ubar, who had obeyed that seal longer than they had obeyed any king, served Vashar under it. Kassur lived out his life as a guarded guest in an orchard house at Kavresh and died there around 676 BR. He was buried on a Vashari hillside with no likeness over him.

Eryndor set the images in his audience hall at Arqesh, where they went on answering in his voice. Petitioners spoke of a weight in the air when they bowed and of dreams in which the new king stood taller than his statues. Eryndor retained Arqeshite secretaries and court dress and accepted eastern officers.

Within a year the images turned on his own people. Eryndor asked no Ortheian to bow; his eastern subjects made the bow as they always had. One morning an officer of the new foot came into the hall to report, standing, as upland soldiers did, and the gilded mouths told him in the king's own accent to get down. The king sat beneath them and said nothing. The officer answered that he took his orders from the mouth that ate. The keepers of the High Image, whose ceremonies Eryndor had retained with the palace, held that a word refused to the images was a word refused to the king. They tried the officer under the old court law and executed him as they would have executed an Arqeshite governor, and the king let them.

Some weeks later a companion who had ridden with the king since boyhood stood before the images, answered them in the king's own voice, upland accent and all, and asked which of the two of them had said *down*. The hereditary bearers strangled him in front of the likenesses with the cords of the carrying poles. Ortheian songs say the king gave no order. The bearers' descendants at Delkos said that he nodded. He had hanged eleven men for his father, and he hanged no one for his friend.

From that day the images stopped answering in the king's voice and spoke in the dead man's. Before governors and petitioners they called the king **Colt**, the name his companions had used in the horse lines, and asked him to count it in front of them. Eryndor could not break them. Arqeshite officials obeyed the faces, and half his authority in the east stood on their poles. Arqeshite courtiers said that the Countenance had tasted a king's shame and liked it better than his praise.

## The farther roads and the closed wood

In 687 BR Eryndor left Arqesh. He gave out that fortresses and mobile opponents still held for House Ubar in the east, and he left the images in the hall with their bearers. His companions said that a king can kill a man and cannot kill a voice. Arezan let him through the highlands and counted his men at every pass. Hostage marriages, new garrisons, executions and burned villages accompanied the advance. The mixed court drew in Arqeshite officials, Jirahar interpreters, Vashari officers, Qahuri specialists and eastern nobles. Veterans expected the captured treasury and provincial commands as their reward. In the Ruzhan cliffs, on the road east through Arezan's highlands, Ortheian grooms took eggs from Sky-Lion nests to raise war-mounts for the king. A pride followed the army for two seasons, stooping on the pack trains at every pass and killing drovers and handlers, until Vashari herders left the eggs on a ledge for it.

Beyond the highlands the road ran into wooded country. In 687 BR the army came to a grove of Root-Minds that spoke through a village interpreter and offered passage on its own terms: the terraces the villages had cut along the valley road were to go back to the wood. Eryndor would not give up ground he had just conquered. He had the speaking trees felled, cut a military road through the grove and marched on.

Within days roots crossed the cut. Felled trunks put out shoots, cart spokes came into leaf, the palisades of the rear camps split along the grain, and the road behind the army closed. Scouts sent back found a different valley every morning. In 686 Eryndor turned and cut his way out. He left behind a stockade of prisoners, the villages that had supplied him and the garrisons he had placed farther east, and the wood closed behind his rear guard. The garrisons beyond it went on writing to him. Their dispatches, carried the long way round by traders, reported musters and asked for their pay for forty years.

Eryndor took a wound in the thigh during the cutting, from an axe or a spear; the accounts differ. It would not close. On the road back a Vashari guide brought him to a seep in a lightless gorge whose water was said to mend flesh. The wound closed while he stayed. The woman who kept the spring told him that the water mended only those who stayed beside it. He stayed through the spring and summer of 685, and Parena came up from Arqesh to join him there, bringing Mehrava from her grandmother's house at Kavresh. It was the only season the three spent under one roof. A Jirahar guide who went down the gorge to find where the water rose never came back. At the end of the summer couriers found the king with news of provinces dividing behind him, and he left. The wound opened again on the road.

## Death and inheritance

Eryndor came back to Arqesh in the autumn of 685 a sick man, and the images greeted him by his camp name. There he sent for the masters of the [[The White Measure and the Second Houses|White Measure]], the Qahuri discipline that promised a disciplined founder a household beyond death. Neresh of Qemaret brought them up from the Basin under the pledge of 695, and **Serevan of Delkos**, commander of the allied fleet, paid for the gold, salts and resins of the regimen. A founder normally prepared for years. Eryndor had months. Road-house tellers say he chose a deathlessness he could carry over one he would have had to stay beside.

In the spring of 684 he decided to go home. He meant to sail for Ortheia with the descendants of the Belyra families from the Nemeru shipyards, keeping at last the promise his songs had made, and to settle accounts with the companies he had left behind. He went down the river to Nemeru and took Kassur's images with him, since whoever held them held the bow of the east. He boarded the flagship of the allied fleet, which the Belyra families had built and now crewed, to wait for a west wind. It did not come for eleven days. He died aboard in the roads of Nemeru, and the masters performed the final rite in the ship's hold.

What killed him was argued at once. Ortheian officers blamed the mineral draughts of the regimen; Vashari stories blamed the grove, whose pale threads, they said, were growing in the wound; camp rumour named fever, bad water and commanders who wanted the treasury.

The commanders were ashore at Nemeru, quarrelling over that treasury, when the wind came round on the twelfth night. The Belyra crews cut the flagship's cables and sailed with the body, the images and the masters aboard, and the transports carrying their families followed. Their grandparents had been marched inland in ropes, and with the king dead they expected the yards again. A king's funeral ship was the one hull no harbour would chain out. They steered for Delkos, the fleet's home port. Serevan came after them in a dispatch galley and found his own harbour holding a dead king, his regalia and several thousand people who would not give him up. Ortheian court singers later said that Serevan stole the king. The Belyra families said that he had promised to bring them home, and that they had brought him.

Nobody inherited. In Ortheia the upland assembly acclaimed the half-brother who had held the seal for eighteen years, while Demeran Kavos kept the companies, the home army and the mints. In the east the commanders recognised no one. Arezan secured Vashar, the eastern roads and much of the Ubar basin. Serevan held islands, ships, eastern harbours and the body. Neresh held the Qahuri store courts and crown towns where the loan's garrisons stood, as surety, he said, for a debt the king had died owing.

Within months veterans admitted to the tomb-hall at Delkos came out saying Eryndor had spoken to them from behind the curtain. The voice used his accent, called old soldiers by their camp names and declared that the king had not died and had no heir. A living king made the man acclaimed in Ortheia a usurper. Demeran had him killed in the king's name in 683, and denounced the voice as a Delkosi puppet a season later, when it promised the upland mines to another captain. Parena rode up the Dawn Road between the relay stations to her daughter at Kavresh. Delkos said the king had no heir. Ortheian commanders said that a girl born in a siege camp to a Vashari wife had never been shown to an upland assembly; Vashari households answered that the king had named her before two armies. Arezan kept mother and daughter at Kavresh, beyond Demeran's reach, and the agents Demeran sent after them were hanged in the gate at Durqesh.

The voice promised the same provinces to rival captains, and the [[Wars of the Inheritance]] ran on for a generation. Around 657 BR the Qemaret masters testified that their rite had failed in the ship's hold. Serevan's grandson walled the body and the images into the tomb, and the living successors settled their borders by about 650 BR. Three island harbours later showed a walled house and called it his.

Caleran recovered room to act as Demeran lost the old hegemony. It received exiles, sailors paid in sealed promises instead of silver, and officers from the divided armies. The ensuing Gateway Wars transformed this recovered city into a sustained overseas republic. Vashar became an imperial peer under House Tiravan, whose later kings descended from Mehrava and called themselves heirs of the Far Road. Delkosi naval culture passed through later officer houses into the kingdom of Sarqen.

## The remembered king

Ortheian royal songs present Eryndor as the young king who inherited his father's unfinished war and went to bring home the families of Belyra. Qahuri tales dwell upon the loan from the dead, his failure to escape death and the embalmers who failed him. Arqeshite laments name cities and households destroyed by the campaign. Vashari stories give greater honour to Arezan, Mithara, Parena and Mehrava. Delkosi veterans' songs keep the voice in the tomb as the king himself. Jirahar travellers give the healing spring to the woman who kept it and would not leave.

These traditions share a ruler of courage, curiosity, generosity and extreme appetite. He crossed boundaries with his soldiers and learned from conquered courts. He also treated cities, companions and labourers as instruments when they obstructed his desired horizon. The unusual incidents of his eastern journey have separate historical settings; their later union into one marvellous voyage belongs to [[The Five Limits of Eryndor]].
