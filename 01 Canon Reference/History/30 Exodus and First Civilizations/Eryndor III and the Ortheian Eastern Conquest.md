# Eryndor III and the Ortheian Eastern Conquest

**Eryndor III of Ortheia**, commonly called the **Far-Road King**, was an upland Atherian ruler whose campaigns transformed the eastern world between 706 and 684 BR. He came to the throne when his father's soldiers killed his father at their pay table, and he paid them with the sack of Olyssar. He crossed the eastern inner sea, joined Arezan Tiravan against the Arqeshite River Empire and carried his army beyond the conquered capital until a wood closed the road behind it. He died in 684 BR aboard a ship in the roads of Nemeru, waiting for a wind to take him home, in the middle of a Qahuri funerary rite meant to keep him. For nearly thirty years afterwards a voice in his tomb at Delkos claimed to be the living king, and the rivals who divided his conquests fought partly over what it promised them.

The failure of Eryndor's dominion taught later Caleran writers that conquest and government were separate achievements.

## Ortheia and Valeron II

Ortheia lay in the colder mountain country between the older Atherian city world and the lands later associated with Thalmyria. Its valleys produced grain and fruit; its pastures supported horses and pack animals; its forests and mines supplied timber, iron and silver. Ortheian speech, marriage and sanctuary practice belonged to the broad Atherian world, although the kingdom retained its own royal houses, upland assemblies and military traditions.

Valeron II inherited a crown threatened by neighbouring valleys and stronger cities. He drew the sons and daughters of subordinate houses into his household, maintained a permanent infantry core around the seasonal levies and employed engineers and clerks trained in the city world. When harvest contributions failed, the silver of the upland mines paid the soldiers. Around 730 BR his smiths began cutting that silver into weighed pieces stamped with the royal mark, so that a company's pay could be counted into soldiers' hands at a camp table. These were the first struck coins of the known world. Marriage, bribery, hostage education and war extended Valeron's influence into the twelve-city Celestial Compact between about 735 and 706 BR.

His war had a grievance to stand on. After Arqesh destroyed First Qiryath in 742 BR, Arqeshite governors on the eastern coast seized Compact ships lying in their harbours and held the crews against ransom. Between about 740 and 730 they marched the Atherian merchant quarter of **Terys** inland to the shipyards and estates of the river cities. Wives and parents of the taken crews petitioned at Compact festivals for a generation. Valeron used their grievance to win the cities' mandate for a common eastern war.

The cities retained their councils and festivals under this arrangement. They supplied money, ships, grain and soldiers for the campaign, much of the money struck as Ortheian silver. Some joined because Valeron restrained their local wars and promised protection from Arqesh. Others paid because Ortheian troops stood outside their walls. Caleran tried to preserve the authority of its assembly while meeting the king's demands.

## The pay table at Pelatha, 706 BR

The silver that paid the army also killed its maker. In 707 BR the council of Olyssar withheld its contribution, arguing that Valeron's levy exceeded what the cities had voted for the eastern war. That winter the pay table at the muster camp of **Pelatha** came up short. Soldiers who had been promised their count in coin watched the clerks stop counting. Someone shouted that the king was keeping back their silver, and in the press around the table men of Valeron's own standing infantry killed him.

Who began the shouting was never settled. Ortheian songs blame Olyssaran agents in the camp. Caleran writers say that a king who paid in promises was killed by the first soldiers able to count. Olyssaran lists say the army had been robbing the cities for years and turned at last on the man who taught it how. Ortheian soldiers kept a saying from that night for centuries: *count it in front of us.*

Eryndor secured the crown against adult relatives and commanders who had served his father. He hanged eleven men at the pay table and paid the rest. Why he kept the war is the oldest argument about him. Ortheian songs say he went east to bring home the deported families of Terys. Caleran writers say an army that had already killed one king over its pay would otherwise have chosen another. Olyssaran lists say the war was made so that the cities would go on paying. Vashari stories say Arezan invited him.

## Olyssar, 705–704 BR

Eryndor owed the army a season's arrears and had no silver to pay them, so he gave the companies Olyssar. The army marched on the city in 705 BR to collect what its council had withheld. Neighbouring cities sent no relief. After a winter's blockade the council opened its gates and offered its treasury, and the treasury was short. Ortheian officers said the council had shipped its silver to Caleran before the gates closed; Caleran never answered the charge.

The soldiers took the difference from the city. They killed defenders and civilians, burned the town in 704 and sold the survivors at the camp market, where clerks wrote each captive's price against a soldier's arrears. Its fields went to the infantry companies, the same companies that had stood around Valeron's pay table. Exiles carried the city's cult objects, workshop skills and grievance to Caleran and the coast. Their lists say the king paid his father's killers with Olyssar's children.

The sack settled the army and frightened the league. Cities later celebrated the eastern war as liberation from Arqesh while omitting the Atherian city sold to pay for it. Olyssaran descendants preserved lists of the dead and sold, and refused to honour Eryndor at festivals established after his victories.

Eryndor left **Demeran Kavos**, a senior commander of his father's, in the uplands as regent, with the settled companies, the home garrisons and the mints. The arrangement kept Ortheia quiet for twenty years and made Demeran master of the west.

After Eryndor's death Caleran bought ships from his unpaid commanders, landed its own sailors on Olyssar's fields, drove off the Ortheian grantees and seated the settlement in the Compact as Olyssar (c. 680–672 BR). The exiles' lists did not win the seat back.

## Crossing the eastern sea

Eryndor sailed in 702 BR with Ortheian infantry and cavalry, Compact contingents, siege engineers and allied ships. The cities had accepted the war mandate under different degrees of pressure. Their contribution lists remained a source of argument for generations because they showed which councils gave willingly, which delayed and which supplied troops only after royal seizure of their grain.

The army defeated Arqeshite field forces along the western approaches. Coastal cities then made separate choices. Some opened their harbours; others changed sides after neighbouring defeats; several fought because Arqeshite hostages or local rivalries gave them no safe neutrality.

## The spring under the sea at Terys, 697–695 BR

Terys, the harbour from which the merchant quarter had been marched away, barred Eryndor from its offshore sanctuary and fleet anchorage. The sanctuary island had no well of its own. Its water rose sweet from the floor of the channel, and Terysian divers drew it up through a bronze bell and a leather hose sealed with pitch, as their families had done for generations.

Eryndor's engineers spent two summers hunting for the spring. A diver from the mainland town sold them its place for his family's freedom. The engineers sank hulks full of stones over the mouth, and when the islanders cleared them, they sent captured Terysian divers down in a bell of their own to cut the hose. Several drowned when something in the deep water struck the bell. The survivors would not say what they had seen.

The island drank its cisterns through one summer and asked for terms in the next. Ortheian officers came ashore looking for the households that had profited from the deportations, and the soldiers massacred inhabitants, enslaved survivors and took the fleet. Ortheian songs call the killing reprisal for the deported families. Terysian survivors said that the households which had profited from the seizures sailed away before the cisterns failed, and that the soldiers killed the people who stayed. When the stones were hauled off the spring, it came up salt, and it has never run sweet again. The sanctuary island was abandoned. Terys supplied ships for the remaining campaign and graves for families scattered across the eastern ports.

## Qahur, the loan from the dead and the Tiravan alliance

Eryndor entered divided Qahur in 695 BR because his army required grain, river craft and guides. The Qahuri crowns responded separately. One recognised him with a local royal title; another supported Arqesh; a third withheld commitment until the military result became clearer.

The grain came from the dead. At Qemaret in the lower Basin the store courts of the **House of the Returning Cargo** opened to the army. Their owner, the ship investor Khaseret, had been dead for more than four centuries and still lent through her stewards (see [[The White Measure and the Second Houses]]). She asked the old Basin price for a great loan: the right of her house's masters to prepare the borrower's body when he died. Eryndor was thirty, and he laughed and pledged it. Ortheian court singers later turned the pledge into an adoption and called him the son of a Qahuri queen. Among the Qahuri who joined his army was **Neresh of Qemaret**, an officer from the lower Basin who knew the embalming houses of his city. Qemaret tradition makes him the House's man in the camp.

In 694 BR Eryndor allied with **Arezan Tiravan**, leader of the Vashari coalition fighting Arqesh from the east. Arezan commanded highland houses, mounted troops and relationships with rebel governors and forced workers. Eryndor supplied the larger siege army, allied fleet and money. Their treaty treated them as independent kings and divided campaigning responsibilities and captured revenue between their commands.

Eryndor married **Parena Tiravan**, adult daughter of Arezan's sister Mithara. Parena acted between the two courts, received Vashari officers and Arqeshite petitioners, and protected parts of her household from rival commanders.

Arqesh fell in 690 BR through their converging campaigns and the action of people inside the empire. Nemeru crews carried troops across a closed channel. Forced workers opened gates. Provincial governors withheld boats and food. Arezan seized eastern roads and water stations while Eryndor pressed the capital with the fleet and principal siege force. Both armies killed inhabitants and plundered districts during the capture.

## The images that changed voice

The last Ubar king, Kassur III, escaped up the eastern channel with several sons, his household guard and the portable royal images, the gilded likenesses before which subject rulers had bowed. Arqeshite courtiers attributed flattering dreams and a weight in the air at the bowing ceremonies to a presence they called the **Crowned Countenance**, and on occasion an image had answered in the reigning king's voice.

In the reed country in 688 BR the images began to answer in another voice, with an upland accent. Their hereditary bearers heard Eryndor speaking out of Kassur's gilded mouths, decided that the images had chosen, and carried them across to the Ortheian lines by night. Kassur's guard scattered, and two of his sons were killed in the confusion. Kassur rode east with a handful of men to Arezan's outposts at Durqesh and surrendered to a man he had known as a hostage in the royal cavalry there.

Eryndor demanded him, and Arezan refused, the allies' first open quarrel. At Durqesh Kassur sealed the water books of the Gate House over to Arezan, and the canal officials of the Ubar, who had obeyed that seal for nearly three centuries, served Vashar under it. Kassur lived out his life as a guarded guest in an orchard house at Kavresh and died there around 676 BR. He was buried on a Vashari hillside with no likeness over him.

Eryndor set the images in his audience hall at Arqesh, where they went on answering in his voice. Petitioners spoke of a weight in the air when they bowed and of dreams in which the new king stood taller than his statues. Eryndor retained Arqeshite secretaries and court dress and accepted eastern officers.

His personal rule also carried intimate bloodshed, and the images stood at the centre of it. Eryndor required his Ortheian officers to bow before the likenesses as Arqeshite governors had done. Veterans of Valeron's army said the king wanted them to kneel twice to one man. An experienced officer who had served his father refused before the assembled army and told his men to stay on their feet; at the king's demand the army's assembly condemned him, and he was executed. Later a companion stood before the images in the audience hall and answered them in the king's own voice, mimicking the upland accent they had learned. Eryndor killed him with his own hands. The Ortheian companies then demanded that their officers be excused the bow, and Eryndor yielded; eastern subjects still made it. Arqeshite courtiers said that the Countenance had been mocked and the king had answered for it.

## The farther roads and the closed wood

Eryndor campaigned east of Arqesh against fortresses and mobile opponents who had survived the fall of the capital. Hostage marriages, new garrisons, executions and burned villages accompanied the advance. The mixed court drew in Arqeshite officials, Jirahar interpreters, Vashari officers, Qahuri specialists and eastern nobles. Veterans expected the captured treasury and provincial commands as their reward. In the Ruzhan cliffs, on the road east through Arezan's highlands, Ortheian grooms took eggs from Sky-Lion nests to raise war-mounts for the king. A pride followed the army for two seasons, stooping on the pack trains at every pass and killing drovers and handlers, until Vashari herders left the eggs on a ledge for it.

Beyond the highlands the road ran into wooded country. In 687 BR the army came to a grove of Root-Minds that spoke through a village interpreter and offered passage on its own terms: the terraces the villages had cut along the valley road were to go back to the wood. Eryndor would not give up ground he had just conquered. He had the speaking trees felled, cut a military road through the grove and marched on.

Within days roots crossed the cut. Felled trunks put out shoots, cart spokes came into leaf, the palisades of the rear camps split along the grain, and the road behind the army closed. Scouts sent back found a different valley every morning. In 686 Eryndor turned and cut his way out. He left behind a stockade of prisoners, the villages that had supplied him and the garrisons he had placed farther east, and the wood closed behind his rear guard. The garrisons beyond it ruled what they held for a generation because no one could reach them.

Eryndor took a wound in the thigh during the cutting, from an axe or a spear; the accounts differ. It would not close. On the road back a Vashari guide brought him to a seep in a lightless gorge whose water was said to mend flesh. The wound closed while he stayed. The woman who kept the spring told him that the water mended only those who stayed beside it. He stayed through the spring and summer of 685, and Parena came up from Arqesh to join him there. A Jirahar guide who went down the gorge to find where the water rose never came back. At the end of the summer couriers found the king with news of provinces dividing behind him, and he left. The wound opened again on the road.

## Death and inheritance

Eryndor came back to Arqesh in the autumn of 685 a sick man. There he sent for the masters of the [[The White Measure and the Second Houses|White Measure]], the Qahuri discipline that promised a disciplined founder a household beyond death. Neresh of Qemaret brought them up from the Basin under the pledge of 695, and **Serevan of Delkos**, commander of the allied fleet, paid for the gold, salts and resins of the regimen. A founder normally prepared for years. Eryndor had months. Road-house tellers say he chose a deathlessness he could carry over one he would have had to stay beside.

In the spring of 684 he decided to go home. He meant to sail for Ortheia with the descendants of the Terys families from the Nemeru shipyards, keeping at last the promise his songs had made, and to settle accounts with a regent grown used to ruling. He went down the river to Nemeru with Kassur's images and boarded Serevan's flagship to wait for a west wind. It did not come for eleven days. He died aboard in the roads of Nemeru, and the masters performed the final rite in the ship's hold.

What killed him was argued at once. Ortheian officers blamed the mineral draughts of the regimen; Vashari stories blamed the grove, whose pale threads, they said, were growing in the wound; camp rumour named fever, bad water and commanders who wanted the treasury. Parena, at Arqesh, was pregnant.

Ortheian court singers later said that Serevan stole the king. He had only to wait for the wind. When it came he sailed with the body, the images and the Terys families aboard, and he steered for Delkos.

The first settlement divided commands while retaining the language of one monarchy. Ortheian commanders recognised an adult half-brother of the king, who was with Demeran in the uplands, and Parena's unborn child as nominal heirs; neither controlled the army, treasury or provincial appointments. Arezan secured Vashar, the eastern roads and much of the Ubar basin. Demeran Kavos held Ortheia, the home army and the mints. Serevan held islands, ships and eastern harbours. Neresh controlled selected Qahuri crown towns through garrisons and marriage.

Within months veterans admitted to the tomb-hall at Delkos came out saying Eryndor had spoken to them from behind the curtain. The voice used his accent, called old soldiers by their camp names and declared that the king had not died and had no heir. A reigning king needed no half-brother; once Delkos proclaimed him alive, Demeran's royal hostage was worth nothing to him, and he killed him. Parena had been carried up the Dawn Road between the relay stations to her mother at Kavresh, where she bore a daughter, **Mehrava**, in the late spring of 684. Delkos said the king had fathered no child. Ortheian commanders said a king under the White Measure's fasts could have fathered none that year; Vashari households answered that Parena had been with him at the gorge before the regimen began. Arezan kept mother and daughter at Kavresh, beyond Demeran's reach.

The voice promised the same provinces to rival captains, and the [[Wars of the Inheritance]] ran on for a generation. Around 657 BR the Qemaret masters testified that their rite had failed in the ship's hold. Serevan's grandson walled the body and the images into the tomb, and the living successors settled their borders by about 650 BR. The tomb's site was later lost.

Caleran recovered room to act as Demeran lost the old hegemony. It received exiles, sailors paid in sealed promises instead of silver, and officers from the divided armies. The ensuing Gateway Wars transformed this recovered city into a sustained overseas republic. Vashar became an imperial peer under House Tiravan, whose later kings descended from Mehrava and called themselves heirs of the Far Road. Delkosi naval culture passed through later officer houses into the kingdom of Sarqen.

## The remembered king

Ortheian royal songs present Eryndor as the young king who inherited his father's unfinished war and went to bring home the families of Terys. Qahuri tales dwell upon the loan from the dead, his failure to escape death and the embalmers who failed him. Arqeshite laments name cities and households destroyed by the campaign. Vashari stories give greater honour to Arezan, Mithara, Parena and Mehrava. Delkosi veterans' songs keep the voice in the tomb as the king himself. Jirahar travellers give the healing spring to the woman who kept it and would not leave.

These traditions share a ruler of courage, curiosity, generosity and extreme appetite. He crossed boundaries with his soldiers and learned from conquered courts. He also treated cities, companions and labourers as instruments when they obstructed his desired horizon. The unusual incidents of his eastern journey have separate historical settings; their later union into one marvellous voyage belongs to [[The Five Limits of Eryndor]].
