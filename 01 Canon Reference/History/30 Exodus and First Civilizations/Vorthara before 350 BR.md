# Vorthara before 350 BR

Vorthara grew from a human refuge inside and around the Black Spire into a substantial pass town by 350 BR. The Spire stood at a mountain knot before Atherian rule, and its original builders and purpose remain unknown. Escaped slaves and other outsiders occupied accessible shelter there while Giant powers avoided close approach. Later ridge households, traders and magical specialists joined the settlement. No founding charter, single ruler or common law made it a city at one recorded moment.

The Spire's effects appear to have made approach especially dangerous to Giants and people with strongly active Giant inheritance. Ordinary humans could use parts of it. That observation does not explain the structure's purpose or make it a general source of magical power. Refugees first survived through concealment, water, small plots and warning paths. Over centuries, occupied interior spaces, buildings at the Spire's foot, the ascent market and neighbouring ridges became one lived settlement.

## From hidden refuge to pass town

The oldest known habitation was a shelter within accessible Spire spaces or immediately against them. Escaped slaves could recognise a destination avoided by their pursuers and enter it with help from people already there. They still needed food, water, places for the sick and ways to warn one another when a master sent human agents. Small refuges spread onto the ridges as the population grew. Their paths and shared stories tied them to the first shelter without making all the hamlets one governed place.

The Unbinding ended much Giant control elsewhere but did not end the need for refuge. Displaced dependants, people pursued by new rulers and workers whose skills attracted dangerous patrons continued to arrive. Some families defended escapees; others profited by selling labour or information. A household could shelter one stranger and refuse another. Long residence did not create common citizenship, and an old refuge path could later carry traders into the same inhabited area.

Across the Hearth and City-State ages, terraces, herds, repair places and exchange on the pass approaches made the settlement more than a hiding place. Accessible interiors became occupied rooms and routes, while buildings gathered against the lower exterior and at the Spire's foot. Freight, water and repeated movement between these areas made the clusters one town in practice. They did not agree on one court. A dispute could cross several defended thresholds, each with a different host and means of enforcement.

## Work and shelter

By the Age of Stars, carriers and food sellers used relatively open approaches. Specialist households held workshops and side passages; dangerous practitioners and gangs defended deeper **occupied** thresholds. The town grew medicinal and magical plants on nearby terraces, raised some food and still imported grain. Repairs, medicine, freight, lodging and services other cities restricted brought customers. A stranger's safety depended on a host, a faction or the ability to leave; there was no citywide police force.

Local gardens, ridge plots and herds made survival possible, but a larger population of clients, carriers and practitioners needed imported grain. The ascent market joined ordinary provisioning to rare services: a visitor might seek a guide, healer, instrument, reagent, discreet lodging or forbidden craft. Repair workers depended on freight; growers depended on buyers and protected water; a powerful mage still needed food carried up the road. These mutual needs encouraged limited agreements to keep a passage usable even among groups that feared one another.

Some useful plants grew naturally in the mountain environs. Other rare species were brought from elsewhere and cultivated through local horticultural skill. Their value did not prove that the Spire's Giant-selective effect produced every magical property. Knowledge of seed, exposure, soil, watering and preparation belonged to particular growers and apothecaries, who could become as valuable as a practitioner using their products.

Vorthara's reputation drew people wishing to disappear, including fugitives from slavery and practitioners avoiding particular authorities. It also drew captive traders. An outside owner's claim did not command automatic extradition inside the town, but reaching Vorthara did not guarantee freedom against a new local seizure. Refuge households and traders rescued, bought out, kidnapped and retaliated against one another in overlapping streets. Other interests intervened when such fighting obstructed trade and usable routes, not from a shared commitment to emancipation. The conflict recurred; no decisive citywide pre-350 BR settlement is established.

## Refuge, captivity and local power

The town offered no universal promise that everyone entering would be protected. A fugitive needed a household, gang, practitioner or other group willing and able to defend a door. A person who reached one refuge could be unsafe on the next public route. This made Vorthara attractive to people fleeing claims from outside while leaving them exposed to bargains or enslavement made inside. An imperial officer, creditor or former owner could try to seize someone, but had no general local institution obliged to enforce the claim and could face several armed opponents.

Captive sellers found a market for labour and for services other cities forbade. Those fleeing captivity had descendants and allies within the same town. Neither interest occupied one clean district. Rescue, purchase of freedom, abduction and reprisal could occur along the same paths used for freight. Traders and refuge households sought help from stronger practitioners according to the case, which meant a dispute over one person could become a quarrel over a market route. Other factions intervened when that threatened their own work. Such intervention kept business moving more often than it freed captives or abolished trade.

Vorthara's autonomy was therefore uneven. A group defended its own premises, and strength, reputation, payment and relationships determined whether neighbours respected a boundary. A vulnerable resident could not appeal to one city magistrate. Conversely, an outside king could not assume that threatening the pass would make every faction submit; even rival practitioners had reasons to resist a power demanding authority over all their doors.

## Death-work and cult courts

Several waves of Qahuri mortuary workers contributed preservation, funerary contracts and estate craft to Vorthara. Their migration cannot be dated to a single caravan or one known Qahuri crisis. By 350 BR a Qahuri-rooted association called the [[The Pale Hand before 350 BR|Pale Hand]] led a recognisable death-working quarter. Other specialists entered its crews. Preserved and animated bodies performed hazardous work; most ordinary working corpses held no returning human soul. Families and clients nevertheless disputed the handling of bodies and the terms under which the dead could be put to labour.

[[Ancient Qahur and the Twin-Reed Crowns|Qahur]] had its own funerary households, preservation, estate obligations and competing ideas about the dead. Small mortuary groups brought parts of those crafts to Vorthara across several migrations. Qahuri crises may have increased the number of arrivals, but none proves the date of a named Vortharan quarter. Once in the town, workers adapted instruments and body custody to pass hazards, contracts and clients who could not seek a public court elsewhere. The craft was Qahuri-rooted without making every member Qahuri or every Qahuri funerary worker a necromancer.

The Pale Hand became the leading organised association within this neighbourhood. It offered equipment, clients, paid tasks and a measure of shelter to useful death-workers from several backgrounds. Crews could prepare a body for a household, study residue, dispose of dangerous remains or direct a soulless corpse porter through hazardous work. Individual Folk practitioners and other Orphaned specialists brought different burial or decay practices; a few much older beings may have sought cover among clients. Cooperation on one task did not make their rites interchangeable. The Hand's public rule forbade binding a human soul, a distinction important to families trusting it with the dead. Neighbours could still accuse particular workers of violating that rule without every accusation proving a returning person was present in a labouring body.

A distinct adjoining area of cult courts grew from older refuge rites and paid services to travellers. Menhir-related courts, Idol houses, Egg White patron rooms and other human cults displayed incompatible claims. The existence of a house did not prove its patron existed as described or granted power. Most advertised openly while concealing particular dealings. The Pale Hand could be hired across the shared edge without governing these courts.

The cult courts acquired a recognisable location through repeated use, not through one founding temple. Refuge households met near familiar paths; later visitors needed lodging, interpreters, witnesses and brokers. Small houses could rent a room, claim a patron, gather adherents and disappear. Menhir-associated courts might offer place-bound bargains, Idol houses human devotion to predatory powers, and Egg White rooms access to particular patrons or intermediaries. A house claiming an angelic visitor was a human institution; it did not gain the right to summon or hire a loyal angel. The labels on doors could be honest, mistaken or fraudulent, and some dangerous dealings remained concealed even when a house advertised its allegiance.

Rival houses defended their own thresholds. A strong cult might take a weaker neighbour's lease or followers, yet an attempt to dominate the whole quarter gave threatened rivals a reason to cooperate for a time. This unstable deterrence was not a cult parliament or a common law. The meeting edge with the Pale Hand drew funerary clients, lodging and witnesses and could be contested by both. Neither the Hand's leadership in death-work nor one patron's power made that cult the ruler of Vorthara.

These were recognisable working precincts, not formally named wards with fixed boundaries. Markets, routes, terraces and guarded thresholds overlapped. The later Spire Truce, Shell anchors, Astral Conclave and Concord membership belong to later histories, not the town at 350 BR.

## The Atherian boundary

Around 350 BR, Atheria claimed the surrounding routes and publicly treated Vorthara as a small lawful market. A temporary gathering of households, pass keepers and influential practitioners reached a limited external understanding that left internal premises in local hands. It created neither an imperial right to seize residents inside nor a permanent Vortharan council. Later exclusive service contracts, inspection arrangements and commissioned protections developed during imperial expansion; they were not terms of this first boundary agreement.

The official description was convenient to both parties. Atheria could watch the approaches and purchase services without taking responsibility for every cult, corpse yard or captive dispute. Vortharan groups could keep their own premises while avoiding a war over land the empire valued mainly as a route and destination for expertise. The arrangement rested on a temporary ability to negotiate externally, not on a new city constitution. Atherian expansion would later produce more formal contracts and perimeter infrastructure, but those developments cannot explain how the earlier refuge had lived.
