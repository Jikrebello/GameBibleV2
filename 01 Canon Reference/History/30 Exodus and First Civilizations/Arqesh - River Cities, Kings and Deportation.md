# Arqesh — River Cities, Kings and Deportation

**Arqesh** was a canal empire of the Ubar riverlands which flourished from the tenth to the seventh centuries BR. House Ubar joined older cities through water control, royal estates, military service and the forced movement of conquered households. Its administration preserved canals, measures and learning used by later states. The same institutions enabled the empire to starve rebellious districts, enslave captives and destroy First Qiryath in 742 BR.

## Country of the Ubar

Arqesh developed in the lower basin of the **Ubar**, a broad eastern river fed by highland snow and seasonal rain. Its two principal channels crossed a flat country of silt, reeds and old lake beds before reaching the eastern sea. Floods arrived less predictably than those of Qahur. Settlements depended upon embankments, drainage cuts and canals maintained across several districts.

The river country supported barley, wheat, sesame, dates, onions, sheep and cattle. Reed boats carried grain and brick. Pack routes brought stone, timber, horses and metal from the eastern highlands. Coastal towns exchanged oil, cloth and worked goods with Atherian and Gateway ships. Irrigation permitted dense settlement while making villages vulnerable to anyone who controlled an upstream gate.

Four cities shaped the mature civilisation. **Arqesh** stood where the river divided and commanded the principal canal junction. **Nemeru** lay downstream among gardens, textile workshops and quays. **Khalbar** guarded the northern crossing towards Qahur. **Durqesh** occupied the road leading east into the highlands. Each city possessed older sacred houses and civic families before Arqesh conquered it.

The Arqeshite language and brick tradition spread along the river and the roads into neighbouring countries. Builders used its water terms, survey marks and storehouse plans long after the city that devised them had lost political control.

## The canal cities, c. 1750–1100 BR

The first Ubar cities belonged to the Bronze Roads. Their principal palaces were large royal or aristocratic households containing the ruler's family, servants, workshops, stores and administrative staff. These households commissioned canals and negotiated with village elders who supplied maintenance crews. Temple estates controlled other stores and fields. Royal families, temples, civic households and cultivating communities therefore held land under different obligations. Enslaved people and debt-bound labourers worked beside free tenants and seasonal crews.

Water required cooperation across political boundaries. A city could open a channel needed by its rival and still attack that rival after harvest. Surveyors, dredging crews and boat builders moved between courts. Marriage joined royal houses while merchants maintained partnerships through wars their rulers had begun.

**Teluq**, an older city above Durqesh, dominated the eastern approaches during the sixteenth and fifteenth centuries BR. Its decline followed the Breaking of the Bronze Roads. Highland suppliers withheld metal, palace households fought over dwindling transport and an abandoned canal changed the course of one distributary. Teluq survived as a temple and market town, but later Arqeshite kings appropriated much of its royal history.

The most famous stolen tradition concerns **Saruq of Teluq**, whose uncertain birth is placed around 1370 BR. One account calls his mother a junior royal daughter; another names an enslaved brewer; a third says only that a canal gardener found him in a pitch-sealed basket among cut reeds. Saruq entered palace service by measuring field water. He later overthrew a ruler who had transferred upstream villages, their taxes and their labour to military creditors, and joined three canal districts under one levy.

His government restored cultivation and conscripted the same villages for ambitious building. Teluq songs remember the gardener who taught him to read the river. Arqeshite royal lists later made Saruq an ancestor of House Ubar and moved his victories to Arqesh. The contradictory birth accounts remained useful because every dynasty could claim that greatness had chosen him without ordinary inheritance.

## Arqesh before empire, c. 1100–960 BR

Arqesh first prospered at the junction where boats entered either principal channel of the Ubar. Its granaries held grain awaiting shipment, and its officials recorded when each district could draw canal water, what part of a harvest it owed and which boats had paid for passage. Merchants brought disputes over measures and damaged cargo before these officials because their decisions were recognised on both channels. The city attracted refugees and craftsmen after wars among the upper cities. Its temple households accumulated land by lending grain during bad harvests and taking fields when debtors could not repay.

Political conflict centred upon the **Gate House**, whose keepers controlled the principal regulators, and the **House of the High Image**, which conducted royal ceremonies. Military captains entered both institutions through marriage. A succession struggle around 1010 BR left several neighbourhoods flooded when one claimant opened a canal to prevent his rival crossing it. The water destroyed poorer housing and deposited silt across fields for two seasons.

Farmers and merchants responded to the flooding by demanding one authority capable of coordinating the principal gates during a crisis. Claimants to the throne recognised that control of those gates could also block an enemy's boats, flood his supporters and direct food towards an army.

## House Ubar and imperial government, c. 960–800 BR

**Merod I Ubar**, a grain contractor and commander related to the Gate House through his mother, seized the throne around 958 BR. He had organised boats during two failed harvests and built a following among soldiers paid in grain allotments. Merod imprisoned the existing king, married one of his daughters and declared that the river had selected a household capable of keeping every gate.

His first campaigns secured nearby canals. Villages that submitted retained their elders and fields under a new grain tax. Settlements that cut embankments or killed royal agents lost inhabitants to deportation. Merod placed transferred families beside neglected channels where their labour enlarged the crown estate.

His grandson **Kassur I Ubar**, ruling approximately 918–879 BR, transformed these practices into imperial institutions. He divided conquered land into river provinces, appointed governors over major gates and required subject cities to provide troops, boats and craftsmen. Royal messengers travelled on maintained embankments. A ration system supported soldiers and workers at fixed stations.

Service to the crown offered limited advancement. A deported mason might be placed over a royal workshop, and a subject prince might retain his title after sending a daughter and sons to live under watch at Arqesh. Merchants benefited from guarded channels and common measures. The same government assigned conquered households to new districts for their labour and held children to secure their parents' obedience.

Kassur's armies captured Nemeru after a three-year struggle. The final assault killed defenders and civilians in the riverside district. Officers deported boatwrights, dyers and metalworkers, settled loyal troops near the quay and installed the royal image in the old civic temple. Nemeru recovered its wealth under Arqeshite rule and remained a centre of resentment.

**Merod II Ubar**, who ruled from approximately 836 to 798 BR, inherited Kassur's provincial system after two shorter reigns. His armies were already competing for the roads and cultivated approaches east of Qahur when the Single Radiance collapsed. In 814 BR, the widowed Qahuri queen Narethi asked him to send a royal son whom she could marry and raise beside the Twin-Reed throne. Merod suspected a trap, demanded a second letter and eventually sent his younger son **Ashkur Ubar** with an escort. Ashkur was killed before reaching the Sacred Basin.

No surviving account identifies the killers. Merod blamed the Qahuri crown houses collectively and used the death to justify a larger intervention. Arqeshite troops occupied road stations, captured eastern canal towns and transferred fields to military supporters between 811 and 802 BR. The marriage proposal did not begin Arqeshite expansion, which had already followed grain and caravan routes. It gave the court a dead prince around whom conquest could be presented as punishment. The killing entered both Arqeshite and Qahuri royal histories, although they assigned responsibility to different houses.

## Court, households and learning

The king ruled with royal relatives, military commanders, palace managers, scribes and temple dignitaries. Provincial governors possessed wide authority but remained vulnerable to denunciation at court. Royal women managed estates, arranged marriages, endowed temples and represented absent sons. The king's sisters frequently married subject rulers or held their children at Arqesh.

Brick palaces contained reception courts, treasuries, armouries, gardens and writing rooms. Scholars copied older river texts, astronomical observations, medical recipes and royal annals. Conquered scribes brought tablets, languages and local knowledge into the royal collections. Some works survived only in Arqeshite copies because the army that seized them had burned the school or temple where they were composed.

Most people encountered the state through grain assessment, water labour and military requisition. Canal maintenance was necessary public work. Governors could increase it until families lost their own planting season. Corvée crews received rations of grain, oil and beer; prisoners and enslaved labourers received less and suffered the highest death rates.

Household life continued beneath imperial demands. Courtyard ovens served several related families. Children played at opening miniature mud gates. Nemeru dyers held evening competitions in which each workshop produced an intentionally ugly cloth and argued that a rival had made the best one by mistake. Boat crews dedicated the first date cake of the flood season to whoever had repaired the worst leak.

## Gods, images and royal splendour

Every city maintained several sacred households. River gates, gardens, storms, ancestors, kingship and particular crafts received offerings through local traditions. Conquest rarely abolished all of them. Arqeshite rulers preferred to place the royal image above or among a city's accepted powers and require public acknowledgement.

The imperial cult called the enthroned king the **Visible Measure of the Lands**. Reliefs showed conquered peoples carrying their characteristic goods towards a ruler larger than every other person. Provincial ceremonies required leading families to bow before a portable image and speak their city's obligations.

During the later empire, these ceremonies attracted the dependent Pride Mask known as the **Crowned Countenance**. Compulsory praise and the public humiliation of subject rulers fed it. The Mask supplied flattering dreams, intensified the presence of royal ceremonies and occasionally made an image answer in the reigning king's voice. The dead kings remained separate from these manifestations.

Several rulers exploited these responses. Others feared them and continued the ceremonies because provincial obedience depended upon the imperial cult. Elsewhere in the riverlands, similar royal or river images belonged to local Menhir relationships, ordinary natural phenomena, frauds or other Idol Masks. Their shared artistic forms reflected Arqeshite culture rather than a single power behind every cult.

## Expansion towards Qahur and Qiryath, c. 820–746 BR

Arqeshite armies reached the eastern approaches of Qahur during a period of divided crowns. Their aim was to secure grain, caravan stages and the road between Khalbar and the Basin. Merod II's campaign after Ashkur's death enlarged an existing military presence. A royal commander repaired one neglected canal and established garrisons beside it. When a local rising killed tax agents, the army executed its leaders, deported skilled households and transferred land to supporters. Direct Arqeshite rule remained confined to the approaches, where garrisons and allied Qahuri houses controlled the road.

Qiryath occupied a different strategic position. Its route houses could move goods and messages around formal provincial stations. Its sanctuary attracted people from districts Arqesh wanted to govern separately. Early treaties secured tribute and guides. Later kings demanded hostages from protector and priestly families.

**Neshkar II Ubar**, who ruled from 758 to 728 BR, inherited a wealthy empire troubled by provincial refusal. His elder sister **Iltara Ubar** had governed Khalbar and directed the Qahuri intervention. She argued that Qiryath could be controlled through merchants and rival protector houses. Neshkar believed visible submission was necessary after revolts elsewhere.

In 746 BR he demanded twelve priestly hostages and installation of his image within Qiryath's upper sanctuary. The city increased its tribute but refused those terms. Unauthorised negotiations and the killing of an Arqeshite envoy ended the remaining peace.

## The destruction of First Qiryath, 742 BR

The Arqeshite army approached through Khalbar with infantry, subject archers, cavalry, engineers and labour levies. Grain moved by river to a fortified depot; pack animals carried it up to the Reqa shelf. The siege required an earth ramp, timber frames and diversion of one spring channel. Captive workers and requisitioned villagers built under guard.

Qiryath fell after two harvests. Imperial reliefs later showed an orderly assault watched by Neshkar from a raised chair. The city's accounts remember hunger, the expulsion of foreigners, bodies at blocked passages and soldiers fighting through houses below the tower.

Arqeshite troops killed defenders and civilians, stripped the sanctuary and destroyed the upper tower. Executed leaders were displayed without burial. Deportation officers selected households by skill, wealth and political standing. Families were divided among royal workshops, estates and agricultural settlements. Soldiers and officials received captives as rewards.

Iltara supervised the first resettlement and ordered the cisterns preserved so the surviving villages could feed the garrison and pay grain tax. Later Jirahar accounts sometimes describe the order as mercy because it kept the district habitable. Arqeshite instructions describe the same work as preparation for assessment and military settlement.

## The empire after Qiryath, 742–706 BR

The victory displayed Arqesh at its greatest extent. Tribute entered from the Qahuri approaches, Reqa, the coast and several highland districts. Deportees enlarged Arqesh and Nemeru. Royal building consumed stone, timber and labour at a scale earlier kings could not have supported.

The enlarged empire became expensive to hold. Garrisons required grain during poor floods, provinces concealed harvests and highland horse suppliers changed allegiance. The crown reassigned canal water to estates serving the army, driving small farmers into debt or from their land. When revenue fell, the royal court increased its assessments and deepened the same shortages.

Neshkar died in 728 BR after illness during a northern campaign. His son **Belatar II** executed two uncles and married a cousin from Iltara's line. He relied upon military households—families whose estates and status came from service in the royal army—while Nemeru and Khalbar sought greater control of local revenue. Iltara died in 719, removing the relative most capable of negotiating among them.

## Succession war and coalition conquest, c. 706–690 BR

Belatar's death without an adult son divided House Ubar. **Kassur III**, supported by Arqesh and the palace army, claimed the throne. **Mardun Ubar**, governor of Durqesh, held the highland road and married into the Vashari House Tiravan. Each opened or blocked canals serving the other's supporters. Soldiers took seed grain and dismantled village roofs for camps.

Provincial revolts followed. Nemeru withheld boats. Qahuri rulers expelled isolated garrisons. Tarkhan and other open-country leaders attacked stations holding animals, grain and hostages. A few wealthy northern houses used chariots or kept scarce riding horses, but most of their fighters travelled by camel or on foot. Vashari highland houses united behind **Arezan Tiravan**, who promised to preserve submitting cities and break the Ubar monopoly.

The second great enemy came from the western sea. **Eryndor III of Ortheia**, later called the Far-Road King, had inherited an Atherian-speaking upland kingdom and a coerced hegemony over several city leagues. He crossed the eastern inner sea in 702 BR with Ortheian infantry, cavalry, Compact levies, engineers and allied ships. His first victories broke the field armies defending the coast. At **Terys**, a fortified harbour which refused him entry, his engineers joined the offshore stronghold to the mainland with a causeway. The final assault ended in massacre and enslavement.

Eryndor entered divided Qahur in 695 BR. Selected crown houses supplied grain and boats in return for recognition against their rivals. He then made a treaty with Arezan. Arezan's command held the highland roads, coordinated provincial defections and attacked Durqesh and Khalbar. Eryndor's command supplied the principal siege army, fleet and treasury. Neither ruler became the other's subordinate.

The allied armies and provincial rebels killed, looted and enslaved as they advanced. At **Khalbar**, workers forced to repair Arqeshite siege engines opened a gate when a smith named **Ishara of the Soot Court** raised her scorched apron above the wall. Rebels and Vashari troops then massacred captured soldiers and the families of several officials. Vasharan coronations later carried an apron in memory of the opened gate and omitted the killings that followed it.

Mardun was murdered by his own guard after attempting to dictate terms to Arezan. Kassur withdrew behind Arqesh's canals. Failed floods and sabotage reduced food in the capital. In 690 BR, Nemeru boats carried Eryndor's soldiers across a supposedly closed channel while Arezan's forces seized the eastern gates and water stations. Arqesh surrendered after street fighting. Both armies killed inhabitants and plundered districts assigned to the other command.

Kassur escaped with several adult sons and the portable royal images. A provincial commander murdered him in 688 BR and offered the body to Eryndor in expectation of reward. Eryndor executed the killers and buried Kassur with royal ceremony, presenting himself as the lawful heir of the king he had pursued. Two of Kassur's sons were killed during the flight; royal daughters and younger children entered guarded households attached to rival successor courts.

## Arqesh under its successors

Eryndor occupied the High Palace and treasury while Arezan held the Gate House, eastern roads and most surviving provincial administration. Canal officials continued working because neither army could feed the city without them. Eryndor accepted Ubar court dress, retained Arqeshite secretaries and allowed the Crowned Countenance to appear in his royal ceremonies. Arezan appointed Vashari officers beside local water managers and strengthened his support among the towns that had opened their gates.

The alliance survived long enough to defeat the remaining Ubar fortresses, but each king continued to govern his own army and conquests. Eryndor marched farther east and died at Arqesh in 684 BR while preparing another campaign. His widow **Parena Tiravan**, daughter of Arezan's sister Mithara, was pregnant. Ortheian commanders, the city leagues, Arezan and the royal household disagreed over who possessed the conquered army and treasury.

Arezan issued the **Decree of Roads and Houses** during the first division of Eryndor's commands. It permitted selected deportees and sacred objects to return. It also required restored communities to register property, maintain roads and acknowledge Vasharan supremacy. Arqeshite families displaced during the conquest received no comparable general restoration.

Four rulers emerged from the first succession. Arezan secured Vashar, the eastern roads and much of the Ubar basin. **Demeran Kavos**, Eryndor's senior western general, took the Ortheian homeland and tried to retain authority over the Atherian cities. **Serevan of Delkos**, commander of the allied fleet, held islands and eastern harbours through a mixed maritime court. **Neresh of Qemaret**, a Qahuri-born officer, controlled selected southern approaches and crown towns through garrisons and marriage. Their wars killed the nominal royal heirs and divided the conquests by 650 BR.

Former governors who submitted kept estates or entered one of these services. Others established small dynasties in districts the successor governments could not immediately control.

Arqesh remained a large city under its successors. Its workshops supplied the new court, its scholars entered Vasharan service and its water managers continued to direct the principal canals. The river population included descendants of Arqeshite citizens, deportees, Vashari officials, Jirahar communities, military settlers and older subject peoples. Later Atherian and Eclessian administrators inherited Arqeshite water terms, survey marks and storehouse plans through this mixed society.

## Historical memory

Arqeshite successor families remember an age of safe canals, learned kings and protected trade. Subject accounts remember the same roads carrying deportees and siege timber. Jirahar tradition gives particular weight to 742 BR. Qahuri histories remember Ashkur's death, occupation of their eastern approaches and Eryndor's later royal visit. Ortheian biographies make Eryndor the sole destroyer of Arqesh; Vasharan inscriptions give that honour to Arezan. Canal-city accounts preserve the governors, boat crews and forced workers without whom neither invader could have entered the capital.

Arqeshite inhabitants maintained difficult waterworks, preserved older learning, raised families and produced celebrated textiles, music and brick architecture. Their taxes and labour also supported armies that destroyed cities, enslaved captives and deported whole households. Successor communities inherited both the useful works and the injuries of imperial rule.
