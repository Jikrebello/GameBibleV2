# Arqesh — River Cities, Kings and Deportation

**Arqesh** was a canal empire of the Ubar riverlands. A grain contractor's family, House Ubar, took its Gate House around 958 BR and lost the city in 690. House Ubar joined older cities through water control, royal estates, military service and the forced movement of conquered households. Its administration preserved canals, measures and learning used by later states. The same institutions enabled the empire to starve rebellious districts, enslave captives and destroy First Qiryath in 742 BR.

## Country of the Ubar

Arqesh developed in the lower basin of the **Ubar**, a broad eastern river fed by highland snow and seasonal rain. Its two principal channels crossed a flat country of silt, reeds and old lake beds before reaching the eastern sea. Floods arrived less predictably than those of Qahur. Settlements depended upon embankments, drainage cuts and canals maintained across several districts.

The river country supported barley, wheat, sesame, dates, onions, sheep and cattle. Reed boats carried grain and brick. Pack routes brought stone, timber, horses and metal from the eastern highlands. Coastal towns exchanged oil, cloth and worked goods with Atherian and Gateway ships. Irrigation permitted dense settlement while making villages vulnerable to anyone who controlled an upstream gate.

Four cities shaped the mature civilisation. **Arqesh** stood where the river divided and commanded the principal canal junction. **Nemeru** lay downstream among gardens, textile workshops and quays. **Khalbar** guarded the northern crossing towards Qahur. **Durqesh** occupied the road leading east into the highlands. Each city possessed older sacred houses and civic families before Arqesh conquered it.

The Arqeshite language and brick tradition spread along the river and the roads into neighbouring countries. Builders used its water terms, survey marks and storehouse plans long after the city that devised them had lost political control.

## The canal cities, c. 1750–1100 BR

The first Ubar cities belonged to the Bronze Roads. Their principal palaces were large royal or aristocratic households containing the ruler's family, servants, workshops, stores and administrative staff. These households commissioned canals and negotiated with village elders who supplied maintenance crews. Temple estates controlled other stores and fields. Royal families, temples, civic households and cultivating communities therefore held land under different obligations. Enslaved people and debt-bound labourers worked beside free tenants and seasonal crews.

Water required cooperation across political boundaries. A city could open a channel needed by its rival and still attack that rival after harvest. Surveyors, dredging crews and boat builders moved between courts. Marriage joined royal houses while merchants maintained partnerships through wars their rulers had begun.

**Teluq**, an older city above Durqesh, dominated the eastern approaches during the sixteenth and fifteenth centuries BR. Its decline followed the Breaking of the Bronze Roads. Highland suppliers withheld metal, palace households fought over dwindling transport and an abandoned canal changed the course of one distributary. Teluq survived as a temple and market town, but later Arqeshite kings appropriated much of its royal history.

The most famous thing Arqesh took from Teluq was a length of timber. Around 1370 BR, in a season when Teluq's ruler had pledged the upstream villages, their taxes and their labour to his military creditors, the head regulator above the city opened in the night with no hand on its windlass. Water meant for the creditors' new estates ran down the old village channels until morning. The gate crew found a boy of six or seven asleep on the regulator beam, dry, with the pins lying beside him. Nobody claimed him. The crew named him **Irduq** and raised him in the gate house.

Irduq grew up measuring field water and became keeper of the gate. Three canal districts placed their regulators under his seal, a sleeping child on a beam, and for a generation they drew water by his schedule whatever Teluq's rulers decreed. He never took the throne. The ruler who had pledged the villages was deposed by his own creditors, and his successors found it cheaper to confirm the keeper than to fight the districts. Irduq conscripted the same villages for dredging and embankment work as hard as any king, and Teluq songs remember both the water and the baskets of silt.

Teluq never agreed about the night. Gate crews said the river had opened the gate for the villages and left them a keeper. Palace scribes wrote that a drunk crewman had drawn the pins and a reed-cutter's runaway had crept onto the beam to sleep. The creditors' families said the villagers had done it themselves and set the child there. After Teluq's decline the beam came down the river to Arqesh, by flood according to the Gate House and by barge and soldiers according to Teluq. The Gate House built it into the great regulator at the junction and sealed its water books with the sleeping child. House Ubar later ruled the river under that seal, which was older than the dynasty and which the canal officials obeyed before they obeyed any king.

## Arqesh before empire, c. 1100–960 BR

Arqesh first prospered at the junction where boats entered either principal channel of the Ubar. Its granaries held grain awaiting shipment, and its officials recorded when each district could draw canal water, what part of a harvest it owed and which boats had paid for passage. Merchants brought disputes over measures and damaged cargo before these officials because their decisions were recognised on both channels. The city attracted refugees and craftsmen after wars among the upper cities. Its temple households accumulated land by lending grain during bad harvests and taking fields when debtors could not repay.

Political conflict centred upon the **Gate House**, whose keepers controlled the principal regulators and held the Sleeper's seal, and the **House of the High Image**, which conducted royal ceremonies. Military captains entered both institutions through marriage. A succession struggle around 1010 BR left several neighbourhoods flooded when one claimant opened a canal to prevent his rival crossing it. The water destroyed poorer housing and deposited silt across fields for two seasons.

Farmers and merchants responded to the flooding by demanding one authority capable of coordinating the principal gates during a crisis. Claimants to the throne recognised that control of those gates could also block an enemy's boats, flood his supporters and direct food towards an army.

## House Ubar and imperial government, c. 960–800 BR

**Merod I Ubar**, a grain contractor and commander related to the Gate House through his mother, seized the throne around 958 BR. He had organised boats during two failed harvests and built a following among soldiers paid in grain allotments. Merod imprisoned the existing king, married one of his daughters and declared that the river had selected a household capable of keeping every gate.

His first campaigns secured nearby canals. Villages that submitted retained their elders and fields under a new grain tax. Settlements that cut embankments or killed royal agents lost inhabitants to deportation. Merod placed transferred families beside neglected channels where their labour enlarged the crown estate.

His grandson **Kassur I Ubar**, ruling approximately 918–879 BR, transformed these practices into imperial institutions. He divided conquered land into river provinces, appointed governors over major gates and required subject cities to provide troops, boats and craftsmen. Royal messengers travelled on maintained embankments. A ration system supported soldiers and workers at fixed stations.

Service to the crown offered limited advancement. A deported mason might be placed over a royal workshop, and a subject prince might retain his title after sending a daughter and sons to live under watch at Arqesh. Merchants benefited from guarded channels and common measures. The same government assigned conquered households to new districts for their labour and held children to secure their parents' obedience.

Kassur's armies captured Nemeru after a three-year struggle. The final assault killed defenders and civilians in the riverside district. Officers deported boatwrights, dyers and metalworkers, settled loyal troops near the quay and installed the royal image in the old civic temple. Nemeru recovered its wealth under Arqeshite rule and remained a centre of resentment.

**Merod II Ubar**, who ruled from approximately 836 to 798 BR, inherited Kassur's provincial system after two shorter reigns. His armies were already competing for the roads and cultivated approaches east of Qahur when the dead prince of Meheret's rent was broken. In 815–814 BR the boat households and allotment farmers of the middle river rose against the stewards who collected in the dead prince's name, and stewards who escaped fled east to Arqeshite road stations carrying copies of the tallies for the fields they had managed. Those tallies were good only if the dead prince's house confirmed them. Merod took up their claims and around 810 sent his younger son **Ashkur Ubar** with an escort to bring the exiles to Meheret and have their stewardships sealed in the palace. Ashkur went into the sealed courts with three of the exiles, and the doors closed behind them.

The escort waited at the gate among the gilded guards that stand in Meheret's streets. On the fourth morning Ashkur came out alone, carrying the exiles' tally-boxes sealed in copper leaf and a tally that named House Ubar steward of the dead prince's fields east of the river. He never said what he had agreed inside. Merod occupied road stations and eastern canal towns as the dead prince's collector, transferred fields to military supporters over the following decade and sent a tenth of the rent to Meheret. Arqeshite histories say the three exiles chose to stay in their lord's service; Qahuri histories say Ashkur sold them for the tally, and no Qahuri crown has recognised it.

## Court, households and learning

The king ruled with royal relatives, military commanders, palace managers, scribes and temple dignitaries. Provincial governors possessed wide authority but remained vulnerable to denunciation at court. Royal women managed estates, arranged marriages, endowed temples and represented absent sons. The king's sisters frequently married subject rulers or held their children at Arqesh.

Brick palaces contained reception courts, treasuries, armouries, gardens and writing rooms. Scholars copied older river texts, astronomical observations, medical recipes and royal annals. Conquered scribes brought tablets, languages and local knowledge into the royal collections. Some works survived only in Arqeshite copies because the army that seized them had burned the school or temple where they were composed.

Most people encountered the state through grain assessment, water labour and military requisition. Canal maintenance was necessary public work. Governors could increase it until families lost their own planting season. Corvée crews received rations of grain, oil and beer; prisoners and enslaved labourers received less and suffered the highest death rates.

Household life continued beneath imperial demands. Courtyard ovens served several related families. Children played at opening miniature mud gates. Nemeru dyers held evening competitions in which each workshop produced an intentionally ugly cloth and argued that a rival had made the best one by mistake. Boat crews dedicated the first date cake of the flood season to whoever had repaired the worst leak.

## Gods, images and royal splendour

Every city maintained several sacred households. River gates, gardens, storms, ancestors, kingship and particular crafts received offerings through local traditions. Conquest rarely abolished all of them. Arqeshite rulers preferred to place the royal image above or among a city's accepted powers and require public acknowledgement.

The imperial cult called the enthroned king the **Visible Measure of the Lands**. Reliefs showed conquered peoples carrying their characteristic goods towards a ruler larger than every other person. Provincial ceremonies required leading families to bow before a portable image and speak their city's obligations. The greatest of these images, gilded likenesses of the reigning king carried on poles from province to province, travelled with the court and were treated as part of the crown itself.

During the later empire, courtiers attributed flattering dreams and a heightened presence at these ceremonies to a power they called the **Crowned Countenance**. On occasion an image answered in the reigning king's voice.

Several rulers exploited these responses. Others feared them and continued the ceremonies because provincial obedience depended upon the imperial cult.

## Expansion towards Qahur and Qiryath, c. 820–746 BR

Arqeshite armies reached the eastern approaches of Qahur during a period of divided crowns. Their aim was to secure grain, caravan stages and the road between Khalbar and the Basin. Merod II's campaign under Ashkur's copper tally enlarged an existing military presence. A royal commander repaired one neglected canal and established garrisons beside it. When a local rising killed tax agents, the army executed its leaders, deported skilled households and transferred land to supporters. Direct Arqeshite rule remained confined to the approaches, where garrisons and allied Qahuri houses controlled the road.

Qiryath occupied a different strategic position. Its route houses could move goods and messages around formal provincial stations. Its sanctuary drew something more dangerous than trade. Canal districts carried their quarrels up the road to be settled at Qiryath's festival offerings, where an accepted offering burned without smoke and the altar stones stayed cool under the hands of the witnesses. A judgement given over cool stones felt beyond appeal. By the middle of the eighth century, Arqeshite provinces were bringing disputes to the sanctuary in the hills instead of to the river-king's governors. Early treaties secured tribute and guides. Later kings demanded hostages from protector and priestly families.

**Ilbarun II Ubar**, who ruled from 758 to 731 BR, inherited a wealthy empire troubled by provincial refusal. His elder sister **Iltara Ubar** had governed Khalbar and directed the Qahuri intervention. She argued that Qiryath could be controlled through merchants and rival protector houses: buy the road, and the city's judges would follow its trade. Ilbarun believed visible submission was necessary after revolts elsewhere. He wanted his image in the upper sanctuary beside the altar. If the altar went on answering with the king's likeness standing over it, his crown would share its authority; if it fell silent, the rival court in the hills was broken. Courtiers called it a wager he could not lose.

In 746 BR he demanded twelve priestly hostages and installation of his image within Qiryath's upper sanctuary. The city increased its tribute but refused those terms. Unauthorised negotiations and the killing of an Arqeshite envoy ended the remaining peace.

## The destruction of First Qiryath, 742 BR

The Arqeshite army approached through Khalbar with infantry, subject archers, cavalry, engineers and labour levies. Grain moved by river to a fortified depot; pack animals carried it up to the Reqa shelf. The siege required an earth ramp and timber frames, which captive workers and requisitioned villagers built under guard. For the city's water Ilbarun brought a woman of House Keresh up from Khalbar. Her mothers' line had held the right to call the Reqa shelf's streams since a guardian's wife used it eighty years earlier, and at the first rain she sent them past Qiryath's cisterns.

Qiryath fell after two harvests. Imperial reliefs later showed an orderly assault watched by Ilbarun from a raised chair. The city's accounts remember hunger, bodies at blocked passages and soldiers fighting through houses below the tower. They also remember the first winter of the siege, when the council declared the city's foreign residents unhoused, canal-country litigants and merchants among them, and let them starve in the caravan yard, and the next offering sent black smoke over the walls and left the altar stones hot. Arqeshite scouts saw the smoke. Ilbarun's reliefs say nothing of it.

Arqeshite troops killed defenders and civilians, stripped the sanctuary and destroyed the upper tower. Executed leaders were displayed without burial. Deportation officers selected households by skill, wealth and political standing. Families were divided among royal workshops, estates and agricultural settlements. Soldiers and officials received captives as rewards.

Iltara supervised the first resettlement and ordered the cisterns preserved so the surviving villages could feed the garrison and pay grain tax. Later Jirahar accounts sometimes describe the order as mercy because it kept the district habitable. Arqeshite instructions describe the same work as preparation for assessment and military settlement.

## The empire after Qiryath, 742–706 BR

The victory displayed Arqesh at its greatest extent. Tribute entered from the Qahuri approaches, Reqa, the coast and several highland districts. Deportees enlarged Arqesh and Nemeru. Royal building consumed stone, timber and labour at a scale earlier kings could not have supported.

Governors carried the same methods to the coast. In the years after Qiryath they seized Atherian ships of the Celestial Compact lying in eastern harbours and held the crews against ransom. Between about 740 and 730 BR they marched the Atherian merchant quarter of **Belyra** inland, families and all, to the royal shipyards at Nemeru and the estates along the canals. The seizures filled the yards with skilled hands. They also gave Valeron II of Ortheia the grievance on which he raised an eastern war.

The enlarged empire became expensive to hold. Garrisons required grain during poor floods, provinces concealed harvests and highland horse suppliers changed allegiance. The crown reassigned canal water to estates serving the army, driving small farmers into debt or from their land. When revenue fell, the royal court increased its assessments and deepened the same shortages.

Ilbarun died in 731 BR of a marsh fever taken while he inspected the new shipyards at Nemeru. His son **Belatar II** executed two uncles and married a cousin from Iltara's line. He relied upon military households—families whose estates and status came from service in the royal army—while Nemeru and Khalbar sought greater control of local revenue. Iltara died in 719, removing the relative most capable of negotiating among them.

## Succession war and coalition conquest, c. 706–690 BR

Belatar's death without an adult son divided House Ubar. **Kassur III**, supported by Arqesh and the palace army, claimed the throne. **Mardun Ubar**, governor of Durqesh, held the highland road and married into the Vashari House Tiravan. Each opened or blocked canals serving the other's supporters. Soldiers took seed grain and dismantled village roofs for camps.

Provincial revolts followed. Nemeru withheld boats. Qahuri rulers expelled isolated garrisons. Tarkhan and other open-country leaders attacked stations holding animals, grain and hostages. A few wealthy northern houses used chariots or kept scarce riding horses, but most of their fighters travelled by camel or on foot. Vashari highland houses united behind **Arezan Tiravan**, who promised to preserve submitting cities and break the Ubar monopoly.

The second great enemy came from the western sea. **Eryndor III of Ortheia**, later called the Far-Road King, had inherited an Atherian-speaking upland kingdom and a coerced hegemony over several city leagues. He crossed the eastern inner sea in 702 BR with cavalry, newly raised foot, Compact levies, engineers and allied ships; the standing companies that had killed his father stayed in Ortheia. His first victories broke the field armies defending the coast. **Belyra**, the fortified harbour from which the merchant quarter had been deported, refused him entry, and in 697 he left the allied fleet to blockade it and marched on. In 692 its governor and leading households fled by sea, scuttling the harbour's hulks with bonded divers and haulers shut inside them. The town opened its gates to the fleet, and the surrender ended in massacre and enslavement.

Eryndor entered divided Qahur in 695 BR. One crown house sold him boats and guides against a share of the plunder, and the grain came on loan from the store courts of a dead Qemaret financier's Second House. He then made a treaty with Arezan. Arezan's command held the highland roads, coordinated provincial defections and attacked Durqesh and Khalbar. Eryndor's command supplied the principal siege army, fleet and treasury.

The allied armies and provincial rebels killed, looted and enslaved as they advanced. At **Khalbar**, workers forced to repair Arqeshite siege engines turned their hammers on the guards and opened a gate, led by a smith named **Ishara of the Soot Court**. Rebels and Vashari troops then massacred captured soldiers and the families of several officials. Vasharan coronation hymns later thanked the workers of Khalbar beside the Daryam flame and left out the killings that followed.

Mardun was murdered by his own guard after trying to withdraw from his agreement with Arezan and dictate new terms. Kassur withdrew behind Arqesh's canals. Failed floods and sabotage reduced food in the capital, and he fed the palace army by cutting the rations of the dredging gangs and the deportee quarters. In 690 BR the gangs rose, killed their overseers and opened the gates. The Belyra families in the Nemeru shipyards launched unfinished hulls and carried Eryndor's soldiers across a supposedly closed channel, while Arezan's forces seized the eastern gates and water stations. Nemeru itself kept its gates shut against all three armies. Arqesh surrendered after street fighting. Both armies killed inhabitants and plundered districts assigned to the other command, and both set the gangs back to the canals under guard, since neither could feed the city without them.

Kassur escaped up the eastern channel with several adult sons, his household guard and the portable royal images. In the reed country in 688 BR the images began to answer in Eryndor's voice. Their hereditary bearers took it for the images' choice and carried them across to the Ortheian lines by night; the guard scattered, and two of Kassur's sons were killed in the confusion. Kassur rode to Arezan's outposts at Durqesh and surrendered to a man he had known as a hostage in the royal cavalry there. Arezan refused Eryndor's demand to hand him over. At Durqesh the last Ubar king sealed the Gate House's water books over to Arezan, and the canal officials, who had obeyed that seal since before House Ubar, served Vashar under it. Kassur lived as a guarded guest in an orchard house at Kavresh and died there around 676 BR; he was buried on a Vashari hillside with no likeness over him. Royal daughters and younger children entered guarded households attached to rival successor courts.

## Arqesh under its successors

Eryndor occupied the High Palace and treasury while Arezan held the Gate House, eastern roads and most surviving provincial administration. Canal officials continued working because neither army could feed the city without them, and after 688 they kept their books under Kassur's seal to Arezan. Eryndor accepted Ubar court dress, retained Arqeshite secretaries and set Kassur's images in his audience hall, where they answered in his voice and courtiers said the Crowned Countenance still attended the bowing. Within a year the images had ordered one of his own officers to bow, the officer had been executed under the old court law for refusing, and a companion who mocked them had been strangled by their bearers. After that they spoke in the dead companion's voice and called the king by his camp name. Arezan appointed Vashari officers beside local water managers and strengthened his support among the towns that had opened their gates.

The alliance survived long enough to defeat the remaining Ubar fortresses, but each king continued to govern his own army and conquests. Eryndor marched east in 687 to get out of their hearing, came back wounded in 685 and went down the river in 684 to sail home to Ortheia. He died aboard the flagship of the allied fleet in the roads of Nemeru, in the middle of a Qahuri funerary rite performed in the ship's hold. Its crew, descendants of the Belyra deportees from the Nemeru yards, sailed the embalmed body and Kassur's images to Delkos. There the images stood in the tomb-hall from which, for nearly thirty years, a voice claiming to be Eryndor promised away provinces, until they were walled into the tomb with the corpse around 655 BR. His widow **Parena Tiravan**, daughter of Arezan's sister Mithara, took the road to Kavresh, where their daughter Mehrava was in her grandmother's keeping. Ortheian commanders, the city leagues, Arezan and the royal household disagreed over who possessed the conquered army and treasury.

Arezan issued the **Decree of Roads and Houses** during the first division of Eryndor's commands. It permitted selected deportees and sacred objects to return. It also required restored communities to register property, maintain roads and acknowledge Vasharan supremacy. Arqeshite families displaced during the conquest received no comparable general restoration.

Four rulers emerged from the first succession. Arezan secured Vashar, the eastern roads and much of the Ubar basin. **Demeran Kavos**, speaker of the standing companies Eryndor had left at home, took the Ortheian homeland and tried to retain authority over the Atherian cities. **Serevan of Delkos**, commander of the allied fleet, held islands and eastern harbours through a mixed maritime court. **Neresh of Qemaret**, a Qahuri-born officer, controlled selected southern approaches and crown towns through garrisons and marriage. Their wars killed the king's half-brother, left Parena's daughter a disputed claim in Vasharan keeping and divided the conquests by 650 BR.

Former governors who submitted kept estates or entered one of these services. Others established small dynasties in districts the successor governments could not immediately control.

Arqesh remained a large city under its successors. Its workshops supplied the new court, its scholars entered Vasharan service and its water managers continued to direct the principal canals. The river population included descendants of Arqeshite citizens, deportees, Vashari officials, Jirahar communities, military settlers and older subject peoples. Later Atherian and Eclessian administrators inherited Arqeshite water terms, survey marks and storehouse plans through this mixed society.

## Historical memory

Arqeshite successor families remember an age of safe canals, learned kings and protected trade. Subject accounts remember the same roads carrying deportees and siege timber. Jirahar tradition gives particular weight to 742 BR. Qahuri histories remember the prince who came out of Meheret with a dead boy's tally, occupation of their eastern approaches and the foreign king who borrowed from their dead. Ortheian biographies make Eryndor the sole destroyer of Arqesh; Vasharan inscriptions give that honour to Arezan. Canal-city accounts preserve the governors, boat crews and forced workers without whom neither invader could have entered the capital.
