# Ilamar before 350 BR

Ilamar was an independent sanctuary and market island in the central [[Elmaric Island Peoples before 350 BR|Elmaric]] sea network. It connected the Elmarai Isles, shoal communities and Seravon-facing harbours. Resident custodians maintained a plural sacred precinct where rival visitors could witness oaths, arrange safe conduct, settle cargo disputes and negotiate ransom.

Different island rites shared the sanctuary. Thalassor had followers there. The resident community maintained its own household and harbour life while drawing pilgrims, merchants, pilots, captains and people seeking shelter. Visitors' willingness to honour a witnessed agreement gave the custodians influence beyond the island. Several of their hearings reached far into mainland history: the sealed jars of about 551 BR, which first put the Gateway capital's plenty on record; the copy of the Roll of Refused Hulls laid in the precinct in 461; Marovan Veyr's last sanctuary in 373–371; and the two refugees of the succession wars, one of Casvilar's killers and Casvilar's son, in 367–359 and from 350.

## Sacred place and resident authority

Ilamar's custodians kept a protected precinct in which several island traditions could witness agreements. Different shrines, inherited rites and accounts of the island's origin coexisted because rivals needed a place to meet. The sanctuary's law concerned conduct on its ground: entry, hospitality, oaths, ransom and the treatment of cargo under its protection.

The island had residents apart from its custodial offices. Their food, housing and harbour labour sustained visitors, and they bore the first cost when a threatened convoy failed or an armed captain demanded better treatment. Ilamar's independence depended on those residents and on other ports continuing to recognise its safe conduct. It could refuse an outside dynasty an appointment to its court, but could not ignore that dynasty's grain, ships or power over the approaches.

Grain made the sanctuary practical. Ilamar could not reliably feed every visitor from its own ground.

## The grain and convoy court

An island whose ships bought food from a rival needed trustworthy weights, records and witnesses. Ilamar supplied them. Its officers could certify a grain measure, record what a captain had promised to deliver, hear why a convoy had lost cargo and proclaim safe conduct during a shortage. These were useful services to people who shared no ruler. A judgement carried force because future partners might refuse to trade with someone who openly defied it.

Political pressure followed that usefulness. A donor could offer food while seeking favourable precedence; a captain guarding a grain ship could demand a better hearing; a harbour dynasty might threaten to withhold supplies. Custodians bargained to preserve access for competing parties. The resulting independence was real but limited, and the court's measures could still serve coercive grain holdings on outer shores.

## Captives and armed visitors

Protection at the threshold gave a fugitive time that might have been impossible on an open quay. It did not usually cancel a claimed ownership or debt. The custodians might arrange a return, an exchange between households or a ransom. A person considered valuable to a sponsor, a political party or a specialist trade had a better chance of a formal hearing than someone without advocates. That inequality was built into the sanctuary's working rules.

Raiders also needed ransom brokers and a place to settle contested prizes. Ilamar permitted conditional entry but forbade attacks launched from its harbour and violence against travellers under its recognised conduct. A visitor entering the sacred precinct could be required to disarm. Captains with strong patrons, hostages, vessels or control of supply negotiated wider harbour privileges than lesser crews. The custodians could resist or publicise abuse, but could not enforce identical treatment against every armed guest.

## Hearings the mainland remembered

**The sealed jars, c. 551 BR.** In the spring of about 551 BR, carters from Esharat and Belqara brought storage jars across to Ilamar's court. The jars had been filled at harvest and stamped with their towns' seals before witnesses. At the spring count they stood a third empty or worse, though every seal was whole. The custodians cut the seals themselves on the court steps, in front of Gateway factors and island buyers, and found the grain far below the marks scratched inside the necks and small bite marks in the underside of the wax. No thief was ever named. The carters said their grain had gone where all the dependent towns' grain went in those years, to the Bronze-Faced Provider's tables at Qarzeth, whose storehouses never ran low. The custodians entered the finding in their record and from that year refused to certify grain from the sanctuary's estates as honest measure. A Qarzethi envoy called the refusal an insult to a guest; the custodians answered that a measure which emptied other men's jars was no measure at all. A generation later both sides of Caleran's great debate quoted the finding: Mera Castren as proof that the sanctuary's plenty could be inspected and stopped, Orsen Valeor as proof that nothing short of the city's fall would stop it.

**The Roll of Refused Hulls, 461 BR.** The Chain Peace that ended the War of the Closed Chains was sworn in Ilamar's precinct, because neither Caleran nor the rebel harbours would cross to the other's ground. It put every harbour's chain in the keeping of its own wardens and made shelter for ships in distress the duty of every harbour, whatever flag the ship flew. The parties needed a place that none of them owned to keep the proof. Pilots from Velathene, who had learned the old Roll of Refused Hulls from Kelarion's reef along with their approaches, wrote out a copy: the names of ships drowned within sight of a closed harbour seven centuries before. It was laid in Ilamar's precinct. From then on captains turned away from a harbour in bad weather brought their complaints to the island, and the custodians heard refuge cases beside grain and ransom. Island crews still touched the chest that held the copy before a winter voyage.

**Marovan Veyr's sanctuary, 373–371 BR.** For four years Marovan Veyr had held the east of a divided Atheria without a fleet, because the eastern ports would not pay for his galleys. In 373 BR he sailed to Sarqen to ask for the grain his army lived on, turned back from a table laid with an empty seat, and found the sea road home held against him. He came to Ilamar with his wife Calvena, the remains of his household and the senator Arest Corven, and entered the precinct disarmed, as every armed visitor did. Teren Casvilar's officers demanded him as a named contestant of the Red Field. The custodians refused, and Casvilar did not storm the island: the certificates on which his army's grain purchases depended were written in its court, and an Ilamar closed to him would have closed half the sea's granaries as well. Veyr lived two years in a lodging above the harbour, writing to both camps with terms that neither answered, and died there of fever in 371 with Calvena nursing him. He had never met his wife's father in the field. Corven stayed and began the history of the republic that Casvilar's heirs would later have to answer.

**The man on the quay, 367–359 BR.** Iren Sallas, one of the seven men who had entered their names as Casvilar's challengers and killed him on Caleran's muster ground, reached Ilamar in 367, the winter the Atherian partners began killing their enemies on the muster ground, and entered the precinct disarmed. While any of the seven lived, the Atherians held, the Red Field stood open and no commander could disband an army. Thalion's men waited on the quay for eight years. The custodians would not give Sallas up, and Thalion would not storm an island whose court wrote the certificates that fed his armies. In 359, after word came of the starving town of Darsil, Sallas walked out of the precinct and down to the quay to them, so that he would not be the reason the war went on. The custodians recorded that he had left of his own will, and island crews afterwards called the steps he went down by his name.

**Casvilar's son, 350 BR.** In 350 Amesar, son of Teren Casvilar and Queen Samyra of Sarqen, killed the last of the seven, Mavren Seneth, at the spared towns' table and came to Ilamar on a fishing boat. He entered the precinct disarmed, as Veyr had. Thalion's officers demanded him; the custodians refused. Thalion, whose cousin Calvena crossed to the island on the boy's behalf, let him stay. Amesar lived the rest of his life in the lodging above the harbour where Veyr had died, and there he finished the history Arest Corven had begun.

By 350 BR Ilamar remained a place where enemies could exchange grain, words and people without acknowledging a common sovereign.
