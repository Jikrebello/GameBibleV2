# Age of Hearths and First Kings

The **Age of Hearths and First Kings**, approximately 1900–1400 BR, marks the growth of durable local societies after the Scattering. Villages maintained fields and winter stores; river towns repaired irrigation; hill forts protected cultivated land; clan halls joined households through marriage, ancestry and defence. The first kings emerged from this local world as war captains, river patrons, oath-chiefs and protectors of food; a few, like the crown-mothers of Narash, ruled because only their blood could keep a bargain with the powers of the land. Their authority could preserve a district through famine or turn a temporary obligation into hereditary service.

The northern fission continued throughout these centuries. Proto-Jirahar communities developed portable courts and custodianship across dispersed settlements. The peoples of the western well country built defended markets, guest-law and territorial oath-coalitions.

Farther north and east, Tarkhan communities deepened the Nine Wells of Tergai, maintained camel roads and moved cattle and sheep through seasonal grass. Taleri craft towns worked around grounded Vathran ruins. The broad Tarkhan name came to connect these western and eastern populations through fosterage, assembly and related speech, although no king governed them all.

## The first durable homes

Between c. 1900 and 1400 BR, settlement made new kinds of power possible. A household could store grain for a second winter, improve a field it expected to inherit and bury its dead beside a hall rather than on a road. Those achievements also made it easier for a chief to demand labour, a creditor to seize a family and a war band to return regularly for tribute.

The age contained cattle kingdoms, hill forts, river villages, island ports and wandering households. A ruined Giant canal could sustain a dense population beside forest communities that preferred seasonal movement to permanent fields. Iron, bronze, stone and salvaged mechanisms coexisted, and communities generally favoured tools they could repair with local materials.

Metal followed the ruins. Along the old roads near the Sacred Basin, smiths recast copper and bronze dug from Giant tomb-estates, and bronze ruled there until the Reclaiming of c. 1450–1325 BR, when the tombs' gilded servants walked the roads collecting it. Folk and Ättar smiths in the cold south, far from any salvage, smelted bog ore from the fens around Ossen and ridge ore from Odrani country from early in the age, because iron was the only metal they could get. Their neighbours on the trunk roads thought iron a poor man's metal until the Reclaiming taught them its one great virtue: nobody dead had ever owned it.

## Protection becomes rule

Cattle defenders acquired followers because they could keep raiders away. Followers fed them; captains settled quarrels; successful captains demanded obedience after the danger had passed. [[G-05 - The Iron Ring before the Crown|The Iron Ring before the Crown]] remembers an outlaw company implicated in rape's aftermath, massacre, the seizure of women and the creation of a coercive kingdom. Coronation songs celebrate the ruler who emerged from the company, while household laments preserve the names of women seized and families killed during his ascent.

A second road to kingship ran through the powers of the land. When a crisis leader bargained with a Menhir court on behalf of a household, the gift, people found, stayed with her line and lasted only while her descendants kept its price. A community that needed the gift came to need the line, whatever it thought of her. The best-known case is **Meya of Narash**, who sealed the Hundred Wells against the broken sky roads after Vathra's fall. Her daughters have walked the whole round of the wells at every accession since, calling each well by its name, and Narashi succession stayed with mothers and daughters while every neighbouring crown passed to sons. Such houses kept the rule of descent they had held on the day of the bargain, for fear of losing the gift. Every heir who fell ill, went to war or refused the price brought a crisis with them. [[Vathra and the Great Unyoking]] tells Meya's bargain.

The Ark-descended camps of the Near-Glass refused that road. Their stories said the Prophet had turned away the offers of the desert powers, and they would not let a judge's or protector's sons inherit a gift no one else could keep. Households still made private bargains for a cistern or a safe path. Public office rested on witnesses, oath and armed service, and a protector could be removed when the emergency ended. Early Jirahar judges stayed judges; Tovan bar Rameq refused the title of protector, though a son murdered his half-brothers to take the power anyway, and the protector house Yedran bar Aqev tried to found at Qiryath broke apart among his own children (see [[01 Canon Reference/Peoples and Cultures/Jirahar/70 History and Conflicts/Historical Atlas of Qiryath before Atheria.md|Historical Atlas of Qiryath before Atheria]]). Ahdari wells beside the Narashi chain had to be held by walls, sworn protectors and blood-price, which is why those things fill the oldest Ahdari histories.

Road traditions kept a different limit upon ambitious men. [[G-06 - The Wolf, the Lantern and the House That Heard Them Laugh|Wolf and Lantern]] were pursued for wrongs committed against several households when the Old Mother granted shelter but not release. Their later three-trial cycle is comic, yet it preserves the reciprocal danger of guest-right, bargains and insulting a power whose apparent frailty concealed deep occult authority.

Other traditions are smaller. A hunting household survived a Menhir-hosted winter by respecting one prohibition, then lost a child when the hunter mistook correct hospitality for permission to take more. A former slave's collar was later carried before a crown: the court called it an honour until a household lament preserved the sale and execution beneath the ceremony. These stories made limits memorable when kings preferred smooth genealogies.

## Cousins facing different enemies

Folk and Ättar shared older Orphaned ancestry. Forest and field clans fought one another, bargained with courts and resisted new Atherian settlements. Farther into the southern cold belt, halls faced Grobi pressure that made watch duty, winter stores and collective defence increasingly decisive.

[[G-61 - The Hall That Faced the Grobi|The Hall That Faced the Grobi]] belongs before the great crisis around 1600 BR. Kin demanded aid for a feud while a threatened hall refused to strip its own walls. Relatives died without help; the feared attack still came. Later singers turned the refusal into a clean birth of a people, but the actual divergence accumulated over generations. [[War of the Empty Halls]] and Mother Moon's firstborn bargain came later and changed already-established halls.

## Sea-kings and linked recoveries

The Aurin's river towns and the eastern Thalassian harbours formed distinct parts of this development. [[Southern Settlement and Maritime Atheria]] follows ship households and their suppliers; [[Arkenan and Saronikan Cities before the Empire]] describes Erenost's grain seizure and the later civic histories of the islands. Caleran's wolf-founders belong to the following city-state period, after these maritime relationships had already developed.

Farther north, Orphaned river communities restored the Sacred Basin after Ishkatar's collapse. [[Ancient Qahur and the Twin-Reed Crowns]] follows their development from the Years of Loose Water to the Senareth kings and first Twin-Reed division. Canal repair joined towns into larger crowns, while royal forced labour and mortuary estates placed new burdens upon the families whose knowledge kept the water moving. [[Life in the Ancient Sacred Basin]] describes the work and domestic life sustained by those systems.

Southern war-settlers became maritime societies through shipbuilding, captured harbours and local pilotage. Mercenaries, raiders, refugees and traders used the same vessels. An island king might buy copper honestly in one port and sell captives in the next. The resulting networks restored crafts and food supplies while making distant households vulnerable to one fleet's defeat.

[[G-73 - The Oar-King without a Country|The Oar-King]] follows a Folk coastal captive who saves abandoned rowers, gains a following, conquers an inhabited harbour and arranges the disguised murder of the fellow rower who opposes his kingship. A prophet exposes both crimes. The king confesses, frees captives still under his control and ends future human-sale payment, but retains the crown and most allotments. A succession war after his death ends hereditary rule and produces the harbour's civic magistracy.

## Seasons and powers

The powers of the land decided harvests, successions and wars in this age, and people remembered them by the damage.

**The Withheld Season.** On the Tidewood–Gale-Crown watershed, sometime between about 1850 and 1725 BR, hall rulers sealed seed, breeding animals and the winter's dead under a preservation compact after three bad winters. The compact saved the halls. When spring came the rulers refused to open their stores, and the High Hunt rode the old seasonal roads to take what had been withheld: corpses from the roofs of the frozen houses, pregnant animals from the stalls, sacks from locked floors and living people who stood in its way. Watershed songs tell of rulers who pointed the riders at their poorer neighbours' barns, and of one rider who would not take the easier prey and rode past the poor barn to the ruler's own loft; a weathered rider's mask in a watershed hall is said to show his face. Hunger followed, because the stores had been hoarded too long and then lost all at once. Release cuts in harness, burial cloth and barn lintels still remember the year (see [[G-67 - The Marks of Necessary Release|The Marks of Necessary Release]]).

**Kingship by maiming.** In the uplands beside Brauvalis, between about 1500 and 1300 BR, several mound courts claimed the same cattle ways, and two Folk claimant houses buried a disputed elder under different royal signs. One court maimed a rival office-holder so that he could no longer walk the return rite at his mound; human reprisals burned the tenant houses of the other side. The settlement left one office vacant for good and divided the cattle route by season. Folk claimants learned the lesson the courts had taught. A ruler who could not perform the rites could not keep the seat, and for generations upland rivals went for a claimant's hands and eyes rather than his heart.

**The Crown-Market.** Later scholars gave the name *Crown-Market* to a horror that recurs at contested coronations from this age onwards. Claimants described bidders in the dark of the coronation night, faces offering victory, beauty, pedigree, vengeance or the right to erase a predecessor, each wanting its gift to define the crown. Those who accepted murdered kin, seized spouses and burned rival genealogies to satisfy what they had bought. The courts that exposed the market often did so after committing the murders that bought them entry.

**The Beaten Animal's Road.** In the western well country a hired seer, riding to curse a household for its enemies, beat a pack animal that would not pass a narrow cutting. Its refusal exposed an ambush and, in the Name-Keepers' telling, a sword-bearing messenger in the road. The seer went home without cursing anyone (see [[The Ahdari before the Covenant]]).

One Menhir court sheltered a household guilty of murder because that household had kept the boundary named in its bargain. Growing towns carried such obligations into the [[Age of City-States]], where larger populations made old promises more valuable and more dangerous.
