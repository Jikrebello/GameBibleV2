# Qahur and the Gateway, c. 2165–350 BR

The relationship between **ancient Qahur and the Gateway country** joined the Sacred Basin to the inner Thalassian Sea between approximately 2165 and 350 BR. Qahur's river economy supported temple estates and repeated traditions of kingship. The Gateway coast possessed several ports, a cultivated hinterland and direct access to maritime exchange. Dry roads, coastal shipping and families with kin in both countries connected their histories.

The two populations had separate origins. Qahuri communities were principally Orphaned survivors of the Giant collapse. Gateway society formed through older coastal inhabitants, Ahdari migrants, maritime settlers, captives and freed people. Centuries of work, marriage and war created shared families and practices, while each country retained its own language, institutions and political life.

Qahuri identity crossed the competing crowns of Tenaret, Qemaret and Sakhret. Gateway ancestry crossed the boundaries of its ports. Qarzethi described the civic population of the dominant republic; Esharati and Sarqeni described other town populations; Hadrathi described a related border people.

## Grain, salt and transport

Qahuri grain, dates, linen and preserved fish travelled towards the coast by river, cart and pack train. Gateway salt, imported timber, metal and ship-borne luxuries moved towards the Basin. A Qemaret merchant might hire Esharat carriers, store goods at Sarqen and ship them through Qarzeth.

Bad floods increased Qahuri demand for imported food and credit. Good floods allowed basin rulers to feed troops and temples beyond the river. Gateway cities used access to shipping in negotiations with inland kings, yet their fleets could not replace a failed harvest.

The roads depended upon wells, animals and local guides. Storms could erase a merchant's investment at sea.

## Qahuri garrisons on the coast

Qahuri garrisons first reached the Gateway coast in the twelfth century BR under the Yasharet kings, whose court was full of Gateway pilots and who paid for the ventures with White Measure loans. The Aramen kings, who took the crown when those loans were called, held selected ports until the ninth century. Qahuri governors demanded harbour dues, hostages and supply, and much of the harbour revenue went upriver to the houses of the dead that had financed the garrisons. They repaired roads, protected some caravans and attempted to impose basin estate practices. Coastal households resisted hereditary labour and foreign temple claims.

Qarzeth preserved its independence through rival coalitions and negotiation with Qemaret. Sarqen and Esharat endured periods of Qahuri garrisoning. Families formed around soldiers, craftspeople and local partners remained after imperial withdrawal.

Gateway writers remembered occupation. Qahuri victory lists described the same ports as eternal possessions. Neither account captured the varied relationships of towns which had resisted one governor and traded willingly with the next.

## Religion along the roads

Qahuri funerary linen, boat images and celestial emblems entered Gateway worship. Shore cults and ancestor forms travelled upriver. Priests constructed divine family trees which joined the powers of the two countries.

White Measure merchants invested in Gateway ships and stores. Funerary objects anchored rooms belonging to Qahuri Second-House Patrons. Coastal clients valued secure storage and ancestral counsel without adopting the complete discipline. Later sale of forgotten anchor objects weakened distant necropolises and drew their Patrons towards the coast.

The Bronze-Faced Provider acquired Qahuri titles in lists compiled during the final centuries of the period. The cult at Qarzeth gave it its organised sacrificial form. Both regions knew the Boatman under local names.

## Divided interests in the Gateway Wars

Qahur never entered the wars as a united state. Rival crowns sold grain, hired soldiers and received envoys from both republics. Qemaret merchants profited from Qarzeth's need. Tenaret temples ransomed captives taken by Atherian fleets. Sakhret officers served abroad and returned with foreign households as well as money and injuries.

During Tashqar Ordan's invasion, c. 612–604 BR, one Basin crown allowed recruitment while another supplied Caleran through intermediaries. Vasharan supervisors taxed both arrangements. These divided interests prevented either belligerent from treating Qahur as a dependable ally.

Qarzeth's destruction sent refugees and enslaved people into basin markets. Some Qahuri families sheltered survivors; others purchased them. Accounts praising ransom existed beside evidence of sale.

## The Successor sea

Eryndor III's conquest and death altered the maritime world as the Gateway Wars began. His fleet commander Serevan established a royal court at Delkos after 684 BR around the king's embalmed body, whose tomb was said to speak in his voice until Qemaret's own embalmers testified against it. The court joined Atherian military speech with Arqeshite administration, Vashari officers and the skills of island and harbour households. Successive rulers lost much of Serevan's territory, but Delkosi ships and officer families remained active across the eastern sea.

Selected Qahuri crowns received foreign garrisons and military marriages during the same succession. These settlements introduced new coin forms and military colonies without replacing the Basin's temple, canal and royal houses. Qahuri funerary work, astronomy and court ceremony travelled towards Delkos through artisans, wives, captives and priests.

The later House of Nerethan descended from one of the Delkosi officer families formed within this Successor world. This inheritance explains the naval titles and court practices brought to Sarqen.

## Lemara, Tazirun and Hesara

The most durable dynastic connection began around 548 BR, when **Lemara of Esharat** married **Prince Tazirun Zaheret**, a junior Qahuri prince involved in the town's carrying trade. Tazirun brought scribes, linen workers, mortuary singers and knowledge of basin ceremony. Lemara controlled the land and stores which made their household powerful.

Their daughter **Hesara** inherited kin in both countries. She concealed children during the siege of Qarzeth, 525–520 BR, and later became Hadrima's mother. Hadrima's marriage to Nerethan of Delkos joined that inheritance to the new government at Sarqen.

The relationship grounded later Nerethan ceremony and royal prestige. Tenaret recognised kinship when useful and rejected Sarqen's broader genealogical claims.

By 350 BR, Caleran controlled Sarqen while Vasharan kings still influenced divided Qahuri crowns. The roads between them carried diplomacy, spies, pilgrims, merchants and limited war into the first imperial generations.
