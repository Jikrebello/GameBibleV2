# Qahur and the Gateway, c. 2165–350 BR

The relationship between **ancient Qahur and the Gateway country** joined the Sacred Basin to the inner Thalassian Sea between approximately 2165 and 350 BR. Qahur's river economy supported temple estates and repeated traditions of kingship. The Gateway coast possessed several ports, a cultivated hinterland and direct access to maritime exchange. Dry roads, coastal shipping and families with kin in both countries connected their histories.

The two populations had separate origins. Qahuri communities were principally Orphaned survivors of the Giant collapse. Gateway society formed through older coastal inhabitants, proto-Ahdari migrants, maritime settlers, captives and freed people. Centuries of work, marriage and war created shared families and practices, while each country retained its own language, institutions and political life.

## Grain, salt and transport

Qahuri grain, dates, linen and preserved fish travelled towards the coast by river, cart and pack train. Gateway salt, imported timber, metal and ship-borne luxuries moved towards the Basin. A Qemaret merchant might hire Esharat carriers, store goods at Sarqen and ship them through Qarzeth.

Bad floods increased Qahuri demand for imported food and credit. Good floods allowed basin rulers to feed troops and temples beyond the river. Gateway cities used access to shipping in negotiations with inland kings, yet their fleets could not replace a failed harvest.

The roads depended upon wells, animals and local guides. Storms could erase a merchant's investment at sea. No single ruler controlled the complete chain linking river field to foreign harbour.

## Qahuri garrisons on the coast

The Aramen empire reached selected Gateway ports between the eleventh and ninth centuries BR. Qahuri governors demanded harbour dues, hostages and supply. They repaired roads, protected some caravans and attempted to impose basin estate practices. Coastal households resisted hereditary labour and foreign temple claims.

Qarzeth preserved its independence through rival coalitions and negotiation with Qemaret. Sarqen and Esharat endured periods of Qahuri garrisoning. Families formed around soldiers, craftspeople and local partners remained after imperial withdrawal.

Gateway writers remembered occupation. Qahuri victory lists described the same ports as eternal possessions. Neither account captured the varied relationships of towns which had resisted one governor and traded willingly with the next.

## Religion along the roads

Qahuri funerary linen, boat images and celestial emblems entered Gateway worship. Shore cults and ancestor forms travelled upriver. Priests constructed divine family trees which joined powers whose actual identities remained separate.

White Measure merchants invested in Gateway ships and stores. Funerary objects anchored rooms belonging to Qahuri Second-House Patrons. Coastal clients valued secure storage and ancestral counsel without adopting the complete discipline. Later sale of forgotten anchor objects weakened distant necropolises and drew their Patrons towards the coast.

The Bronze-Faced Provider acquired Qahuri titles in lists compiled during the final centuries of the period. The cult at Qarzeth gave this dependent Gluttony Mask its organised sacrificial form; the titles did not make it an Ishkatari god or river Menhir. The Boatman appeared in both regions under local names, although neither priesthood possessed authority over him.

## Divided interests in the Gateway Wars

Qahur never entered the wars as a united state. Rival crowns sold grain, hired soldiers and received envoys from both republics. Qemaret merchants profited from Qarzeth's need. Tenaret temples ransomed captives taken by Atherian fleets. Sakhret officers served abroad and returned with foreign households as well as money and injuries.

During Zamaret Ordan's invasion, one basin crown allowed recruitment while another supplied Caleran through intermediaries. High-Road supervisors taxed both arrangements. These divided interests prevented either belligerent from treating Qahur as a dependable ally.

Qarzeth's destruction sent refugees and enslaved people into basin markets. Some Qahuri families sheltered survivors; others purchased them. Accounts praising ransom existed beside evidence of sale.

## Lemara, Tazirun and Hesara

The most durable dynastic connection began around 548 BR, when **Lemara of Esharat** married **Prince Tazirun Zaheret**, a junior Qahuri prince involved in the town's carrying trade. Tazirun brought scribes, linen workers, mortuary singers and knowledge of basin ceremony. Lemara controlled the land and stores which made their household powerful.

Their daughter **Hesara** inherited kin in both countries. She concealed children during Qarzeth's Years of Three Collections and later became Hadrima's mother. Hadrima's marriage to Nerethan of Delkos joined that inheritance to the new government at Sarqen.

The relationship grounded later Nerethan ceremony and royal prestige. It conveyed no claim to rule the Sacred Basin. Tenaret recognised kinship when useful and rejected Sarqen's broader genealogical claims.

By 350 BR, Caleran controlled Sarqen while High-Road kings still influenced divided Qahuri crowns. The roads between them carried diplomacy, spies, pilgrims, merchants and limited war into the first imperial generations.
