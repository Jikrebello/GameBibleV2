# Historical Atlas of Vathra and the Northern Ground

The **northern ground** extends from the Glassward approaches and the western well country to the seasonal lakes and colder grasslands of the east. Qahur lies beyond its southern approaches. Roads from Qiryath and the Arqeshite river cities enter it from the south-east, while the northern Copper Stair passes lead towards mining districts and the farther interior. Water, winter pasture, safe crossings and the reach of armed followers gave its political boundaries their practical form.

Between the twenty-fourth and fourth centuries BR, the region passed through two great transformations. Vathra first joined its wells and grazing country beneath flying Giant courts. The [[Vathra and the Great Unyoking|Great Unyoking]] destroyed that dominion and returned control of movement to dispersed human communities. The ancient Tarkhan developed among Orphaned survivors. The Ahdari emerged later from the Near-Glass camp continuum and older well-country populations.

These peoples shared roads, marriages, fosterage, markets, raids and captivity. They retained different relationships to the Glass refuge. Tarkhan ancestry arose principally outside it. The Ahdari carried a strong inheritance from the people formed within it.

## The country beneath Vathra

Vathra governed vertically. Its crown-cities crossed the Material sky along prepared currents, while a population many times larger maintained anchor towers, wells, herds, fields, quarries and workshops below. The western system depended on the Hundred Wells of Narash and smaller cistern chains near the Glassward country. The central road crossed the deep-water stations later associated with Tergai. Farther east, seasonal lakes and open grass supported cattle, sheep, hunting and a small number of royal horse estates.

The sky cities required regular descent. Their hollow foundations needed timber, bone, metal and stone. Great woven mantles caught and circulated atmospheric pressure. Anchors joined each city to known landing places. Human pilots, weavers and route callers carried out the work which made departure possible. Most food and water reached the landing fields by camel train.

Vathran officials divided their human subjects by work and obligation. Narashi caravan crowns controlled water, sanctuary and bride exchange along western roads. Tether-town households supplied builders, smiths and mantle workers. Eastern camps moved royal animals between pasture and winter shelter. Captive workers came from the Copper Stairs, the southern basins and defeated northern communities. These populations spoke several languages and possessed histories older than the aerial kingdom.

## The War of Altitude, c. 2300–2260 BR

Vathra's last succession opened during the wider crisis of the Great Unbinding. **Ozhur IV**, ruler of the high crown-city Urzavad, demanded additional food, ballast and workers after familiar sky roads began to fail. His cousin **Varakesh of Dazhur** claimed that the lower cloud houses could preserve the kingdom by taking control of the route calendar. Each court stripped the stations below its rivals. A well could supply one city in spring and lose its remaining animals to another in summer.

The dispute became the **War of Altitude**. War-platforms bombarded anchor towers. False beacons drew rival cities towards thin air and violent weather. Giant rulers withheld rain from districts which supplied the opposing side. Dazhur cut loose an inhabited lower ward to preserve its lift; the falling houses killed the people inside and crushed part of the tether town below. Urzavad answered by destroying three western landing fields and executing their human crews.

Resistance joined several existing networks. **Seyra Vatan**, a human mantle-weaver raised in Kel-Orun, distributed altered knot patterns among the weaving houses. **Dorun of Tal-Kesh**, a route-master whose children had been divided between two royal households, coordinated drivers at the central anchors. **Meya of Narash** removed breeding camels from the tribute count and supplied camps leaving the western road. Enslaved metalworkers forged knives capable of severing mooring cable under tension.

The first open revolt began when Ozhur ordered Tal-Kesh emptied to lighten Urzavad's approach. Drivers cut the animal traces and opened the royal corrals. Families attacked overseers, seized stores and abandoned four anchors on the same night. Giant clients killed several hundred people at the eastern pens. Rebels retaliated against tether-house families, including children and human servants who had taken no part in the punishment.

## The last descents

Urzavad struck a rival platform above Tal-Kesh and fell across the landing country. The crash killed Ozhur, most of his court and thousands of humans in the city and below it. Treasure stories place the heart of Vathra's First Road-Mother beneath the ruined central tower. No reliable account confirms the claim.

Kel-Orun descended under the control of Seyra's pilots and the Dynastic Giant **Kelvara**, who turned against her royal kin after the massacre. Two foundation chambers split during the landing and killed hundreds. Most inhabitants escaped. Later settlements grew among the cisterns and metal ribs; a processional court became a market, and people quarried palaces for houses and wells.

Dazhur survived the battle in which it discarded its ward, then disappeared from dependable records within three years. Sairat entered a route after rebels destroyed its destination anchor. Molqara lost steering while part of its lift remained active. Yazuran attempted an unknown passage and left no confirmed wreck. Northern stories still claim sightings of all four cities. The stories disagree over whether they describe original structures, broken route images, copied processions or ordinary lights in difficult weather.

Vathra ended because no surviving house could reconnect court, food, movement and obedience across the northern system. Individual Giants, grounded buildings, human specialists and old instruments endured. Their survival did not restore the kingdom.

## The Grounding, c. 2260–2050 BR

The first post-Vathran generations lived among useful and dangerous remains. People stripped grounded buildings for cistern linings, roof stone and metal. Wind mantles became tents, saddle cloths and burial covers. A royal carpet could rise during the correct weather and drag an untrained rider towards its former destination. Most fragments had lost every trace of flight.

Human communities reorganised around what had endured. Narashi well houses kept camel herds and long-distance knowledge. Tether-town survivors retained metalwork, masonry and textile skills. Eastern families rebuilt cattle and sheep herds from animals released during the revolt. Hunters crossed districts where fallen weapons still answered movement from above.

The old royal horses nearly disappeared. Vathra had concentrated its best animals at upland estates and military platforms, where fodder, healers and breeding records could be controlled. Horses died in crashes, were eaten during sieges, were taken south by neighbouring rulers or failed to reproduce in the harsher open country. A few chariot houses preserved small lines. Ordinary movement relied upon feet, carts, sledges, donkeys and camels.

Freedom carried severe material costs. Anchor towns no longer distributed grain, and several escaped camps died during the first winters. Armed bands took animals from poorly defended settlements. Former officers tried to restore tribute under human rulers. Human coalitions defeated most surviving Giant enclaves over the following two centuries.

## The Near-Glass encounter, c. 2050–1750 BR

The people who remained near the Glass after the southern departure continued to use offices inherited from the Sojourn. Judges travelled between camps, provisioners supervised common stores, and household elders maintained burial and marriage relationships. The Prophet's commission passed to none of them.

Their northern neighbours already possessed wells, caravan circuits and memories of Vathra. Narashi guides sold passage. Tarkhan hunters served as escorts, stole herds and took refugees into fosterage. Near-Glass judges heard disputes involving several roads; well rulers enforced access at a particular cistern with walls and armed retainers.

The difference widened across generations. Families committed to travelling judgement and dispersed custody entered the early Jirahar world. Families committed to defended water, guest protection and territorial oath formed the Ahdari core. Older inhabitants joined the latter through marriage, adoption, service, manumission and public acceptance of common obligations.

The story called [[G-59 - The Walking Court and the Wall That Stayed|The Walking Court and the Wall That Stayed]] preserves one local choice during this fission. Part of a threatened camp carried its court away. Another part built a wall around food and graves. Families continued to marry across the two institutions.

## Camel roads and first assemblies, c. 1900–1450 BR

Western camel breeding enlarged the distance a household could cross between dependable waters. Camels carried grain, salt, cloth, ore and passengers. Donkeys and carts remained valuable on shorter roads. A large caravan required investors, drivers, guards, cooks, healers, water knowledge and agreements with the people controlling each stage.

The Nine Mother-Wells of Tergai were deepened over several generations by Orphaned well houses, former tether-town workers and allied camps. Tarkhan-speaking communities maintained most outer stations. Ahdari households also worked and buried relatives there. Rights attached to labour, graves, seasonal use and negotiated protection.

The old Narashi crowns lost much of their Giant-age authority while preserving influential maternal houses. Their marriage networks joined camel owners, gardeners, ransom brokers and sanctuary keepers. Some crown-mothers protected fugitives. Others sold captives or used sanctuary debts to compel years of service. The Hundred Wells remained a chain of related jurisdictions.

Eastern cattle and sheep families gathered at seasonal lake shores. They raced carts, exchanged foster children, arranged marriages and heard singers recite the Great Unyoking. Assemblies settled access to winter shelter and organised retaliation against raiders. A leader's following depended upon food, gifts and judgement.

## The Breaking of the Bronze Roads

Northern routes belonged to a wider exchange world connecting Qahur, coastal ports, eastern river cities and the Copper Stairs. Bronze work depended upon metal moving over long distances. Royal chariot houses required imported fittings, wheelwrights and concentrated fodder.

Poor floods, drought, royal seizure and unpaid soldiers broke much of this system between approximately 1450 and 1325 BR. Armed carriers kept cargo intended for failing palaces. Palace guards took food from villages to preserve metal shipments. Raiders attacked convoys already divided by disputes over payment.

At **Daram Ford**, the chariot lord **Boran Tall-Hand** ordered porters to discard grain so that his teams could carry copper through mud. The porters killed him and distributed the grain among nearby settlements. Rival captains divided his horses, and most of the lines disappeared within a generation. Later Tarkhan songs called the episode the **Feast of the Copper Wheels**.

Western communities survived most successfully where families still possessed camels, goats, gardens and short local routes. Some protectors guarded dependants through the drought. Others sold slaves and poorer kin before surrendering their own herds.

## Hearth crowns and market roads, c. 1325–800 BR

Smaller political worlds followed the collapse. Iron tools spread gradually from eastern and upland centres. Settlements repaired short roads between dependable waters. Taleri smiths worked around grounded Vathran structures. Elkari herders moved through the eastern seasonal lakes. Tergai and Narashi houses connected them to the west.

The broad names **Tarkhan** and **Ahdari** became useful during these centuries. Tarkhan joined several related northern communities whose speech, fosterage and assemblies had grown mutually intelligible. Ahdari described houses admitted to shared protection across western wells and markets. Both names coexisted with local identities.

Around 1280 BR, the protector **Harun Savel** concealed spearheads beneath date sacks during the protected market at Saqara Well. His followers attacked after a satirist accused him of purchasing peace. They killed merchants, pilgrims, slaves and children who had entered without armour. Survivors abandoned the market for nearly fifty years. Traffic moved towards the poorer wells of **Mazar**, whose storehouses and repair yards grew around the safer route.

At **Kor Ashal**, an oasis ruler answered three failed seasons by killing a debtor family's child beneath a high stone. The well remained brackish. When the child's mother broke the altar, the ruler executed her. Caravan houses sheltered her surviving daughter and abandoned the oasis. The cult and settlement declined together.

## Arqeshite clients and Vasharan roads, c. 800–520 BR

Arqesh extended power into the open country through garrisons at dependable water, royal gifts, grain payments and hostages. Its officers could not hold the deep interior without local cooperation.

The cousins **Savan Uruz** and **Demer Khol** became the principal clients of the period. Savan accepted an Arqeshite crown and annual grain in return for guarding the north-eastern approach. Demer controlled western camel stages with Qahuri support. They exchanged hostages, intermarried and raided households beyond the districts which paid them.

Arqeshite payments failed during the succession struggles after 706 BR. Savan's soldiers killed him when he seized grain from their families. Demer tried to absorb the unpaid companies and was defeated by camps whose relatives he had sold. The courts disappeared, leaving trained soldiers, poets, smiths and mixed households across the region.

Tarkhan leaders joined the coalition against Arqesh for different reasons. **Yalun of the White Camel** sought hostages held at Durqesh. **Marat Two-Bows** wanted the imperial herds and accepted Vasharan gifts. Yalun released captives from several peoples. Marat sold captured grooms after the victory. Arezan Tiravan recognised both men as local protectors, but Vashar gained no general sovereignty over the northern ground.

## Rethmar and the Atherian approach

Rethmar grew around winter water, storage and a market where western circuits met. Permanent gardeners, smiths and water keepers lived beside temporary compounds used by visiting houses. Tarkhan and Ahdari communities heard disputes through their own customs and invited mediators when a case crossed communal lines.

In 381 BR, the Atherian financier and commander [[Lethren Ostar]] marched towards Rethmar. He expected local leaders to provide water, animals and passage. **Kharuz of the Stone Wells**, a Tarkhan leader, organised foot archers and camel scouts. **Tavisha**, an Ahdari water keeper, moved stores away from the line of advance and marked false camps with empty skins.

Ostar's columns reached dry wells and were attacked before they could reunite. Ostar and his son died. The victors took soldiers and camp followers captive. Fever entered the prisoner camp while families attempted to raise ransom. Tavisha secured the release of sick captives and water workers; Kharuz exchanged several officers for weapons and animals. Other captives entered local households as slaves when payment failed.

The coalition divided after preserving Rethmar. Tavisha's authority concerned water and the people depending upon it. Kharuz commanded those who had followed him to war. Neither office created a northern crown.

## The balance in 350 BR

The northern world at Atheria's imperial threshold was populous, skilled and divided. Rethmar guarded the western approach through cooperation among several communities. Tergai joined ancestral Tarkhan wells to roads used by Ahdari and Jirahar travellers. Narashi houses maintained older western connections. Eastern Tarkhan assemblies dealt with Vasharan clients and remnants of Arqeshite stations. Fallen Vathran structures supplied building material, danger and political memory.

No army could master this country through one victory. It had to carry water, secure animals, bargain with local keepers and prevent guides from changing sides. Ostar's defeat demonstrated the cost of neglecting those requirements. Thalion inherited that lesson when he began turning Caleran power into an empire.
