# Age of Exodus

The Age of Exodus, approximately c. 2300–2160 BR, begins when the Great Prophet's commissioned movement becomes the center of the [[The Great Unbinding|Great Unbinding]]. The Prophet gathers the largest deliberate convergence of liberated human communities and leads that representative remnant into the [[Great Glass Desert]]. During the century-long [[The Glass Sojourn|Glass Sojourn]], the mixed host becomes one people before dividing after the Prophet's death. It is not the only place human communities survive the end of Giant rule.

## The Liberation

The Prophet does not merely defeat tyrants. He exposes them. Giant kings who call themselves gods are shown to be corrupt, contingent, and breakable. Their cities fall through a providential convergence of prophetic warning, plagues, human revolt, client defection, Giant civil war, divine judgement, internal rot, failed escape, and the collapse of the systems used to bind human beings.

The Prophet announces, interprets, intercedes, prepares, and leads. He is not the metaphysical source of the plagues and cannot deploy Heaven as a personal army. Giant rulers remain responsible for the wars they choose, while human liberation depends upon the work of households, scouts, burial fraternities, artisans, soldiers, officials, and subject communities as well as the Prophet at their head.

Four Imperial Horizons are broken directly in liberation memory. Anshara destroys itself concurrently in the Drinking Wars and receives no authorially fixed prophetic plague cycle.

## The Glass Refuge

The Glass predates the Prophet, his plagues, and the Giants' attempts to approach it. Giant powers left failed marginal incursions but never established a permanent deep-Glass settlement. The Lord deliberately sends the host into it as shelter while the Giant world continues collapsing outside.

The ordered camp becomes a dry ark: people, animals, seed, tools, medicine, records, law and living crafts survive through both high-sign provision and ordinary material labor. Anchor camps become long occupations rather than momentary way-stations. Common worship, law, rationing, service, language, intermarriage and burial form the refugees into one people over approximately a century.

The sojourn does not explain what the Glass is or make its miracles repeatable. The later Ark-derived traditions remember the common inheritance differently, although they do not separate simultaneously at the outward threshold:

- the Jirahar remember commandments, names, routes, and failed obedience;
- the Ahl-e-Ahd remember water, oath, provision, and covenant;
- Atherian descendants remember liberation but often sand away the Prophet's harder demands;

Folk, Ättar, Tarkhan and Nsizwa memory arises principally from Orphaned survival outside the Ark. Sestaran, Vesari and Eclessian traditions inherit Glass material through later Braided histories rather than direct descent from one separate Ark branch.

## The Prophet's Failure and Death

By the end of the sojourn, the Prophet is the last living human with direct memory of the Giant world. His death around c. 2165 BR is one of the great disputed wounds of history. Some traditions call it punishment for refusing the southern sanctuary meant to shelter the people for three generations. Others call it murder, tragic exhaustion, or a mystery no nation has the right to simplify.

## Inherited Wound

The host is freed and genuinely formed before it fractures. Common law, institutions and memory mature in the Glass, but the people cannot carry that unity securely into land, succession and possession after the Prophet's death. Southern war-settlement leagues separate first; the continuing Near-Glass population divides generations later into proto-Jirahar and proto-Ahl-e-Ahd formations. All three later inherit both deliverance and fracture; other peoples remember the event through Orphaned or later Braided histories.

Human communities outside the principal host are not rejected humanity. Some revolt after the central columns pass, remain to preserve a failing city, survive in isolated regions, or enter separate successor histories. Later cultures may claim exclusive descent from the Exodus, but no one surviving genealogy contains the whole end of the Giant Age.
