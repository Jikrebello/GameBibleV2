# The Hadrathi Border People

The **Hadrathi** inhabited the drier country behind the Gateway coast. Their settlements guarded wells, grazing ground and the roads connecting inland markets with Esharat, Qarzeth and Sarqen. They belonged to the wider Gateway cultural world while maintaining a distinct border identity shaped by local households and kings.

Hadrathi descent combined older shore-interior populations with migrants and captives absorbed over many generations. Belonging rested on acknowledged kin, land use, military service and admission to a settlement. Marriage connected well households to carrying families and coastal merchants. Fosterage between allied houses trained children in another district and helped secure agreements. Enslaved labourers and prisoners lived within these households without gaining equal membership through service alone.

Hadrathi communities spoke related Gateway forms and used coastal, Qahuri and inland languages in trade. Household worship centred on ancestors, wells and the safety of roads. Merchants and soldiers introduced coastal cults, including forms associated with Qarzeth, but no single sanctuary governed the border people.

## Country and livelihood

The border supported mixed farming, herding and caravan work. A settlement required dependable access to water, animals capable of carrying goods and agreements with neighbours controlling the next stage of a road. Hadrathi guides knew seasonal pasture and the points where flash floods could destroy a crossing. Coastal rulers valued this knowledge and repeatedly tried to place their own garrisons beside the principal wells.

Local kings derived authority from the protection of roads and the distribution of water during scarcity. Their military households collected tolls, settled quarrels and organised defence. The same powers allowed them to seize animals, compel labour and favour their dependants over older claimants. Royal allegiance could change after defeat or marriage while the Hadrathi population and its local relationships continued.

## Relations with Qarzeth and Sarqen

Qarzeth treated Hadrath as a source of soldiers, pack animals and control over inland traffic. Hadrathi houses entered its armies and trade, yet resented tribute and the appointment of coastal favourites. The Late Council's child collections sharpened those divisions. Some border officials carried out demands; others warned households or diverted collectors towards rival districts.

During the last Gateway War, Hadrathi factions chose different sides. The destruction of Qarzeth removed a dominant creditor and employer while exposing inland roads to raiding and confiscation. Sarqen's rising kings secured alliances through marriage and military aid. Hadrathi rulers kept their local titles and customs within these alliances.

By 350 BR the Hadrathi formed a recognised border people under several competing houses. Their exact political course after the Gateway Wars remained contested among the surviving dynasties. Their separate identity, road knowledge and relationships with the Spared Towns were firmly established.
