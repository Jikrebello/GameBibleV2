# Elmaric Island Peoples before 350 BR

The Elmaric peoples were related but politically separate island communities of the Thalassian inner sea. By 350 BR they inhabited a central network of archipelagos, shoals, harbours and larger islands stretching between the [[Elmarai Isles before 350 BR|Elmarai Isles]] and [[Seravon before 350 BR|Seravon]]. Shared speech, kinship, seafaring and sacred meeting places made the family recognisable; citizenship and loyalty ordinarily belonged to a particular island, harbour or household. No ancient Elmaric state ruled the whole sea.

Local human populations had occupied these islands under and beyond the reach of Giant powers. Their descendants were the principal roots of the Elmaric world. Later Folk kindreds from the south and Glass-descended voyagers settled in particular communities after the Ark dispersals began. Newcomers joined older households by agreement, marriage, dependency and force. Neither migration nor conquest made the islands an empty colonial frontier.

The broader name is a useful historical and linguistic classification. Islanders ordinarily named their own harbour, island, lineage or inland district first. The Elmarai were one prominent archipelago, not the owners of every related island. Kin could cross political borders; so could war. The [[Historical Atlas of the Elmaric Sea before 350 BR]] follows the chronology and the changing relationships with mainland powers.

## Formation and regional branches

The Giant collapse reached an already inhabited sea. Some human communities had supplied Giant-linked harbours; others lived beyond the dependable reach of any imperial estate. The Unbinding damaged stores and crossings, but islanders repaired cisterns, terraces, fisheries and boats. Repeated work on the same slopes and landings gave their descendants durable claims. Short voyages for food and marriage joined survivor communities before an Elmaric name described them.

The later southern arrivals did not form one fleet. Folk coastal kindreds, refugees, boat families and marriage groups entered particular islands. Glass-descended voyagers sometimes stopped at a useful harbour, while others continued towards settlements that became Atherian. In one port a newcomer house might gain land by agreement; in another it built a fortified quarter and seized grain. Over generations many descendants entered local language and kin networks. Southern-facing coasts retained stronger Folk ties, while selected outer ports preserved Glass-derived rites, names or origin claims. Those differences did not make the heartland three unmixed ancestral blocs.

The most closely connected homeland included the Elmarai archipelago, Ilamar's sanctuary and the [[Shoal and Lagoon Islands before 350 BR|low shoal-and-lagoon islands]] whose pilots worked shallow water. Its boundaries were routes and reciprocal obligations rather than a border. Large Seravon also held locally rooted Elmaric peoples, but its coasts and interior produced several distinct polities. [[Near-Mainland Harbour Islands before 350 BR|Near-mainland islands]] formed mixed societies with fields, relatives and sacred obligations on the opposite shore. Those places were particularly exposed to foreign garrisons and claims, while a central island could also be threatened if another power controlled its grain.

## Land, water and households

Small crossings joined communities with different strengths. Some had good grazing or orchards, others sheltered landings, shallow-water pilotage or stores for passing ships. Cisterns, terraces, fishing and seasonal movement supported households, but denser harbours needed grain from opposite shores and selected outer islands. Timber, pitch and masts also came by sea. Losing a supply route could therefore turn a minor channel into a matter of famine or war.

Island food came from several kinds of work rather than fishing alone. Terraced plots and orchards needed water carried and stored through dry seasons. Herds used ground that could not bear grain; fishing crews depended on people drying, repairing and distributing their catch. A larger harbour concentrated shipwrights, pilots, storekeepers, carriers and families who could not all leave for a harvest. It required regular shipments, reserves and agreements with growers beyond its own jurisdiction.

The heartland secured grain through both treaty purchase and outer holdings. Some opposite-shore districts retained their earlier cultivators under rent or tribute; others gained Elmaric settler farms or fortified stores by grant or seizure. Enslaved people worked alongside free farmers in some places. A treaty partner could withhold food in war, while a colonial grain port might draw its sponsor into conflict with residents and rival states. Timber, pitch and masts came from wooded islands and mainlands, making even a strong fleet dependent on other people's land and labour.

Fosterage, adoption, marriage and seasonal crewing joined kin across islands. Captivity joined them too. Raiders seized men, women and children for ransom, sale, labour and incorporation into households. Captured women were sometimes retained as concubines, coerced wives or reproductive dependants; some island authorities distributed captives among recognised families. An acknowledged child might acquire a household's standing while the mother remained enslaved. These practices helped form mixed populations without making the violence consensual. Local rules differed, and no single marriage or captive law governed every island.

## Membership, coercion and family power

A household could include children by birth or adoption, fosterlings from another island, working crews, freed dependants and enslaved people. Recognition by a citizen household was a common route into island belonging for a child of captive origin. It could give access to inheritance, protected rites and future crew or civic service. The same rule gave powerful adults the means to deny acknowledgement to another child and leave that child dependent or saleable. Maternal status and the terms of a union mattered differently from island to island.

In many places captains surrendered a recognised portion of captives to an island, harbour or lineage authority, which allocated them by household loss, labour demand, patronage and perceived demographic need. Elsewhere captors retained greater personal control. A smaller number of islands recorded ancestry and assigned or prohibited unions for supposed inherited qualities. Those official theories shaped lives but are not proof that the qualities followed the rulers' categories. Captive women and descendants transmitted language, skills and rites, sought protection or escape and made claims upon relatives even while a later household song called them honoured foreign mothers. The song could be true about their influence and false about their freedom.

Local disagreements mattered. An island that rejected official assigned unions might still tolerate concubinage. A sanctuary court could refuse to recognise a marriage forced under one harbour's law and nevertheless arrange the woman's return as a captive. Island kinship therefore supported common identity while exposing sharp differences in rights and in the treatment of vulnerable people.

## Harbours, courts and warfare

The [[Ilamar before 350 BR|sanctuary island of Ilamar]] provided an independent place to witness oaths, measure grain, arrange safe conduct and negotiate ransom. Its custodians could make an agreement credible to enemies but could not compel the islands as a national government. A fugitive protected from immediate seizure within its precinct could still be returned, exchanged or ransomed.

Custodians certified weights and cargo records, heard losses on shared convoys and proclaimed protected conduct during famine. A ruler with grain or a captain with ships could press for better terms, so the court's independence required bargaining rather than an assumption of impartial power. Ilamar's shrines belonged to several traditions. Thalassor's followers could attend without making the Menhir patron of all island law.

Local councils, ship-owning associations, dynastic houses and temporary leagues organised different parts of the sea. [[Seravon before 350 BR|Seravon]] contained several polities, with prominent dynastic houses and older shore communities. Western and Saronikan-facing routes more often relied on civic councils and ship-owning groups. These were tendencies within a varied region, not a division into two already formed modern nations. Professional crews also raided, escorted, traded or served foreign wars, sometimes changing work within one career.

Leagues could protect a convoy for one season, share a harbour during a shortage or unite against an outside fleet. They did not appoint every island's magistrates or replace its law. Rival Seravon dynasties used marriage, captives, sanctuary recognition, ships and stores to bind their own districts. Farther west, civic councils and ship-owning coalitions were influential in the later Saronikan-facing region. The striking later reversal—a Sermoran republic growing from a more dynastic ancient zone and a Saronikan kingdom overlaying older councils—belongs to subsequent centuries, not an inevitable ancient programme.

Independent captain-led share crews were a characteristic form of raiding. A captain gathered experienced sailors and investors for a voyage, agreed on provisions, losses and prize shares, then might hire a different crew for escort or trade. Dynasts and merchants also financed attacks. Warrants naming an enemy or a debt affected where a raider could sell cargo or seek protection; they did not erase the harm to seized coastal households. Nor did this occupation describe every Elmaric sailor.

During the Gateway Wars, c. 650–520 BR, Elmaric crews served Caleran, the Gateway Republic or their own captains. Families at different ports supported opposing sides. After Qarzeth fell in 520 BR, Caleran hired or pardoned some captains and hunted others as pirates. Its strongest direct footholds lay along the Gateway-facing outer approaches. Eastern channels remained a patchwork of treaties, paid crews and contested ports. No foreign fleet conquered the central heartland as a whole.

The two outer theatres imposed different pressures. [[Gateway-Facing Outer Approaches before 350 BR|Gateway-facing routes]] connected to Qarzeth, Sarqen and Caleran. The [[Eastern Channels before 350 BR|Delkosi–Vashari-facing channels]] met eastern kings, Atherian-speaking ports and older coastal populations. Kin and captives crossed between them, but a fleet fighting on one side of a war did not bind its distant relatives. Caleran's selective postwar pardons rewarded useful pilots and well-patronised captains while less protected former opponents could be condemned for similar service. Rival Elmaric ports and foreign states continued to offer shelter or work.

## Cult, memory and sea knowledge

Household and harbour rites remained local. Ilamar's plural precinct made cooperation possible without proving that every island revered one patron in the same way. Thalassor's followers were present in the region, and the exceptional covenant behind [[Marelith]] changed one city's life, but its later tribute cannot define Elmaric religion as a whole. Cult, civic membership and ship service crossed each other in different patterns.

Pilot knowledge was a form of power. A shoal community could guide vessels through a channel that a distant ruler could neither survey quickly nor hold with a fort. Knowledge travelled through practice, fostering and crewing, not merely through written charts. It could protect a food convoy, help a raiding party or make a small island valuable to a mainland fleet. An islander who changed service did not thereby surrender knowledge of home waters or acquire a new ancestry.

Origin traditions remembered recovered harbours, foreign mothers, storms and victorious crews. They carried real memories in selective form. The harbour later absorbed into Sermora was remembered as founded first by settlers who seized shore and grain, then by older shore houses, returned exiles, escaped captives, defectors and pilots who defeated a punitive fleet. Each commemoration honoured one claim to civic precedence and risked suppressing participants who did not fit it. [[Caelovar]] and the [[Black Reef]] likewise entered songs of a drowned and a burned city, but they were separate sites with different populations and dates.

## The boundary at 350 BR

By 350 BR Elmaric islanders remained numerous in the central sea and were the largest cultural family in the Seravon zone. Their settlements also met older Atherian-speaking port worlds on the Saronikan side. [[Arkenan and Saronikan Communities before 350 BR]] describes those mixed eastern harbours; their civic history should not be mistaken for the ancestry of every island household. Neither the later [[The Sermoran Republic Overview|Sermoran Republic]] nor the [[The Maritime Kingdom of Saronika Overview|Saronikan kingdom]] yet existed.

The drowned remains of [[Caelovar]] and the burned [[Black Reef]] were separate hazards and memories within this wider sea. Their histories did not make all Elmaric peoples subjects of one lost civilisation. The island network entered Atheria's imperial age divided, dependent on outside supplies and capable of resisting or bargaining through its many ports.

Several blocs could pool ships for a convoy, famine or war; none ruled all the islands. Caleran had selected outer footholds, strongest towards the Gateway. Ilamar retained its precarious autonomy. Seravon and its neighbouring chain remained politically separate; the Elmarai and shoal communities remained cultural heartlands with their own governments. The coming imperial expansion would change each relationship differently. At 350 BR it had not already made Sermorans, Saronikans or a Sestaran Concord out of the whole sea.
