# The Three Regions of Ancient Qahur

Ancient Qahur contained three enduring regional centres: **Tenaret** on the middle river, **Qemaret** across the lower fan and **Sakhret** near the stone country and southern reaches. Their inhabitants belonged to the wider Qahuri civilisation. Royal rivalry, different landscapes and centuries of local institutions gave each region a distinct public identity.

The demonyms **Tenareti**, **Qemareti** and **Sakhreti** are conventional translations of the forms used in later Basin records. Earlier people more often named a town, water district, temple, occupation or household. A change of crown could alter a family's taxes and military obligations without changing these local affiliations.

## Tenaret and the middle river

Tenaret drew wealth from old spillways, cultivated banks and traffic moving between the upper and lower Basin. Households commonly described themselves through the chain of channels and landing places they maintained. Flood work required neighbouring villages to clear silt, mend banks and decide which fields received scarce water. Royal officers could coordinate this labour over a wide district, though they also seized workers for palaces, armies and mortuary estates.

The Senareth and Zaheret houses drew much of their prestige from Tenaret. Service at their courts gave some temple, military and scribal families recognition beyond the middle river. Other communities remembered royal reunification as the arrival of collectors, forced labour gangs and soldiers demanding grain.

## Qemaret and the lower fan

Qemaret stood among branching waterways, fertile grain districts and routes leading towards the sea. Its boat households, cultivators and merchants depended on constant negotiation over channels and transport. The lower fan received immigrants readily because seasonal work and maritime trade created demand for labour. Residence alone did not confer the standing of a recognised free household. Marriage, manumission, temple admission and service to a district could establish that standing over time.

Qemareti rulers frequently claimed to reunite the Two Reeds from the abundance of the lower country. Their wealth supported temples, craft quarters and fleets. It also financed campaigns against Tenaret and the seizure of workers from defeated districts.

## Sakhret and the stone approaches

Sakhret connected the Basin to quarries, desert approaches and the southern river. Quarry households, caravan workers, soldiers and cultivators met in its markets. The region supplied stone and metal for royal building, including the mortuary estates that consumed increasing land and labour during several dynasties.

Sakhreti communities developed strong ties between military protection and access to water. Frontier service admitted some newcomers to local households and estates. Captives and debtors worked quarries under compulsion. A ruler who controlled the approaches could claim a royal title without commanding Tenaret or Qemaret, giving the Basin periods with several simultaneous crowns.

## One civilisation under several crowns

The Twin-Reed ideal represented a united river under a ruler who protected both upper and lower life. Actual history included repeated division, conquest and negotiated reunion. Foreign authors sometimes described the three regions as separate peoples. Royal texts made the opposite mistake by presenting Qahur as permanently united.

Qahuri households crossed these political divisions through marriage, pilgrimage, boat traffic and craft. A Tenareti family could serve a Qemareti king while retaining its middle-river tombs. A Sakhreti soldier could settle in the lower fan. Temple communities recognised related practices while disputing precedence and royal patronage.

By 350 BR, the three regional identities remained strong under separate crowns and Vasharan pressure. None constituted a distinct ancestry. Their rivalry belonged to the political and regional life of one broad river civilisation.
