# Ubar Canal Peoples and the Arqeshites

The river cities of Arqesh, Nemeru, Khalbar and Durqesh belonged to related Orphaned populations joined by canals, cultivation and trade. Their inhabitants ordinarily named a city, neighbourhood, temple or water district. House Ubar united much of this world through conquest and administration, creating an Arqeshite imperial culture that outlived the dynasty.

## The canal peoples

Settlement depended on the annual repair of embankments, channels and lifting works. Cultivators, boat crews, surveyors, potters, metalworkers and animal keepers relied upon one another. City councils and temple estates organised labour locally. Kings enlarged the system, assigned water and moved workers between districts.

Belonging arose through recognised household and civic residence. Marriage linked farming villages to urban trades. Temples adopted dependants and trained specialists. Manumitted people could establish households, though former masters and creditors often retained claims. Captives from wars worked estates, palaces and canal gangs without becoming members of the free civic population merely through residence.

Nemeru, Khalbar and Durqesh possessed their own histories and civic pride. Royal officials called them parts of one river order; local people remembered periods of resistance, collaboration and advantage. **Ubar canal peoples** is the encyclopaedia's collective name for these related civic populations.

## Arqeshite identity

An **Arqeshite** was first a person of Arqesh. Imperial expansion gave the name further meanings. It described the language and administrative habits taught in royal schools, the court culture of House Ubar and, in foreign usage, the officials and settlers who represented the empire.

The distinction mattered. A Nemeru boat family could be subject to Arqesh while retaining Nemeru identity. A provincial scribe might use Arqeshite forms without possessing citizenship in the capital. A soldier settled on confiscated land could belong to an imperial household and remain descended from another people.

House Ubar recruited local elites, conquered specialists and deported populations into its service. The empire's records, instruments and standard measures improved coordination across the river system. They also made grain, labour and suspected rebels easier to seize. Arqeshite culture therefore spread through schooling, opportunity and coercion.

## Deportees and imperial households

Jirahar deportees entered the river world after the destruction of First Qiryath. Some remained under guards or masters. Others became musicians, builders, interpreters and water officials. Their children could speak Arqeshite, participate in an urban trade and continue Jirahar worship. Arqeshite belonging followed when a family left Jirahar communal life and entered a local household; deportation alone conferred no such membership.

Similar choices faced captives from Qahur, Tarkhan districts and rebellious river towns. The empire contained many ancestries. Imperial membership described rule and service; Arqeshite civic culture centred on the capital and spread unevenly through the cities.

## Survival after House Ubar

The coalition which took Arqesh in 690 BR destroyed the ruling house's supremacy and divided its territories. It did not empty the cities or erase the canal population. Farmers continued cultivation, officials served new governors and schools preserved administrative practice. Vasharan rulers adopted useful parts of Arqeshite government while condemning the dynasty they had defeated.

By 350 BR Arqeshite identity remained active as an urban and river culture. House Ubar had fallen. Its former subjects belonged to several successor governments, and the canal cities continued to distinguish themselves from both their conquerors and one another.
