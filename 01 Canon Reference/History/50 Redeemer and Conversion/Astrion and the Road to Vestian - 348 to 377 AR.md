# Astrion and the Road to Vestian

Astrion succeeded Sevrin in 340 AR and restored the Stars to imperial favour. For a decade his colleges sat in judgement on the Church's wonders. He tried to give the Stars a sign as public as the unfreezing river at Velos, and in 350 the Red Star answered his test once. In 351, on the retreat from a northern desert campaign, a timber span he had insisted on standing beneath broke his hips and legs. He lived. Caleran, told that he was dead, made Sevrin's son-in-law Lucar Aster emperor and would not unmake him. For twenty years Astrion was carried under the standard of his test as emperor of the east, and the Star gave him no second answer. Lucar lost his army in the Kellovar ford in 377, rode out of the river dry and was killed by his own soldiers three days later. Vestian took power that year; compulsory faith followed in 385. ## Astrion at Caleran, 340–351 AR

### 340–341 · Stipends and the Entering

Astrion spent his youth with Flame tutors and readers who still knew how to interpret Star calendars. As emperor he wanted the colleges to recover the authority they had held when nothing in the empire counted as a sign until an augur had written it down. He restored suspended stipends and let temple stewards sue for rents that had been diverted from Star endowments, and governors sorted out the disputed plots by Cassian's rules. Cassian's restitution decree and the legal right to Redeemer worship remained in force. Property awarded to an assembly stayed with it. A market town could reopen its temple, keep its church and owe taxes to both institutions' former patrons.

The Great Purge's edict against publishing a wonder the colleges had not entered had lain unused since 292 and had never been repealed. Astrion had it read again in the forums. Under the order the towns called **the Entering**, every wonder claimed since legalisation was to be brought before a chartered college and entered with its witnesses, or struck. Bishops' readers were summoned with their books of healings and kindled lamps. The Warden of the Reach was told that an augur would countersign his winter book. Astrion sent no one to prison for worship, and he made the Church's whole record of its own signs subject to the colleges' judgement.

The Entering stayed in force for as long as Astrion sat at Caleran. The colleges called in the sees' books year after year, and by 350 they had struck a decade of the Church's wonders from the record.

### 349–350 · Velos, Qiryath and the public answer

The open water at Velos was the Redeemer's strongest argument, and every winter crowds walked out on the ice above the bridge to look at it. Since Cassian's day the reach had stood in the augurs' books as a sign answered to the emperor, kept by a crown warden. Astrion had the augurs enter it again as an answer of Hecatara, the Star of crossings, since a bridge is a crossing. The bishops called the new entry a forgery of authorship. The bridge crowds kept calling the black water the Flame's, and the chaplain at the bridge chapel went on saying the prayers of the Velos Concord. The quarrel showed Astrion that a book entry would not move a crowd. He needed a sign people could watch.

Jirahar petitioners meanwhile asked for supervised access to the old sanctuary precinct at Qiryath, permission to tend graves and relief from the Litter Debt for work on the ruined cisterns. Astrion authorised a survey and let representatives from outside the empire attend under safe conduct. Some petitioners sought an eventual sanctuary, others wanted legal access to graves and water, and some would accept neither under the garrison's conditions. Astrion hoped to reopen a politically useful road, gain a source of credit for the northern command and embarrass bishops who spoke as though Qiryath could never prosper again. His treasury hoped that the clearance would reach the gate chamber and settle the oldest debt on the eastern rolls. Clerks went through the ruin for a season writing down who kept which cistern, which household held which house and field, and who claimed which grave.

Then the wardens of the Red Star arrived. By the emperor's own restored rule every public work was loaded before the Star before it opened, and they set up their frame over the galleries of the river quarter. The Jirahar crews put down their tools. Every tower child knew what a frame broken before the Red Star had done to those galleries in the siege, and to Shahar's before them. The representatives from beyond the frontier left under their safe conducts within the week. The garrison commander had forty of the crew flogged for breaking their contract, and two of them died of it. The works stopped for want of hands, and Astrion left for war before resolving the petitions. The charter annulled in 72 stayed annulled. No sacrifice resumed, and the Silent Gate remained closed. The crews' families were still asking for their wages years later, and the survey's lists stayed in the provincial archive.

Astrion staged his public verification in **350** at a Caleran mustering ground. The augurs put a serviceable bronze standard and its stone socket under an agreed load before a crowd of soldiers, citizens and clergy. The lower support showed a hairline fracture, and the fracture changed direction as engineers shifted the load. They found the weakness it disclosed and cleared the ground before the support failed. The event was entered in two forms. The public proclamation said the Red Star had answered the emperor. The augurs' signed record specified the test, what was risked and how far the response extended. The record eventually reached the Sanctum, where it was sealed.

The augurs' account described an answer within the Red Star's domain. Soldiers took it as a promise of victory, engineers as a warning and Redeemer clergy as evidence that a created power could answer. Astrion asked for a second public test, of victory itself. The augurs would give no terms for it, and it was called off.

### 350–351 · The northern expedition and the Emperor's Cut

A dispute over requisition at the northern wells ended with imperial couriers killed and stores burned. Several Ahdari well houses demanded compensation for water, animals and people seized by imperial agents. Well-Tarkhan carriers withdrew from some contracts, while other crews continued carrying supplies under their own agreements. Astrion treated the attacks as a breach of safe conduct and took a punitive column past the dependable depot line. His early seizure of a fortified store gave him a victory to report. When defenders removed animals and closed secondary wells, the captured store could not supply the column or the civilians following it.

On the retreat in **351**, well-house riders harried the column from the ridges, and the baggage jammed at a dry wash too steep for wagons. The army's carpenters threw a span across it in a night, from roof beams pulled out of the captured store. Every public work in the empire was meant to be loaded before the Red Star until a crack showed its weakest point, and the official who opened it stood beneath it while the first wagons crossed; emperors had excused themselves since Velen I. Under the riders' arrows there was no time for the loading. The master carpenter offered to stand under the span in the emperor's place. Astrion refused him, because the builders' tales say a work comes down on the man who sends another to stand under it, and climbed down into the wash.

The wagon with the augurs' register crossed first. Under the heavier wagons of wounded that came after it, the span broke and fell on him and on the men in the carts. He was dug out with his hips and legs crushed. The surgeons expected him dead by morning, and a courier left for Caleran that night to say that he was. The field record gives him one sentence from that night, a question: had the baggage with the augurs' register crossed? It had.

Officers came to **Savel Orren**, commander of the imperial guard, and offered to acclaim him before the army lost heart. Savel answered that the guard did not bury living men. He had a litter made from the timbers of the span and walked beside it. Astrion did not die, and he never stood again.

Soldiers said he had stood under the span so that the wounded could cross. The eastern army said he had kept the Stars' law when every emperor since Velen had dodged it. Preachers said the Stars had broken their own priest-king and had not troubled to finish him. Engineers said green timber and haste were enough to explain it. Some augurs said he had made his own body the test of victory for which they had refused him terms, and had been answered. The soldiers called the wash the Emperor's Cut. ## 351–377 · Two standards

### 351–352 · Water on the depot road, and the news at Caleran

The column came back across the dry belt with its wounded on carts and its animals dying. What saved it was water. **Tellan**, Astrion's younger brother and Adran's other son, held the eastern depot road. He bought water from the Well-Tarkhan crews who had kept their contracts and brought it west in skins on camels, under the bronze standard of the 350 test. Soldiers who had watched the crack run through its socket now drank under it. The eastern army has called it the **Answered Standard** ever since, and called Tellan the Water-Bringer. As the price of the road home Tellan returned the expedition's captives to the well houses and recognised their right to set caravan terms beyond the depot line.

The first courier reached Caleran weeks ahead of the truth. The capital heard that the emperor lay dead under a bridge in the desert, that he left no son, and that the heir under the terms of 307 was his brother, somewhere in the dry belt with a dying army. The bishops of the four sees had watched the colleges strike their wonders for ten years, and wanted no second emperor of Astrion's mind. The port councils wanted someone who could guarantee pay after a lost expedition. **Lucar Aster**, Sevrin's son-in-law, commanded the southern reserve, and his elder brother **Corlan** held the Aurin approaches. Before the second courier came in, Corlan's troops had lined the river road, the guard had taken a pay warrant under Lucar's seal and the bishop of Caleran had prayed for him by name in the forum.

The second courier said that the emperor was alive at Arcelon and would never walk. Caleran did not go back. An emperor opened each campaign season on his feet before the priests of the Red Star, the port councils had their warrants, and the sees had their man. Lucar repealed the Entering, restored stipends to recognised Redeemer congregations and left ordinary Star property under local title.

### 352–c. 372 · The Carried Emperor

The east kept Astrion. It had a paid command, a sign it could show, the Vasharan frontier at its back and soldiers who had drunk Tellan's water. At every muster the eastern units carried their emperor out in a litter and set him down under the Answered Standard. Savel Orren commanded his household until he died. Corlan Aster, who held the Aurin approaches and ran the southern defences as his brother's marshal, died about 354, and Lucar could not hold both himself. For two seasons, from 355 to 357, the two armies faced each other along the road garrisons and neither marched.

About 357 they settled in the **Accord of the Two Standards**. Astrion kept the name of emperor for his lifetime east of the depot line, and bound himself to name no heir to it. Lucar confirmed the eastern command's share of local revenue on the terms Sevrin had given Adran in 307, its augurs and its standard. Eastern units swore a Star oath beneath the Answered Standard; western units swore Cassian's service oath. Officers who had hoped the east would march on Caleran found their pay secured instead, and most units accepted the settlement.

Astrion lived another fifteen years at Arcelon. Each spring his augurs set a load before the Red Star and asked for terms on which it would answer a second time, and each spring they wrote down that it had given none. He never altered their entries. He died about 372, and the eastern title died with him as the Accord required. Tellan, who had been his brother's legs for twenty years, kept the command, the augurs and the standard, and never claimed the throne.

### 352–368 · Lucar at Caleran

Lucar financed the roads and kept frontier levies in separate accounts. He kept the wardenship of the reach at Velos in crown hands and sold the bridge chapel's revenue to bishops who accepted his terms for army chaplains. In disputes between Redeemer bishops he began giving disputed revenues to whichever claimant accepted his terms for supervising chaplains and provisioning. Clergy appealed to other sees and governors under older rules. A temple that could supply the government might still keep its estate.

Corlan ran the southern defences for him and kept the peace with the fort districts. He died about 354 while inspecting them, and Lucar took the southern command into his own hands and kept it.

### 368–377 · The tokens and the Kellovar ford

Since Cassian's market treaties the southern army had crossed the river-courts' fords behind Folk guides. Allied households lived in the fort districts, sold the garrisons cattle and timber and sent a guide whenever a column had to cross. He waded in first with his household's token tied at his wrist, a small thing of iron, bone or plaited hair, or silver for a chief's house, and the column followed holding a rope, and the river let it pass. Lucar's new chaplains saw idols at the head of a Redeemer army. From about 368, when the emperor had held the southern command himself for fourteen years, they took the tokens from the guides, and from every Folk trader who came through the fort gates to market, and burned the bone and hair ones in the fort yards. The officers sold the silver ones to dealers in Caleran, where Folk silverwork fetched a good price.

The winters of 369–371 were the hardest anyone living could remember. Rivers froze to the bed, and Grobi war-bands came down them on the ice. With their guides' tokens gone, the garrisons could reach the far banks only by the stone bridges, and the Grobi did not come by the bridges. Soldiers watched allied villages burn from the walls.

There was an older way across. A column with no token could still pass a ford if it delivered a traveller: one person walked into the water ahead of the rope and came out on the far bank unable to come back. The river that let the column return would not let that one return with it. Folk households had paid their courts in hair and bone for as long as anyone remembered so that nobody had to be paid in. The fort officers now took a child from each allied household in their districts, "for the crossing", and walked the children into the fords ahead of the columns. When the soldiers came home the children were standing on the far bank. Ford-keepers took some of them in. Folk beyond the rivers took others, and the Grobi took some. Stewards who had been handed children for the fords sold a number of them to dealers at the back gates instead and wrote them down as delivered, and hostages were taken beyond anything the treaties allowed.

From 372 the fort-district households began to leave, and they left towards their children. A family that had hidden a token, sewn into a hem or plaited into a daughter's hair, could wade a ford at night with others holding its belt, and a token could go back across for more. A token put into a delivered child's hand would have let the child come home, but few households now wanted a child brought back to the forts. Other children were carried off the fort estates in grain carts before the stewards could deliver or sell them. The forts hanged token-carriers at the fords as idolaters, Folk war-bands from beyond the rivers burned estate barns to get at the children held in them, and the chaplains searched every hem. The war of 372–377 was fought over crossings and children, and few towns changed hands in it. Other Folk kept trading with the forts and helped towns evacuate.

In the spring of **377** the last and largest company of fort-district households, several thousand people, gathered under the wooded hill of Kellovar, where Casvilar had starved the Folk seven and a half centuries before, to cross out of the empire at low water. Without them the southern forts had no guides, and the Grobi would come again with the ice. Lucar came south himself to bring them back. The households crossed in the night, a token to each chain of people. At dawn the field army followed them into the water behind its chaplains, who had blessed the ford and called its court an idol. It carried no token and delivered no one. The water rose under the column and took it. Folk war-bands on the far bank watched the field army drown.

Lucar crossed dry. On his horse's breast-strap hung a silver otter, the token of a chief's house taken from a guide in 369, which his officers had given him from the confiscations. He came out of the water alone among the people whose children his officers had walked into the fords, and rode to their fire. The Otter house had crossed in the night on a bone token sewn into a child's hem. Folk say the river had carried the emperor as one of that house's kin; he ate its salt before anyone knew his face, and afterwards gave his true name, which the songs count the one thing he did right. Hearth-law gave a guest three days, and on the third morning he was kin, servant or gone. The songs say **Cathra**, mother of the house, whose grandson a steward had written down as delivered and sold at a back gate, offered him the first two before she gave him the third. She hung the silver otter back on his horse and sent him home across the ford. He rode out of the water dry. On the near bank the survivors of his rearguard, who had watched him ride out of the river that drowned their comrades three days before, pulled him from the saddle and killed him. Who struck first was never established, and one of them cut the otter from the strap and threw it into the ford. Folk elders say the river exacted its toll and judged nothing. Vestian's preachers called the drowning a judgement on a divided empire. [[Folk Faith and Appeasement]]

**Vestian**, already a senior commander with a reserve on the inner roads, held the depots and drew support from garrison officers and grain towns by keeping their stores open and paying surviving units. The capital recognised him in 377 while Lucar was still a guest at a Folk fire, after he promised an inquiry into the frontier officers, protection for the main roads and the bridge chapel at Velos to the bishops. He put no one on trial for Lucar's death, and the men who had done it went on drawing his pay. Some Folk leaders negotiated directly with his command and were settled under their own elders by 379. His investigators traced the children the stewards had sold through ledgers and merchants' receipts. The delivered children were beyond the rivers and beyond his law. The tokens the chaplains had burned could not be traced, and he would not buy back idols. The status of individual households, captives and disputed grants took longer to settle.

## What Vestian inherits

Vestian inherited an army whose commands kept their own pay chests, and a court used to deciding which congregations got buildings and stipends. The four sees were already half a government: Caleran carried petitions to court, Aurelium kept copies of everything, in two books since the Entering, and Halmenth and Sermora knew how to house refugees, ransom captives and feed armies. Vestian used all of them while he rebuilt the field army.

He also inherited two signs in two sets of books. The river at Velos still refused to freeze, and its book was a crown warden's. The Red Star's answer of 350 stood in an augurs' record, and an eastern army still marched under the standard it had answered, five years after the emperor it had answered was dead. Vestian, who had read Kellovar as a judgement, wanted one authority to hold every such sign. That conviction led to the dissolution of the augur colleges in 383 and to the war with the eastern command.

He inherited land quarrels as well: one field might have been granted in turn by a temple restoration, a church, a frontier settlement and a requisitioning officer. In the eastern provinces creditors and neighbours who wanted tower lands could point to the old rolls of the Litter Debt and to Astrion's survey lists and petitions, which showed who kept the cisterns and who held which house. Those lists passed into Vestian's chancery. His accession in 377 gave him the means to impose one faith, and his court later chose to turn old prejudice and new greed into persecution.
