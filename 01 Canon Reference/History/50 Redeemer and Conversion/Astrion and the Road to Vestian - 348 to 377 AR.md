# Astrion and the Road to Vestian

Astrion succeeded Sevrin in 348 AR and restored the Stars to imperial favour. He tried to give them a sign as public as the unfreezing river at Velos, and in 350 the Red Star answered his test once. He died at thirty-four on the retreat from a northern desert campaign in 351, under a timber span that had not been tried before the Red Star, which he had insisted on standing beneath while the wagons crossed. His brother Tellan saved the retreating army with water and kept the eastern command, its augurs and the standard of the test for a generation. The Aster brothers returned imperial favour to the Redeemer's faith. The younger, Lucar, lost his army in the Kellovar ford in 377, rode out of the river dry and was killed by his own soldiers three days later. Vestian took power that year; compulsory faith followed in 385. [[The Reign of Sevrin]] · [[The Conversion of the Empire]]

## Astrion, 348–351 AR

### 348–349 · Temples, stipends and schools

Astrion spent his youth with Flame tutors and readers who still knew how to interpret Star calendars. As emperor he wanted the civic cult to recover the authority it had once drawn from schools, relief and public ceremony. He restored suspended stipends and let temple stewards sue for rents that had been diverted from Star endowments, and governors sorted out the disputed plots by Cassian's rules. Cassian's restitution decree and the legal right to Redeemer worship remained in force. Property awarded to an assembly stayed with it. A market town could reopen its temple, keep its church and owe taxes to both institutions' former patrons.

Astrion directed endowed temples to reserve grain for travellers and burial money for people without kin. The order worked where a temple still had tenants, wells or stores. A poor shrine might lose its stipend altogether when its steward could not make the returns the court demanded. Astrion also tied publicly paid posts in rhetoric, calendar teaching and civic history to a teacher's willingness to honour the rites in the classical texts. Redeemer pupils could attend. Some teachers lost their salaries and moved to household schools. Families that could pay followed them, while other pupils lost access to the training that opened a clerkship.

### 349–350 · Velos, Qiryath and the public answer

The open water at Velos was the Redeemer's strongest argument, and every winter crowds walked out on the ice above the bridge to look at it. Since Cassian's day the reach had stood in the augurs' books as a sign answered to the emperor, kept by a crown warden. Astrion had the augurs enter it again as an answer of Hecatara, the Star of crossings, since a bridge is a crossing. The bishops called the new entry a forgery of authorship. The bridge crowds kept calling the black water the Flame's, and the chaplain at the bridge chapel went on saying the prayers of the Velos Concord. The quarrel showed Astrion that a book entry would not move a crowd. He needed a sign people could watch.

Jirahar petitioners meanwhile asked for supervised access to the old sanctuary precinct at Qiryath, permission to tend graves and relief from tribute for work on the ruined cisterns. Astrion authorised a survey and let representatives from outside the empire attend under safe conduct. Some petitioners sought an eventual sanctuary, others wanted legal access to graves and water, and some would accept neither under the garrison's conditions. Astrion hoped to reopen a politically useful road, gain a source of credit for the northern command and embarrass bishops who spoke as though Qiryath could never prosper again. A wall gave way during clearance and injured workers. The garrison closed the works, and Astrion left for war before resolving the petitions. The charter annulled in 65 stayed annulled. No sacrifice resumed, and the Silent Gate remained closed. The injured workers' families were still asking for their wages years later.

Astrion staged his public verification in **350** at a Caleran mustering ground. The augurs put a serviceable bronze standard and its stone socket under an agreed load before a crowd of soldiers, citizens and clergy. The lower support showed a hairline fracture, and the fracture changed direction as engineers shifted the load. They found the weakness it disclosed and cleared the ground before the support failed. The event was entered in two forms. The public proclamation said the Red Star had answered the emperor. The augurs' signed record specified the test, what was risked and how far the response extended. The record eventually reached the Sanctum, where it was sealed.

The augurs' account described an answer within the Red Star's domain. Soldiers took it as a promise of victory, engineers as a warning and Redeemer clergy as evidence that a created power could answer. Astrion asked for a second public test, of victory itself. The augurs would give no terms for it, and it was called off.

### 350–351 · The northern expedition

A dispute over requisition at the northern wells ended with imperial couriers killed and stores burned. Several Ahdari well houses demanded compensation for water, animals and people seized by imperial agents. Well-Tarkhan carriers withdrew from some contracts, while other crews continued carrying supplies under their own agreements. Astrion treated the attacks as a breach of safe conduct and took a punitive column past the dependable depot line. His early seizure of a fortified store gave him a victory to report. When defenders removed animals and closed secondary wells, the captured store could not supply the column or the civilians following it.

On the retreat in **351**, well-house riders harried the column from the ridges, and the baggage jammed at a dry wash too steep for wagons. The army's carpenters threw a span across it in a night, from roof beams pulled out of the captured store. Every public work in the empire was meant to be loaded before the Red Star until a crack showed its weakest point, and the official who opened it stood beneath it while the first wagons crossed; emperors had excused themselves since Velen I. Under the riders' arrows there was no time for the loading. The master carpenter offered to stand under the span in the emperor's place. Astrion refused him, because the builders' tales say a work comes down on the man who sends another to stand under it, and climbed down into the wash.

The wagon with the augurs' register crossed first. Under the heavier wagons of wounded that came after it, the span broke and fell on him and on the men in the carts. He was dug out with his hips and legs crushed and lived through the night. The field record gives him no last words, only a question: had the baggage with the augurs' register crossed? It had. Soldiers said he had stood under the span so that the wounded could cross. The eastern army said he had kept the Stars' law to the end, and preachers that the Stars had killed their own priest-king. Engineers said green timber and haste were enough to explain it; some augurs said he had made his own body the test of victory for which they had refused him terms. Church tradition later gave him the words *“You have won, Carpenter”*: churchmen hear the Redeemer in them, and army songs the master carpenter who had offered to stand there instead. Sestaran humanists keep the words as tragedy. The soldiers called the wash the Emperor's Cut. ## 351–377 · From the retreat to Vestian

### 351–352 · Water on the depot road

The column came back across the dry belt with its wounded on carts and its animals dying. Officers acclaimed **Savel Orren**, commander of the imperial guard, while the army was still withdrawing. What saved it was water. **Tellan**, Astrion's younger brother and Adran's other son, held the eastern depot road. He bought water from the Well-Tarkhan crews who had kept their contracts and brought it west in skins on camels, under the bronze standard of the 350 test. Soldiers who had watched the crack run through its socket now drank under it. The eastern army has called it the **Answered Standard** ever since, and called Tellan the Water-Bringer.

Savel returned captives to the well houses and recognised their right to set caravan terms beyond the depot line. Two exposed road forts were abandoned in the withdrawal. Families attached to the forts left with the garrisons; households and traders who stayed faced new fees and patrons. Savel restored stipends to recognised Redeemer congregations, repealed Astrion's religious condition for public teachers and left ordinary Star property under local title. When Caleran declared for Corlan Aster in 352, Savel gave the crown back at the depot line and lived out his life on an estate. Guard songs remember him as the emperor who gave it back.

### 352–366 · Two brothers and the eastern standard

The commanders and civil officers in the capital recognised **Corlan Aster**, who controlled the Aurin approaches and could guarantee pay after the failed expedition. Corlan held Caleran and the west. He named his brother **Lucar** co-emperor with the southern and northern commands. They shared one imperial title and law, with separate field treasuries and appointments.

The east stayed with Adran's house. Tellan had a paid command, a sign he could show, the Vasharan frontier at his back and soldiers whose fathers he had saved. He never claimed the throne. In all the years that followed the Red Star never answered the east a second time, and his augurs said so honestly. About 357, after two seasons of stand-off on the road garrisons, the brothers and Tellan settled in the **Accord of the Two Standards**. Corlan confirmed the eastern command's share of local revenue on the terms Sevrin had given Adran in 316, its augurs and its standard. Eastern units swore a Star oath beneath the Answered Standard; western units swore Cassian's service oath. Officers who had hoped the east would march on Caleran found their pay secured instead, and most units accepted the settlement.

Corlan financed the southern defences. He died about 366 while inspecting them.

### 366–377 · The tokens and the Kellovar ford

Lucar ruled alone from 366. He financed the roads and kept frontier levies in separate accounts. He kept the wardenship of the reach at Velos in crown hands and sold the bridge chapel's revenue to bishops who accepted his terms for army chaplains. In disputes between Redeemer bishops he began giving disputed revenues to whichever claimant accepted his terms for supervising chaplains and provisioning. Clergy appealed to other sees and governors under older rules. A temple that could supply the government might still keep its estate.

Since Cassian's market treaties the southern army had crossed the river-courts' fords behind Folk guides. Allied households lived in the fort districts, sold the garrisons cattle and timber and sent a guide whenever a column had to cross. He waded in first with his household's token tied at his wrist, a small thing of iron, bone or plaited hair, or silver for a chief's house, and the column followed holding a rope, and the river let it pass. Lucar's new chaplains saw idols at the head of a Redeemer army. From about 368 they took the tokens from the guides, and from every Folk trader who came through the fort gates to market, and burned the bone and hair ones in the fort yards. The officers sold the silver ones to dealers in Caleran, where Folk silverwork fetched a good price.

The winters of 369–371 were the hardest anyone living could remember. Rivers froze to the bed, and Grobi war-bands came down them on the ice. With their guides' tokens gone, the garrisons could reach the far banks only by the stone bridges, and the Grobi did not come by the bridges. Soldiers watched allied villages burn from the walls. The survivors came across the ice to the forts at night. The officers, short of grain themselves, fed them on terms: one child from each household pledged to the crown estates behind the forts until the grain was repaid. Stewards sold some of the pledged children on, and hostages were taken beyond anything the treaties allowed.

From 372 the fort-district households began to leave. A family that had hidden a token, sewn into a hem or plaited into a child's hair, could wade a ford at night with others holding its belt, and a token could go back across for more. Children were carried off the crown estates hidden in grain carts and handed over the fords. The forts hanged token-carriers at the fords as idolaters, Folk war-bands from beyond the rivers burned estate barns to free pledged children, and the chaplains searched every hem. The war of 372–377 was fought over crossings and children, and few towns changed hands in it. Other Folk kept trading with the forts and helped towns evacuate.

In the spring of **377** the last and largest company of fort-district households, several thousand people with the children they had recovered, gathered under the wooded hill of Kellovar, where Casvilar had starved the Folk seven and a half centuries before, to cross out of the empire at low water. Without them the southern forts had no guides, and the Grobi would come again with the ice. Lucar came south himself to bring them back. The households crossed in the night, a token to each chain of people. At dawn the field army followed them into the water behind its chaplains, who had blessed the ford and called its court an idol. The water rose under the column and took it. Folk war-bands on the far bank watched the field army drown.

Lucar crossed dry. On his horse's breast-strap hung a silver otter, the token of a chief's house taken from a guide in 369, which his officers had given him from the confiscations. He came out of the water alone among the people whose children his officers had sold, and rode to their fire. The Otter house had crossed in the night on a bone token sewn into a child's hem. Folk say the river had carried the emperor as one of that house's kin; he ate its salt before anyone knew his face, and afterwards gave his true name, which the songs count the one thing he did right. Hearth-law gave a guest three days, and on the third morning he was kin, servant or gone. The songs say **Cathra**, mother of the house, whose grandson had been sold off the fort estates, offered him the first two before she gave him the third. She hung the silver otter back on his horse and sent him home across the ford. He rode out of the water dry. On the near bank the survivors of his rearguard, who had watched him ride out of the river that drowned their comrades three days before, pulled him from the saddle and killed him. Who struck first was never established, and one of them cut the otter from the strap and threw it into the ford. Folk elders say the river exacted its toll and judged nothing. Vestian's preachers called the drowning a judgement on a divided empire. [[Folk Faith and Appeasement]]

**Vestian**, already a senior commander with a reserve on the inner roads, held the depots and drew support from garrison officers and grain towns by keeping their stores open and paying surviving units. The capital recognised him in 377 while Lucar was still a guest at a Folk fire, after he promised an inquiry into the frontier officers, protection for the main roads and the bridge chapel at Velos to the bishops. He put no one on trial for Lucar's death, and the men who had done it went on drawing his pay. Some Folk leaders negotiated directly with his command and were settled under their own elders by 379. His investigators traced pledged children through stewards' ledgers and merchants' receipts. The tokens the chaplains had burned could not be traced, and he would not buy back idols. The status of individual households, captives and disputed grants took longer to settle.

## What Vestian inherits

Vestian inherited an army whose commands kept their own pay chests, and a court used to deciding which congregations got buildings and stipends. The four sees were already half a government: Caleran carried petitions to court, Aurelium kept copies of everything and the teachers Astrion had dismissed, and Halmenth and Sermora knew how to house refugees, ransom captives and feed armies. Vestian used all of them while he rebuilt the field army.

He also inherited two signs in two sets of books. The river at Velos still refused to freeze, and its book was a crown warden's. The Red Star's answer of 350 stood in an augurs' record, and an eastern army still marched under the standard it had answered. Vestian, who had read Kellovar as a judgement, wanted one authority to hold every such sign. That conviction led to the dissolution of the augur colleges in 383 and to the war with the eastern command.

He inherited land quarrels as well: one field might have been granted in turn by a temple restoration, a church, a frontier settlement and a requisitioning officer. In the eastern provinces creditors and neighbours who wanted tower lands could point to the old Jirahar tribute rolls and to Astrion's survey petitions, which showed who kept the cisterns and who held which house. Those lists passed into Vestian's chancery. His accession in 377 gave him the means to impose one faith, and his court later chose to turn old prejudice and new greed into persecution.
