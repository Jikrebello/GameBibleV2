# The Church after Qiryath

After the fall of Second Qiryath in 70 AR, the Church grew through households that received refugees, merchants who carried letters and aid, and congregations that trained people to teach after the eyewitnesses died. By the third century its principal regional sees were Caleran, Aurelium, Halmenth and Sermora. In 249 AR Emperor Tervan Marcellan issued the first empire-wide order requiring public sacrifice and written proof of compliance. Its enforcement and a later campaign of confiscation tested the institutions that survived to receive Cassian's legalization in 292 AR.

## 70–95 AR — Relief goes out and testimony comes back

Some believers left before the siege closed. Households went east with Jirahar caravan kin into territory held by Vashar. Others reached independent dry-margin towns, where a protector could secure room at a well or a burial plot in return for fees and sureties. Soldiers closed crossings and creditors pursued debts. Families that started out together sometimes ended up in separate towns when one host had room for children and another had work for adults. [[Great Tower Revolt and the Second Destruction]]

Those who got away carried what they could use. Burial lists gave host families names to notify and somewhere to start tracing relatives. Copies of the Qiryath admission letter and Isfir's sayings carried the decisions and testimony known to individual houses. Assemblies along the eastern river roads and in ports raised money for provisions and ransoms. Regular merchants took it out and brought back the names of people found, news of where captives had been sold, and copies of testimony.

The hosts had experience receiving displaced people. Some exiles expected the authority their households had held in Qiryath to continue abroad. Others found that a host court had the final word over guardianship, inherited tools or the use of a grave enclosure. Jirahar teachers preserved the law of vows, festivals and burial after the loss of sacrifice. Believers from those same families still asked them to judge a marriage or identify a grave even when the parties disagreed about Isfir.

## The principal sees

| City | How its see developed |
|---|---|
| **Caleran** | The capital's houses near the docks, workshops and burial grounds preserved accounts of Jareth, Tyros and Aquilan. Imperial traffic let them receive travellers and transmit letters quickly. The city's overseer coordinated burial, lodging and common relief among houses with separate patrons. Visiting congregations asked Caleran to intercede with the court because the city had people who could carry a petition there. |
| **Aurelium** | Myra's martyrdom in an Aurelian bakehouse gave the inland assemblies an early witness associated with feeding and sheltering children. Household schools and relief kitchens trained readers who served smaller eastern towns. Copyists in Aurelium compared Jirahar and Atherian versions of witness accounts and sent replacement readings to houses that had lost theirs. Its later claim through Jareth's Commission of the Pen grew from this teaching and archival work. The surviving Ash Spine reached the Sanctum in later history. |
| **Halmenth** | Naris's mission on the Celion linked port houses, ferry crews and coastal settlements. Halmenth coordinated lodging, burial of people without local kin and aid for prisoners or captives. Its readers and teachers went out with crews returning to smaller harbours. The see's reach followed shipping and the river more closely than provincial borders. |
| **Sermora** | Congregations associated with Virella and Aquilan brought Jirahar seafarers and local households to the same table. Their overseers trained interpreters and recognized letters of introduction used across the Elmaric harbours. Merchants carried copies of readings and letters with manifests and family correspondence. The see maintained relations across ports with different terms of imperial rule. |

Virelos and Veridun Minor formed an inland refuge and teaching route. Virelos retained the memory of Aquilan's baptism. Amaryah's lamp at Veridun Minor remained in the custody of local households, whose work sustained travellers as well as worship. Eastern river and Via Celestis assemblies maintained local elders and burial workers. Congregations also persisted beyond the imperial frontier among the exiles. The four principal sees describe the reach of particular cities by the third century. Each had its own oversight and relied on other churches to receive its letters, teachers and judgements.

## c. 95–170 AR — From witnesses to people trained to teach

As living links to the eyewitnesses grew fewer, congregations compared copies of testimony, exchanged Aquilan's letters and asked neighbouring assemblies who had trained their teachers. Short texts travelled easily. A complete collection cost copying time and materials. Teachers read aloud from a few texts and explained them in the local language. Preachers carried recommendations, and hosts who doubted a letter could ask the sending house before opening their records.

The office of bishop grew in larger cities with multiple meeting houses. Local elders, servers and hosts recognized an overseer to hear disputes between houses, recommend teachers and account for common relief. Smaller congregations continued to share those tasks among several elders. When a house disputed a teacher's authority, it could ask who had taught them and compare the public reading with copies held elsewhere. Later bishops' lists preserve lines of succession with uneven detail about the duties their earliest members performed.

The common gathering took a recognizable form. Readers gave the Scrolls and the received accounts of Isfir. The congregation prayed, remembered the meal and put aside provisions for people without means. Servants and labourers needed a meeting they could attend around work, so places and times varied. In mixed assemblies, shared food, including what might be served and when people arrived, remained a test of whether the Qiryath agreement was being practised.

## Local prosecutions before 249 AR

Some magistrates pursued gatherings after a denunciation and tested an accused person's loyalty by requiring an offering before the Star images. Elsewhere officials tolerated households that paid their dues and kept worship out of public view. A change of governor could expose the same people and property. The case of Senator Velmion Arcasta shows how authorities could reach a congregation through an estate, its owner's papers and the people who carried them. The exact year of his trial is uncertain. [[The Fireless Trial]] · [[The Ember Underground]]

The tribute imposed after Qiryath's fall remained a liability of Jirahar households under Atherian rule. A Jirahar believer could face the tribute assessment as well as a charge of unlawful worship. A believer from another people could face the latter charge while owing no sanctuary tribute. Officials who treated the two registers as evidence of the same offence exposed whole households to investigation.

## 249–253 AR — Tervan Marcellan and the Edict of Public Sacrifice

Emperor **Tervan Marcellan** (244–253 AR) had risen through the provincial commands. After mutinies over arrears and failures to supply several garrisons, he demanded a public demonstration of allegiance across the empire. His **Edict of Public Sacrifice** in 249 AR ordered residents of directly governed provinces to offer before the Stars at an appointed place. Town commissioners recorded names, witnessed the offering and issued written certificates. The edict applied across the population. It gave officers a way to identify and prosecute Redeemer believers without waiting for an accusation against a particular house.

Enforcement was strongest from 249 to 251 AR and varied by district. Where a Jirahar charter exempted a community from Star rites, its advocates could present the charter and its receipts, and some commissioners recognized the exemption. Others ignored it. A person could have a certificate bought in their name, sacrifice under pressure or refuse and risk prison, confiscation or death. A dependent had fewer ways out than a patron with officials on their payroll.

Tervan's successor suspended the general requirement in 253 AR. Local cases continued. The assemblies had to decide how to restore a person who offered to keep their children housed, a person who bought a certificate and a person who gave up other believers' names. Elders in the main sees exchanged letters about admission to worship and restitution. They could feed a returning household while delaying its access to the names of couriers and hosts. They disagreed over how long penance should last and what weight to give a confessor's intercession.

## 253–287 AR — Congregations recover property and records

After the suspension, some governors reopened burial grounds to their former custodians. Others left disputed property with the person who held the deed. In Caleran, elders gathered witnesses to identify grounds that a deceased patron had paid for on behalf of several houses. In Halmenth, harbour households pooled money for graves and kept copies of the payments with the people who tended them. In Aurelium, readers replaced texts seized during the persecution using copies that had travelled to smaller congregations. Sermoran hosts used ships to return letters and transmit recommendations between ports when inland routes were closed.

Congregations grew in the quieter years and some could again maintain regular gathering rooms. A recognized bishop could answer for several houses in a property claim. The same recognition made the bishop and the congregation's common records easier for officials to find during the next round of seizures.

## 288–291 AR — The closing prosecutions

During the civil war that brought Cassian to power, the court holding Caleran treated the routes between assemblies as potential channels for men, money and messages. Its orders targeted known meeting places, books and recognized officers. Officials seized rooms registered to patrons, demanded copies from readers and arrested elders who kept correspondence. A congregation with several copies and a trusted burial association could continue meeting. Another lost its only reader and the person who knew where its funds were hidden. Provincial officers applied the orders unevenly as commands changed hands. These were the last sustained prosecutions before legalization.

At Ironvale in winter 290 AR, arrests at the Ashforges led to three days of rising. After troops retook the yards, the authorities confined forty Redeemer prisoners in an unlit furnace. The prison and burial rolls, and the account of the flame seen when the doors opened, later passed into the local commemoration of [[The Forty Flames]].

## 292 AR — What Cassian legalizes

After his victory at the Stone Bridge of Velos, Cassian legalized the Redeemer's faith in 292 AR. His order permitted open assembly and worship, ended the outstanding orders against books and meetings, and let congregations petition for confiscated meeting rooms and burial grounds. A house might be registered to a patron whose heirs claimed it as private property. Petitioners therefore brought witnesses and records of donations and upkeep. The officials who decided those claims also had to decide who could speak for a congregation. The Church came into public life with bishops, readers, stewards, property claims and disagreements already in place.

In Caleran, legalization made public worship possible at the martyrs' forum. During Cassian's reign, the keepers from Veridun Minor brought a daughter flame there from Amaryah's lamp. [[The Conversion of the Empire]] · [[Caleran and the Eternal Ember]]
