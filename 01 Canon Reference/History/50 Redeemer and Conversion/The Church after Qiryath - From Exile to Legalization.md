# The Church after Qiryath

After the fall of Second Qiryath in 65 AR, the Church grew through households that received refugees, merchants who carried letters and aid, and congregations that trained people to teach after the eyewitnesses died. By the third century its principal regional sees were Caleran, Aurelium, Halmenth and Sermora. All that time it lived beside the augur colleges, which alone could say in law that a wonder had happened, and its people learned to keep their own wonders quiet. In 245 AR Emperor Tervan Marcellan, frightened by a lamp cult that had fed mutinies in the eastern garrisons, issued the first empire-wide order requiring public sacrifice and a licence for every household's graves and lamps. Its enforcement and a later campaign of confiscation tested the congregations that survived to receive Cassian's legalisation in 292 AR.

## 65–95 AR — Relief goes out and testimony comes back

Some believers left before the siege closed. Households went east with Jirahar caravan kin into territory held by Vashar. Others reached independent dry-margin towns, where a local protector would sell a family room at a well or a plot to bury its dead. Soldiers closed crossings and creditors pursued debts. Families that started out together sometimes ended up in separate towns when one host had room for children and another had work for adults. [[Great Tower Revolt and the Second Destruction]]

Those who got away carried what they could use. Burial lists gave host families names to notify and somewhere to start tracing relatives. Copies of the Qiryath admission letter and Isfir's sayings carried the decisions and testimony known to individual houses. Assemblies along the eastern river roads and in ports raised money for provisions and ransoms. Regular merchants took it out and brought back the names of people found, news of where captives had been sold, and copies of testimony.

The hosts had experience receiving displaced people. Some exiles expected the authority their households had held in Qiryath to continue abroad. Others found that in a host town the host's elders decided who raised an orphan, who kept a dead man's tools and who could be buried where. Jirahar teachers preserved the law of vows, festivals and burial after the loss of sacrifice. Believers from those same families still asked them to judge a marriage or identify a grave even when the parties disagreed about Isfir.

## The principal sees

| City | How its see developed |
|---|---|
| **Caleran** | The capital's houses near the docks, workshops and burial grounds preserved accounts of Jareth, Tyros and Aquilan. Imperial traffic let them receive travellers and transmit letters quickly. The city's overseer coordinated burial, lodging and common relief among houses with separate patrons. Visiting congregations asked Caleran to intercede with the court because the city had people who could carry a petition there. |
| **Aurelium** | Myra's martyrdom in an Aurelian bakehouse gave the inland assemblies an early witness associated with feeding and sheltering children. Household schools and relief kitchens trained readers who served smaller eastern towns. Copyists in Aurelium compared Jirahar and Atherian versions of witness accounts and sent replacement readings to houses that had lost theirs. Its later claim through Jareth's Commission of the Pen grew from this teaching and archival work. The surviving Ash Spine reached the Sanctum in later history. |
| **Halmenth** | Naris's mission on the Celion linked port houses, ferry crews and coastal settlements. Halmenth coordinated lodging, burial of people without local kin and aid for prisoners or captives. Its readers and teachers went out with crews returning to smaller harbours. The see's reach followed shipping and the river more closely than provincial borders. |
| **Sermora** | Congregations associated with Virella and Aquilan brought Jirahar seafarers and local households to the same table. Their overseers trained interpreters and recognised letters of introduction used across the Elmaric harbours. Merchants carried copies of readings and letters with manifests and family correspondence. The see maintained relations across ports with different terms of imperial rule. |

Virelos and Veridun Minor formed an inland refuge and teaching route. Virelos retained the memory of Aquilan's baptism. Amaryah's lamp at Veridun Minor remained in the custody of local households, whose work sustained travellers as well as worship. Eastern river and Via Celestis assemblies maintained local elders and burial workers. Congregations also persisted beyond the imperial frontier among the exiles. The four principal sees describe the reach of particular cities by the third century. Each had its own oversight and relied on other churches to receive its letters, teachers and judgements.

## c. 95–170 AR — From witnesses to people trained to teach

As living links to the eyewitnesses grew fewer, congregations compared copies of testimony, exchanged Aquilan's letters and asked neighbouring assemblies who had trained their teachers. Short texts travelled easily. A complete collection cost copying time and materials. Teachers read aloud from a few texts and explained them in the local language. Preachers carried letters of recommendation, and a host who doubted one could write back to the sending house before trusting the bearer with names.

The office of bishop grew in larger cities with multiple meeting houses. Local elders, servers and hosts recognised an overseer to hear disputes between houses, recommend teachers and account for common relief. Smaller congregations continued to share those tasks among several elders. When a house disputed a teacher's authority, it could ask who had taught them and compare the public reading with copies held elsewhere. Later bishops' lists preserve lines of succession with uneven detail about the duties their earliest members performed.

The common gathering took a recognisable form. Readers gave the Scrolls and the received accounts of Isfir. The congregation prayed, remembered the meal and put aside provisions for people without means. Servants and labourers needed a meeting they could attend around work, so places and times varied. In mixed assemblies, shared food, including what might be served and when people arrived, remained a test of whether the Qiryath agreement was being practised.

Many congregations kept a reception lamp. A newly baptised believer lit it from the assembly's flame, and now and then, before the whole room, it took a white fire that gave light and consumed nothing. No one could make it happen and no one was owed it. Since the Great Purge it had been a crime to publish a wonder the augur colleges had not entered in their books, so a letter said only that "the lamp took well" and left the reader to understand. [[The Ember Underground]] tells how that habit outlived the danger.

## Local prosecutions before 245 AR

Some magistrates pursued gatherings after a denunciation and tested an accused person's loyalty by requiring an offering before the Star images. Elsewhere officials tolerated households that paid their dues and kept worship out of public view. A change of governor could expose the same people and property. The case of Senator Velmion Arcasta shows how authorities could reach a congregation through an estate, its owner's papers and the people who carried them. The exact year of his trial is uncertain. [[The Fireless Trial]] · [[The Ember Underground]]

Many of the magistrates who hunted the congregations were afraid of them for reasons they could name. The empire had long experience of cults that fed something. Grief cults sprang up in garrisons after bad campaigns, images of the imperial cult drew a devotion that officials were trained to watch, and every province had its story of a shrine whose generosity had turned out to be hunger. A magistrate who found people meeting at the graves of executed men, keeping lamps burning for them, reporting lights on the dead and refusing to let the augurs examine any of it saw a pattern he had been trained to see. Some prosecutors were careerists and some were cruel. Others were sincere men who had watched a Mask feed and believed they were protecting the city. Their sincerity spared no one the rack or the fire.

The tribute laid on the Jirahar after Qiryath's fall followed every Jirahar household under Atherian rule. A Jirahar believer could be taxed as a Jirahar and tried as a Redeemer believer in the same month; a believer of another people faced only the trial. Officials who went down the tribute lists looking for believers dragged whole families before the magistrate.

## 245–248 AR — Tervan Marcellan and the Edict of Public Sacrifice

Emperor **Tervan Marcellan** (241–248 AR) had risen through the provincial commands. The eastern garrisons he inherited had gone unpaid since the last Vashar war, and in the 240s a cult spread through them that the soldiers called the **Kept Lamps**. Its keepers promised that comrades who had died still owed their pay were not lost but "kept", and set lamps over their graves. The lamps leaned towards mourners as they passed and burned on without oil. Garrisons that tended them grew sullen, then mutinous, and the mutinies were harder to put down than hunger alone explained. Augurs found the cult's central image about 243 and broke it, and officers burned the grave-lamps across three provinces.

Tervan drew his own lesson. He demanded a public demonstration of allegiance across the empire, and a licence for every lamp kept over a grave. His **Edict of Public Sacrifice** in 245 AR ordered residents of directly governed provinces to offer before the Stars at an appointed place. In every town commissioners set up an altar in the market, watched each household make its offering and handed over a written certificate, and the same certificate licensed the household's graves and the lamps it kept for its dead. The Redeemer burial fraternities, who also kept lamps at the graves of executed men, fell under the licence at once. The edict was meant for everyone. It also let officers find Redeemer believers without waiting for a neighbour to denounce them: they looked for the households with no certificate.

Enforcement was strongest from 245 to 247 AR and varied by district. Where a Jirahar charter exempted a community from Star rites, its elders carried the charter to the market altar; some commissioners honoured it and others did not. A person could buy a certificate, sacrifice with a soldier at their elbow, or refuse and risk prison, confiscation or death. A servant had fewer ways out than a patron with officials in his pay.

Tervan's successor suspended the general requirement in 248 AR. Local cases continued. The assemblies had to decide how to restore a person who offered to keep their children housed, a person who bought a certificate and a person who gave up other believers' names. Elders in the main sees exchanged letters about admission to worship and restitution. They could feed a returning household while delaying its access to the names of couriers and hosts. They disagreed over how long penance should last and what weight to give a confessor's intercession.

The lamps raised a quarrel of their own. A congregation whose grave-lamps had been licensed under the edict had kept its dead lit by bowing to the Stars' commissioners; many would not worship by those flames again. Most relit from a flame that had never been licensed. The most sought-after was Amaryah's at Veridun Minor, which its keepers had hidden through the edict in a covered firepot at the back of a neighbour's bread oven. Through the winter after 248, travellers carried daughter flames from that oven to congregations along the river roads, and the custom of lighting a congregation's lamp from an older lamp's flame spread from that winter. ## 248–287 AR — The quieter years

After the suspension, some governors reopened the burial grounds to the families who had tended them; others left them with whoever held the deed. In Caleran, old men walked the cemetery lanes with the elders to point out the plots a dead patron had bought for several houses. Halmenth's harbour households pooled their wages for graves. Aurelium's readers recopied the seized texts from copies that had gone out to village congregations and came back by mule. When the inland roads closed, Sermoran ships carried the letters.

Congregations grew in the quiet years, and some could again keep a regular room. A known bishop could speak for several houses in a quarrel over property, and a known bishop was also easy for officials to find when the seizures began again.

## 288–291 AR — The closing prosecutions

During the civil war that brought Cassian to power, the court holding Caleran treated the routes between assemblies as potential channels for men, money and messages. The capital's augurs had declared the frontier command's omens void, and Cassian, who had few augurs of his own, was fighting without the Stars' public favour. The capital's searchers went looking above all for the congregations' books of signs, the quiet records of healings and lamps that the augurs called counter-augury and that sometimes carried dispatches between their pages. Its officers went to the known meeting rooms, took the readers' copies and arrested the elders who kept the letters. A congregation with several copies and a trusted burial association could continue meeting. Another lost its only reader and the person who knew where its funds were hidden. Provincial officers applied the orders unevenly as commands changed hands. These were the last sustained prosecutions before legalisation.

At Ironvale in winter 290 AR, arrests at the Ashforges led to three days of rising. After troops retook the yards, the authorities confined forty Redeemer prisoners in an unlit furnace. The prison and burial rolls, and the account of the flame seen when the doors opened, later passed into the local commemoration of [[The Forty Flames]]. When Cassian's officers took Ironvale for its forges in 291, a commander with no augurs listened to the forge guards. Two men swore before his officers that the furnace had been cold and the flame on the dead was no forge-fire: the guard who had opened the doors, and the Ashforges' enslaved script-keeper, Brask, whom the officers freed so his oath would stand. In the spring of 292 Cassian's camp had the sworn account read aloud in the Aurin towns.

## 292 AR — What Cassian legalises

After his victory at the Stone Bridge of Velos, where the Aurin has never frozen since, Cassian legalised the Redeemer's faith in 292 AR. He kept the augur colleges and had them enter the open water in their books as the emperor's sign, which the bishops refused to commemorate. Congregations could meet in the open, their books stopped being contraband, and they could ask for their confiscated rooms and graveyards back; general restitution followed about 296 as the bishops' price for accepting a crown warden at the bridge. A meeting room bought in a patron's name might now be claimed by his heirs, and the officials who settled such quarrels found they were also deciding who spoke for a congregation. The Church came into public life already arguing over both.

In Caleran, legalisation made public worship possible at the martyrs' forum. During Cassian's reign, the keepers from Veridun Minor brought a daughter flame there from Amaryah's lamp. [[The Conversion of the Empire]] · [[Caleran and the Eternal Ember]]
