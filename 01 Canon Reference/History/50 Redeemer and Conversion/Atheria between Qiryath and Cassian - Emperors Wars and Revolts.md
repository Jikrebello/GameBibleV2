# Atheria between Qiryath and Cassian

The capital that Cassian won in 292 depended on grain from the Aurin, Raveth and Sarqen, on working harbours and on soldiers willing to accept the same treasury's pay. The centuries since Isfir explain how the provincial commanders and councils gained enough leverage to decide a civil war. ## 0–78 AR · The Othrans lose the east's cooperation

Othran II remained emperor at the beginning of the age. Under him, governors continued to rely on local charters, established taxes and military grain contracts. The trial of Isfir was a provincial capital case. In the years after it, the succession outlasted the emperor's ability to keep eastern tax officers, sanctuary households and local elites working together. The Great Purge in c. 36–45 reached Redeemer gatherings in several cities. The Great Tower Revolt in 66–73 brought Atherian armies against Qiryath, cost the city its sanctuary and charter in 70, and left a garrison, confiscated land and an exile population.

A short succession war pitted the capital's preferred heir against an eastern commander. The east could field an army after the siege, but the capital controlled access to the fleet and treasury. In 78, an Aurin supply commander, **Tavian Dascen**, won recognition after gaining the support of enough port councils to keep grain moving. He offered back pay and confirmed municipal privileges. He also confirmed direct rule at Qiryath. [[The Last Dynasties of the Age of Glory]] · [[The First Church - From Qiryath to the Tower Revolt]] · [[Great Tower Revolt and the Second Destruction]]

## 78–171 AR · Four reigns remembered for public works and order

| Emperor | What people inherited from him |
|---|---|
| **Tavian Dascen, 78–91** | He paid arrears, confirmed charters and opened petitions over seizures made in the succession war. Relief favoured families who could prove their title. Some soldiers returned to civilian occupations, while officers and suppliers kept gains that the treasury could not afford to unwind. His adult daughter oversaw the household accounts and negotiated with creditors. He adopted **Daven Talar**, a commander the eastern army would accept. |
| **Daven Talar, 91–119** | He fought a major war with Vashar c. 98–107 over passage and client towns beyond Arcelon. Atheria held selected road stations and briefly occupied an outer stretch of the Avesh corridor. Convoys stretched too far and Vasharan forces recovered the exposed posts. The court claimed victory when it gained toll and safe-conduct concessions. Veterans carried injuries and ransom debts home. Captive sales fed estates and mines. Daven gave his nephew Rovan charge of the surviving frontier before naming him heir. |
| **Rovan Caelis, 119–147** | He withdrew the exposed garrisons left by Daven and fixed supply along shorter routes. His southern treaties protected markets and required the exchange or return of designated captives. He funded canals and granaries where cities could supply labour and match the expense. Southern communities that supplied scouts and ford crews gained market privileges. Households cleared from the fort approaches retained fewer winter grazing rights. With no surviving son, Rovan adopted Neral, who already directed the southern commands. |
| **Neral Vardun, 147–171** | He held southern allied districts through a war c. 154–166 while paying for relief at home. His envoys bargained separately with river clans, hill communities and coastal pilots. Several clans split over supply and the return of fugitives. Neral brought his son into command early. Officials who wanted continuity accepted the arrangement, and the son inherited a large network of officers who owed their advancement to him. |

Municipal writers remembered these four reigns for repair, regular justice and the reliability of pay. A merchant whose convoy arrived, a farmer whose land was taken and a Vasharan household ransoming a relative had different accounts of those same years. Vashar retained the Ubar and the highland core, and its border towns bargained with both crowns. Jirahar exiles and their descendants sometimes knew the roads, languages and market law better than the envoys who hired them. ## 171–244 AR · Court predation, the Sarqen rising and provincial armies

**Marek Vessan, 171–185,** Neral's son, took the name of his mother's family. He began with grain distributions and a peace that allowed some units home. When pay fell behind, his household sold appointments and prosecuted merchants and officers whose assets it could seize. In the southern treaty belt, his officers seized livestock in lieu of deliveries and interfered with local decisions over inheritance. One coalition raided the supply road while a different group kept its agreement to escort traders. Guards eventually killed Marek. Over the following years, two provincial commands issued their own warrants. A commander expelled from Caleran returned to his former southern district, promised to restore its treaty terms and gained troops and guides from some towns. Other communities used the conflict to expel imperial officers. The next court had to bargain separately with those communities when it tried to reclaim the crossings.

**The Sarqen rising, c. 189–192.** Enslaved workers from grain estates and port yards seized a store and used its shipping lists to trace people recently sold. Field hands, transport crews and some free tenants joined after troops took carts and food for the succession war. Some groups sought passage out by sea and others protected families sheltering inland. The treasury sent troops when the revolt interrupted grain delivery to Caleran. It offered recognition of freedom to a limited number of people who turned over boats and stores, then recaptured and sold many others in dispersed lots. For years, shippers demanded armed escorts and owners kept sale records in separate hands. Former fugitives and relatives used dock work and marriages to reconnect households. This rising came generations after [[The Twelve Companions of the Redeemer|Menos's Ironvale risings]]. The Sarqen rebels included Star worshippers, Redeemer believers and people following other rites. Their aims came from enslavement, family separation and the immediate grain seizure.

**c. 193–244.** The restored court relied on larger regional commands to guarantee supplies. It fought a second major war with Vashar c. 222–233. Vasharan officers seized road stations and encouraged border towns to redirect tolls east. Atherian officers pressed wagons and river craft into service, then transferred units south when forts and families there came under attack. A negotiated evacuation and exchange of captives preserved the main roads, but officers had already promised more coin and grain than the treasury could deliver. The rival states remained capable of fighting and trading. The authoritarian turn under Tervan followed a long fiscal and military strain. Religious conformity was his chosen response to that strain. ## 244–316 AR · From arrears to Cassian's settlement

| Phase | Political development |
|---|---|
| **Tervan Marcellan, 244–253** | Tervan briefly improved delivery by assigning revenue to field commands and had municipal commissioners report on supplies and compliance. The offices that issued certificates under his 249 public sacrifice order also identified people and premises for seizure. Towns with grain or useful transport officers negotiated relief from other burdens. |
| **253–c. 275** | Tervan's successor suspended the general sacrifice requirement in 253 and tried to bring depot revenue back to Caleran. The eastern road command and the southern army insisted on keeping enough to pay locally. Each started acting in the emperor's name without waiting for his approval. Local governors who controlled the roads could also decide whether confiscated church property went back. |
| **c. 275–288** | The court brought taxes and appointments back under one authority through negotiated returns, selective campaigns and the reassignment of commands. Officials kept copies of emergency warrants and contracts because the central treasury might deny old debts. Caleran kept confiscation as a tool against suspected couriers, suppliers and rivals. |
| **288–292** | Cassian's advantage in the civil war was his ability to feed and pay an army on the Aurin approach and secure the cooperation of suppliers. The capital prosecuted known Redeemer networks during the contest. Those seizures left the public Church with a backlog of claims once Cassian won. |
| **292–316** | Cassian honoured some obligations incurred by rival commanders, restored southern market treaties and sought stability with Vashar. Once the four sees became public, he could route relief and petitioning through their officers. They had the same strengths and problems as the empire's older municipal and military networks. His court could fund them and could favour the local factions willing to work with it. [[Cassian and the Four Sees - The Settlement of 292 to 316 AR]] |

The power that port councils and provincial commands gained during these wars survived Cassian. His successors inherited the need to supply frontier armies and keep grain moving alongside the new power to fund churches and settle their civil disputes.
