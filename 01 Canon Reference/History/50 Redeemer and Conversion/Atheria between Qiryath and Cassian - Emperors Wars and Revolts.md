# Atheria between Qiryath and Cassian

The capital that Cassian wins in 292 depends on grain from the Aurin, Raveth and Sarqen, on working harbours and on soldiers willing to accept the same treasury's pay. The centuries since Isfir explain how the provincial commanders and councils gained enough leverage to decide a civil war. ## 0–78 AR · The Othrans lose the east's cooperation

Othran II stays emperor at the beginning of the age. Under him, governors continue to rely on local charters, established taxes and military grain contracts. The trial of Isfir is a provincial capital case. In the years after it, the succession outlasts the emperor's ability to keep eastern tax officers, sanctuary households and local elites working together. The Great Purge in c. 36–45 and the Great Tower Revolt in 66–73 remain distinct. The first reaches Redeemer gatherings in several cities. The second brings Atherian armies against Qiryath, costs the city its sanctuary and charter in 70, and leaves a garrison, confiscated land and an exile population.

A short succession war pits the capital's preferred heir against an eastern commander. The east can field an army after the siege, but the capital controls access to the fleet and treasury. In 78, an Aurin supply commander, **Tavian Dascen**, wins recognition after gaining the support of enough port councils to keep grain moving. He offers back pay and confirms municipal privileges. He also confirms direct rule at Qiryath. [[The Last Dynasties of the Age of Glory]] · [[The First Church - From Qiryath to the Tower Revolt]] · [[Great Tower Revolt and the Second Destruction]]

## 78–171 AR · Four reigns remembered for public works and order

| Emperor | What people inherit from him |
|---|---|
| **Tavian Dascen, 78–91** | He pays arrears, confirms charters and opens petitions over seizures made in the succession war. Relief favours families who can prove their title. Some soldiers return to civilian occupations, while officers and suppliers keep gains that the treasury cannot afford to unwind. His adult daughter oversees the household accounts and negotiates with creditors. He adopts **Daven Talar**, a commander the eastern army will accept. |
| **Daven Talar, 91–119** | He fights a major war with Vashar c. 98–107 over passage and client towns beyond Arcelon. Atheria holds selected road stations and briefly occupies an outer stretch of the Avesh corridor. Convoys stretch too far and Vasharan forces recover the exposed posts. The court claims victory when it gains toll and safe-conduct concessions. Veterans carry injuries and ransom debts home. Captive sales feed estates and mines. Daven gives his nephew Rovan charge of the surviving frontier before naming him heir. |
| **Rovan Caelis, 119–147** | He withdraws the exposed garrisons left by Daven and fixes supply along shorter routes. His southern treaties protect markets and require the exchange or return of designated captives. He funds canals and granaries where cities can supply labour and match the expense. Southern communities that supply scouts and ford crews gain market privileges. Households cleared from the fort approaches retain fewer winter grazing rights. With no surviving son, Rovan adopts Neral, who already directs the southern commands. |
| **Neral Vardun, 147–171** | He holds southern allied districts through a war c. 154–166 while paying for relief at home. His envoys bargain separately with river clans, hill communities and coastal pilots. Several clans split over supply and the return of fugitives. Neral brings his son into command early. Officials who want continuity accept the arrangement, and the son inherits a large network of officers who owe their advancement to him. |

Municipal writers remember these four reigns for repair, regular justice and the reliability of pay. A merchant whose convoy arrives, a farmer whose land is taken and a Vasharan household ransoming a relative have different accounts of those same years. Vashar retains the Ubar and the highland core, and its border towns bargain with both crowns. Jirahar exiles and their descendants sometimes know the roads, languages and market law better than the envoys who hire them. ## 171–244 AR · Court predation, the Sarqen rising and provincial armies

**Marek Vessan, 171–185,** Neral's son, takes the name of his mother's family. He begins with grain distributions and a peace that allows some units home. When pay falls behind, his household sells appointments and prosecutes merchants and officers whose assets it can seize. In the southern treaty belt, his officers seize livestock in lieu of deliveries and interfere with local decisions over inheritance. One coalition raids the supply road while a different group keeps its agreement to escort traders. Guards eventually kill Marek. Over the following years, two provincial commands issue their own warrants. A commander expelled from Caleran returns to his former southern district, promises to restore its treaty terms and gains troops and guides from some towns. Other communities use the conflict to expel imperial officers. The next court has to bargain separately with those communities when it tries to reclaim the crossings.

**The Sarqen rising, c. 189–192.** Enslaved workers from grain estates and port yards seize a store and use its shipping lists to trace people recently sold. Field hands, transport crews and some free tenants join after troops take carts and food for the succession war. Some groups seek passage out by sea and others protect families sheltering inland. The treasury sends troops when the revolt interrupts grain delivery to Caleran. It offers recognition of freedom to a limited number of people who turn over boats and stores, then recaptures and sells many others in dispersed lots. For years, shippers demand armed escorts and owners keep sale records in separate hands. Former fugitives and relatives use dock work and marriages to reconnect households. This rising comes generations after [[The Twelve Companions of the Redeemer|Menos's Ironvale risings]]. The Sarqen rebels include Star worshippers, Redeemer believers and people following other rites. Their aims come from enslavement, family separation and the immediate grain seizure.

**c. 193–244.** The restored court relies on larger regional commands to guarantee supplies. It fights a second major war with Vashar c. 222–233. Vasharan officers seize road stations and encourage border towns to redirect tolls east. Atherian officers press wagons and river craft into service, then transfer units south when forts and families there come under attack. A negotiated evacuation and exchange of captives preserve the main roads, but officers have already promised more coin and grain than the treasury can deliver. The rival states remain capable of fighting and trading. The authoritarian turn under Tervan follows a long fiscal and military strain. Religious conformity is his chosen response to that strain. ## 244–316 AR · From arrears to Cassian's settlement

| Phase | Political development |
|---|---|
| **Tervan Marcellan, 244–253** | Tervan briefly improves delivery by assigning revenue to field commands and has municipal commissioners report on supplies and compliance. The offices that issue certificates under his 249 public sacrifice order also identify people and premises for seizure. Towns with grain or useful transport officers negotiate relief from other burdens. |
| **253–c. 275** | Tervan's successor suspends the general sacrifice requirement in 253 and tries to bring depot revenue back to Caleran. The eastern road command and the southern army insist on keeping enough to pay locally. Each starts acting in the emperor's name without waiting for his approval. Local governors who control the roads can also decide whether confiscated church property goes back. |
| **c. 275–288** | The court brings taxes and appointments back under one authority through negotiated returns, selective campaigns and the reassignment of commands. Officials keep copies of emergency warrants and contracts because the central treasury may deny old debts. Caleran keeps confiscation as a tool against suspected couriers, suppliers and rivals. |
| **288–292** | Cassian's advantage in the civil war is his ability to feed and pay an army on the Aurin approach and secure the cooperation of suppliers. The capital prosecutes known Redeemer networks during the contest. Those seizures leave the public Church with a backlog of claims once Cassian wins. |
| **292–316** | Cassian honours some obligations incurred by rival commanders, restores southern market treaties and seeks stability with Vashar. Once the four sees become public, he can route relief and petitioning through their officers. They have the same strengths and problems as the empire's older municipal and military networks. His court can fund them and can favour the local factions willing to work with it. [[Cassian and the Four Sees - The Settlement of 292 to 316 AR]] |

The power that port councils and provincial commands gain during these wars survives Cassian. His successors inherit the need to supply frontier armies and keep grain moving alongside the new power to fund churches and settle their civil disputes.
