# Atheria between Qiryath and Cassian

The capital that Cassian won in 292 depended on grain from the Aurin, Raveth and Sarqen, on working harbours and on soldiers willing to accept the same treasury's pay. In the centuries since Isfir the people who held the bread and the people who held the roads had learned that an emperor needed both of them, and the augur colleges had learned that he needed them as well. ## 0–78 AR · The Othrans lose the east's cooperation

Othran II remained emperor at the beginning of the age. Under him, governors continued to rely on local charters, established taxes and military grain contracts. The trial of Isfir was a provincial capital case. The governor sent the emperor his account of it in a case chest with the instruments of execution as proof of the sentence, and the chest went into the judgement archive of the Celestial Forum. In the decades after it, the Othran emperors lost their grip on the east, where tax officers, sanctuary households and local grandees stopped pulling together.

The **Great Purge** of c. 36–45 grew from a quarrel inside the augur colleges. Every college's book of answers was blank for the three days between Isfir's execution and the third morning, and the colleges had sealed those days. Tyros, the astrologer-priest turned Companion, made the silence a public question, and junior augurs and register-keepers in several provincial colleges went over to him with copies of their books. A college could enter a sign in public law, so a defected college was a threat to the High Augurate's whole authority. About 36 the High Augurate at Caleran obtained a commission of inquiry. The court granted it because the succession troubles made the colleges' ceremonies indispensable to whoever held Caleran, and because Menos's risings in the Ironvale forges threatened the army's iron. Augurs staffed the commission. It paid informers from the confiscations and charged its prisoners with forging or stealing sacred records. It crucified, drowned, burned and beheaded Companions from Virelos to Halmenth, dissolved the defected colleges and recovered their books. It ended about 45, after notables in several cities complained of its extortion. [[The Twelve Companions of the Redeemer]]

The Purge's edict against publishing an unentered wonder started the next war. In 61 the emperor's daily offering lay unburnt on Qiryath's altar for nine mornings in front of the spring pilgrims. The governor's augur entered it as a fraud, the governor sent soldiers up the sanctuary stair to burn a bull in pitch, and the custodians who stood in their way were killed with two hundred others. The Great Tower Revolt lasted until 67. The legate Karsen Maruth dug down into the deep tower of Migdal Shahar on the Dawn Road, whose households escaped through its Way-Gate in 64, and then dried out Qiryath by dropping its cistern galleries. He burned the sanctuary in 67 and found that its vessels had gone through the city's gate ahead of him. Qiryath lost its charter and gained a garrison, and the treasury laid the price of the vessels on every Jirahar household in the empire. [[Great Tower Revolt and the Second Destruction]]

Maruth's triumph carried empty litters through Caleran, and the city laughed. When the last Othran died the eastern army put Maruth forward against the capital's preferred heir. The east could field an army after the siege, but the capital controlled access to the fleet and treasury, and neither candidate could feed the other's soldiers. In 78 an Aurin supply commander, **Tavian Dascen**, won recognition after gaining the support of enough port councils to keep grain moving. He offered back pay and confirmed municipal privileges. He also confirmed direct rule at Qiryath. [[The Last Dynasties of the Age of Glory]] · [[The First Church - From Qiryath to the Tower Revolt]]

## 78–162 AR · The store-house and the roads

**Tavian Dascen, 78–91,** paid arrears, confirmed charters and let people sue for what the succession war had taken; most of the land that went back went to families who could prove they had owned it. Some soldiers returned to civilian occupations, while officers and suppliers kept gains that the treasury could not afford to unwind. He had no son. His daughter **Ysane** kept the household ledgers and dealt with the creditors, and the port councils trusted her figures more than they had ever trusted an emperor's word. He named no heir.

When he died the eastern army, which had lost in 78, marched for its commander **Daven Talar**, and the port councils shut the grain fleet in harbour for Ysane. The army held the roads through one hungry winter while the ports held the bread. In the spring of 92 the two of them married in the old store-house above Caleran's muster ground, from which Thalion had once governed, and ruled as colleagues. Soldiers called him the emperor. The ports called her the Paymistress and sent their petitions to the store-house.

Daven took his army east. The war with Vashar of c. 94–103 was fought over passage, client towns beyond Arcelon and the Dawn Portion, Vashar's share of Qiryath's pilgrim dues, which had ended with the sanctuary in 67. Atherian columns held selected road stations and for a time an outer stretch of the Avesh corridor. Convoys stretched too far and Vasharan forces recovered the exposed posts. The court claimed victory when it gained toll and safe-conduct concessions. Veterans carried injuries and ransom debts home, and captive sales fed estates and mines.

In 104 Ysane struck the remaining Avesh garrisons from the pay ledger. Daven would not march them home and would not leave them, and he governed the eastern roads from Arcelon for the rest of his life. She sent him his army's pay and he sent her his tolls. Their only son died of a coughing fever at nine. Daven gave his sister's son **Rovan Caelis** charge of the frontier, and when Daven died at Arcelon in 119 the eastern army acclaimed Rovan on the spot. Ysane was past sixty and had seen one hungry winter. She took Rovan as her colleague on the roads and went on keeping the ledger until she died at her desk in 126.

**Rovan Caelis, 119–147,** fixed supply along shorter routes. His southern treaties protected markets and required the exchange or return of designated captives. He funded canals and granaries where cities could supply labour and match the expense. Southern communities that supplied scouts and ford crews gained market privileges, and households cleared from the fort approaches kept fewer winter grazing rights. He outlived his sons, named no heir, and said the throne would go where it had gone since Tavian, to whoever could feed the capital.

In 147 the store-house was empty after his canals. The **Vessan** house, owners of the largest grain estates in Sarqen, advanced a year's pay for the southern army, and its commander **Neral Vardun** married their daughter **Irsa** and took the throne. He spent most of his reign in the south. In the war of c. 150–162 his envoys bargained separately with river clans, hill communities and coastal pilots, and several clans split over supply and the return of fugitives. In the spring of 162 three river clans laid his letters side by side at a ford gathering and found that he had promised each of them the same winter pasture. He rode out to explain and was killed on the road back to the forts. No one was ever charged.

Municipal writers remembered these eighty years for repair, regular justice and the reliability of pay, and they credited the Paymistress with most of it. A merchant whose convoy arrived, a farmer whose land was taken and a Vasharan household ransoming a relative had different accounts of those same years. Vashar retained the Ubar and the highland core, and its border towns bargained with both crowns. Jirahar exiles and their descendants sometimes knew the roads, languages and market law better than the envoys who hired them. ## 162–189 AR · The Vessan regency and the table at Sarqen

Neral's son was eight. His mother raised him at Sarqen in her father's house, he took her family's name, and until 171 Irsa Vessan and her brothers governed for **Marek Vessan**. Vessan stewards sat in the store-house where Ysane had sat. The estates bought up the army's supply contracts and met them by working their people harder and selling households apart when a contract fell due. In the southern treaty belt Marek's officers seized livestock in lieu of deliveries and interfered with local decisions over inheritance. One coalition raided the supply road while a different group kept its agreement to escort traders.

**The Sarqen rising, 186–189.** Enslaved workers from grain estates and port yards seized a store and used its shipping lists to trace people recently sold. Field hands, transport crews and some free tenants joined after troops took carts and food for the capital in the short harvest of 185. Some groups sought passage out by sea and others protected families sheltering inland.

The governor of Sarqen had sworn the spared towns' Table Oath on the landing every spring since Thalion: *no child for the table; no seat kept empty; no guest served by worth.* The towns supplied no governor who had not eaten at their table and sworn, and a stranger who reached the table could not be sold from it. On the morning of the oath in 187 a tally-woman of the port yards, **Melqa of Belqara**, walked onto the landing with some three hundred runaways and sat down. The towns' witnesses served them. The governor, a Vessan cousin, could not swear without eating among his family's property and making guests of them, and he went back to his ship.

No grain fleet sailed that year. Troops took what they could carry from the towns and fed their officers first, and at the autumn count the towns' sealed jars were found a third empty, the wax bitten from beneath, as they had been in the last year of the queens. The towns said what they had said then, that the Provider was back at the table. No fleet sailed in 188 either. Caleran's bread dole stopped, and the port councils that had made the Dascens shut the Aurin mouth to the court's ships.

The treasury sent more troops. It offered recognition of freedom to a limited number of people who turned over boats and stores, then recaptured and sold many others in dispersed lots. It could not touch the table. In 189 the towns sent Marek the message they had once sent Thalion: the landing was his if he came in person, sat down and swore. He came. He sat between a carter and Melqa, was served in his turn, ate, and swore the oath, which made every guest on those benches a guest no one could sell, his mother's people's property among them. The Vessans disowned him before he left the landing, and his escort sailed without him. At the next count the wax was whole.

Marek never left the Gateway country. He lived at Esharat as the towns' guest, as Queen Samyra had five centuries earlier, and died there about 204. Elsewhere shippers demanded armed escorts for years and owners kept sale records in separate hands, and former fugitives and relatives used dock work and marriages to reconnect households. The rising came generations after [[The Twelve Companions of the Redeemer|Menos's Ironvale risings]]. The Sarqen rebels included Star worshippers, Redeemer believers and people following other rites. Their aims came from enslavement, family separation and the grain seizure.

## 189–241 AR · An emperor's name without an emperor

Nobody deposed Marek, and nobody could use him. For fifteen years the provincial commands issued orders in the name of a man who ate at a carters' table and answered no letters. Two commands each claimed to speak for him. A commander expelled from Caleran returned to his former southern district, promised to restore its treaty terms and gained troops and guides from some towns. Other communities used the conflict to expel imperial officers, and the next court had to bargain separately with them when it tried to reclaim the crossings.

After Marek's death the commands agreed on an emperor from among themselves, and each later court relied on larger regional commands to guarantee supplies. It fought a second major war with Vashar c. 214–225. Vasharan officers seized road stations and encouraged border towns to redirect tolls east. Atherian officers pressed wagons and river craft into service, then transferred units south when forts and families there came under attack. A negotiated evacuation and exchange of captives preserved the main roads, but officers had already promised more coin and grain than the treasury could deliver. The rival states remained capable of fighting and trading. Tervan's turn to forced conformity answered that long strain, and a fear the eastern garrisons had taught him. ## 241–316 AR · From green lamps to Cassian's settlement

| Phase | Political development |
|---|---|
| **Tervan Marcellan, 241–248** | Tervan inherited eastern garrisons unpaid since the Vashar war and gripped by the Kept Lamps, a cult whose grave-lamps burned without oil and whose keepers fed the mutinies; augurs broke its central image about 243. His 245 public sacrifice order made every household relight its grave-lamps from the Stars' fire in the market and keep them burning green on the colleges' salted oil. The college stores that sold the oil also told officers which households had not bought it, and which premises to seize. The green came from verdigris scraped off the temple bronzes, and by the winter of 247 the Stars' images in half the empire's temples were pitted and bright. Tervan died on the eastern road in the spring of 248. The High Augur of Caleran would not open the year for his successor before a scraped image, and the successor suspended the edict that summer. |
| **248–288 · The sold seasons** | The colleges had made an emperor give way by refusing to open a year, and they learned what the refusal was worth. A campaign the augurs had not opened could not lawfully be paid from the treasury. Commands that could afford it bought their openings at Caleran. Commands that could not fought unopened and kept depot revenue to pay themselves. From about 281 the court and the High Augurate worked as one house: a command that held back depot money found its season voided, and a command that paid found it opened. Officers kept copies of every emergency order and supply contract, because the treasury had a habit of forgetting old debts. |
| **288–292** | In 287 the augurs voided the season of the southern frontier army, which fought the winter raids anyway, unpaid. In 288 it acclaimed its commander Cassian. His advantage in the civil war was his ability to feed and pay an army on the Aurin approach and secure the cooperation of suppliers. The capital's augurs declared his omens void. They also condemned the ground of the Redeemer congregations, whose members filled his ranks: meeting halls and burial yards were entered in the colleges' books as cursed, forfeited to the treasury and sold, and the dead were carted out of them to common pits. Those condemnations left the Church a long list of things to reclaim once Cassian won. In 291 his officers took Ironvale and heard two men swear to the flame on the Forty; his camp read their oath in the Aurin towns before the march on Velos in 292. |
| **292–316** | No battle was fought at Velos. The Flame stood on the frozen Aurin under the Stone Bridge, the ice went out beneath the arches, and the court's army came over to Cassian by companies. He had the Caleran augurs enter the open water as the emperor's sign. He honoured some obligations incurred by rival commanders, restored southern market treaties and sought stability with Vashar. About 296 he bought the bishops' acceptance of a crown warden at the bridge with empire-wide restitution. Once the four sees became public, he could route relief and petitioning through their officers. They had the same strengths and problems as the empire's older municipal and military commands. His court could fund them and could favour the local factions willing to work with it. [[Cassian and the Four Sees - The Settlement of 292 to 316 AR]] |

The power that port councils, provincial commands and sign-keepers gained during these centuries survived Cassian. His successors inherited the need to supply frontier armies and keep grain moving alongside the new power to fund churches and settle their civil disputes.
