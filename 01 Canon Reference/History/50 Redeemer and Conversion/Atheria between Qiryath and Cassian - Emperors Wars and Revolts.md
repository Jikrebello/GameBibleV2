# Atheria between Qiryath and Cassian

The capital that Cassian won in 292 depended on grain from the Aurin, Raveth and Sarqen, on working harbours and on soldiers willing to accept the same treasury's pay. The centuries since Isfir explain how the provincial commanders and councils gained enough leverage to decide a civil war. ## 0–78 AR · The Othrans lose the east's cooperation

Othran II remained emperor at the beginning of the age. Under him, governors continued to rely on local charters, established taxes and military grain contracts. The trial of Isfir was a provincial capital case. The governor sent the emperor his account of it in a case chest with the instruments of execution as proof of the sentence, and the chest went into the judgement archive of the Celestial Forum. In the decades after it, the Othran emperors lost their grip on the east, where tax officers, sanctuary households and local grandees stopped pulling together.

The **Great Purge** of c. 36–45 grew from a quarrel inside the augur colleges. Every college's book of answers was blank for the three days between Isfir's execution and the third morning, and the colleges had sealed those days. Tyros, the astrologer-priest turned Companion, made the silence a public question, and junior augurs and register-keepers in several provincial colleges went over to him with copies of their books. A college could enter a sign in public law, so a defected college was a threat to the High Augurate's whole authority. About 36 the High Augurate at Caleran obtained a commission of inquiry. The court granted it because the succession troubles made the colleges' ceremonies indispensable to whoever held Caleran, and because Menos's risings in the Ironvale forges threatened the army's iron. Augurs staffed the commission. It paid informers from the confiscations and charged its prisoners with forging or stealing sacred records. It crucified, drowned, burned and beheaded Companions from Virelos to Halmenth, dissolved the defected colleges and recovered their books. It ended about 45, after notables in several cities complained of its extortion. [[The Twelve Companions of the Redeemer]]

The Great Tower Revolt in 61–68 brought Atherian armies against Qiryath, cost the city its sanctuary and charter in 65, and left a garrison, confiscated land and an exile population.

A short succession war pitted the capital's preferred heir against an eastern commander. The east could field an army after the siege, but the capital controlled access to the fleet and treasury. In 78, an Aurin supply commander, **Tavian Dascen**, won recognition after gaining the support of enough port councils to keep grain moving. He offered back pay and confirmed municipal privileges. He also confirmed direct rule at Qiryath. [[The Last Dynasties of the Age of Glory]] · [[The First Church - From Qiryath to the Tower Revolt]] · [[Great Tower Revolt and the Second Destruction]]

## 78–171 AR · Four reigns remembered for public works and order

| Emperor | What people inherited from him |
|---|---|
| **Tavian Dascen, 78–91** | He paid arrears, confirmed charters and let people sue for what the succession war had taken; most of the land that went back went to families who could prove they had owned it. Some soldiers returned to civilian occupations, while officers and suppliers kept gains that the treasury could not afford to unwind. His adult daughter oversaw the household accounts and negotiated with creditors. He adopted **Daven Talar**, a commander the eastern army would accept. |
| **Daven Talar, 91–119** | He fought a major war with Vashar c. 94–103 over passage, client towns beyond Arcelon and the Dawn Portion, Vashar's share of Qiryath's pilgrim dues, which had ended with the sanctuary in 65. Atheria held selected road stations and briefly occupied an outer stretch of the Avesh corridor. Convoys stretched too far and Vasharan forces recovered the exposed posts. The court claimed victory when it gained toll and safe-conduct concessions. Veterans carried injuries and ransom debts home. Captive sales fed estates and mines. Daven gave his nephew Rovan charge of the surviving frontier before naming him heir. |
| **Rovan Caelis, 119–147** | He withdrew the exposed garrisons left by Daven and fixed supply along shorter routes. His southern treaties protected markets and required the exchange or return of designated captives. He funded canals and granaries where cities could supply labour and match the expense. Southern communities that supplied scouts and ford crews gained market privileges. Households cleared from the fort approaches retained fewer winter grazing rights. With no surviving son, Rovan adopted Neral, who already directed the southern commands. |
| **Neral Vardun, 147–171** | He held southern allied districts through a war c. 150–162 while paying for relief at home. His envoys bargained separately with river clans, hill communities and coastal pilots. Several clans split over supply and the return of fugitives. Neral brought his son into command early. Officials who wanted continuity accepted the arrangement, and the son inherited a large following of officers who owed their advancement to him. |

Municipal writers remembered these four reigns for repair, regular justice and the reliability of pay. A merchant whose convoy arrived, a farmer whose land was taken and a Vasharan household ransoming a relative had different accounts of those same years. Vashar retained the Ubar and the highland core, and its border towns bargained with both crowns. Jirahar exiles and their descendants sometimes knew the roads, languages and market law better than the envoys who hired them. ## 171–241 AR · Court predation, the Sarqen rising and provincial armies

**Marek Vessan, 171–185,** Neral's son, took the name of his mother's family. He began with grain distributions and a peace that allowed some units home. When pay fell behind, his household sold appointments and prosecuted merchants and officers whose assets it could seize. In the southern treaty belt, his officers seized livestock in lieu of deliveries and interfered with local decisions over inheritance. One coalition raided the supply road while a different group kept its agreement to escort traders. Guards eventually killed Marek. Over the following years, two provincial commands each gave orders in the emperor's name. A commander expelled from Caleran returned to his former southern district, promised to restore its treaty terms and gained troops and guides from some towns. Other communities used the conflict to expel imperial officers. The next court had to bargain separately with those communities when it tried to reclaim the crossings.

**The Sarqen rising, c. 186–189.** Enslaved workers from grain estates and port yards seized a store and used its shipping lists to trace people recently sold. Field hands, transport crews and some free tenants joined after troops took carts and food for the succession war. Some groups sought passage out by sea and others protected families sheltering inland. The treasury sent troops when the revolt interrupted grain delivery to Caleran. It offered recognition of freedom to a limited number of people who turned over boats and stores, then recaptured and sold many others in dispersed lots. For years, shippers demanded armed escorts and owners kept sale records in separate hands. Former fugitives and relatives used dock work and marriages to reconnect households. This rising came generations after [[The Twelve Companions of the Redeemer|Menos's Ironvale risings]]. The Sarqen rebels included Star worshippers, Redeemer believers and people following other rites. Their aims came from enslavement, family separation and the immediate grain seizure.

**c. 190–241.** The restored court relied on larger regional commands to guarantee supplies. It fought a second major war with Vashar c. 214–225. Vasharan officers seized road stations and encouraged border towns to redirect tolls east. Atherian officers pressed wagons and river craft into service, then transferred units south when forts and families there came under attack. A negotiated evacuation and exchange of captives preserved the main roads, but officers had already promised more coin and grain than the treasury could deliver. The rival states remained capable of fighting and trading. Tervan's turn to forced conformity answered that long strain, and a fear the eastern garrisons had taught him. ## 241–316 AR · From arrears to Cassian's settlement

| Phase | Political development |
|---|---|
| **Tervan Marcellan, 241–248** | Tervan inherited eastern garrisons unpaid since the Vashar war and gripped by the Kept Lamps, a cult whose grave-lamps burned without oil and whose keepers fed the mutinies; augurs broke its central image about 243. Tervan briefly improved delivery by assigning revenue to field commands and had municipal commissioners report on supplies and compliance. His 245 public sacrifice order required a witnessed offering and licensed every household's graves and lamps. The offices that issued the certificates also identified people and premises for seizure. Towns with grain or useful transport officers negotiated relief from other burdens. |
| **248–c. 281** | Tervan's successor suspended the general sacrifice requirement in 248 and tried to bring depot revenue back to Caleran. The eastern road command and the southern army insisted on keeping enough to pay locally. Each started acting in the emperor's name without waiting for his approval. Local governors who controlled the roads could also decide whether confiscated church property went back. |
| **c. 281–288** | The court brought taxes and appointments back under one authority through negotiated returns, selective campaigns and the reassignment of commands. Officers kept copies of every emergency order and supply contract, because the treasury had a habit of forgetting old debts. Caleran kept confiscation as a tool against suspected couriers, suppliers and rivals. |
| **288–292** | The frontier army acclaimed Cassian in 288. His advantage in the civil war was his ability to feed and pay an army on the Aurin approach and secure the cooperation of suppliers. The capital's augurs declared his omens void, and the capital prosecuted known Redeemer congregations during the contest, seizing their books of signs as counter-augury. Those seizures left the Church a long list of things to reclaim once Cassian won. In 291 his officers took Ironvale and heard two men swear to the flame on the Forty; his camp read their oath in the Aurin towns before the march on Velos in 292. |
| **292–316** | Cassian won under the sign at Velos and had the Caleran augurs enter it as the emperor's. He honoured some obligations incurred by rival commanders, restored southern market treaties and sought stability with Vashar. About 296 he bought the bishops' acceptance of a crown warden at the bridge with empire-wide restitution. Once the four sees became public, he could route relief and petitioning through their officers. They had the same strengths and problems as the empire's older municipal and military commands. His court could fund them and could favour the local factions willing to work with it. [[Cassian and the Four Sees - The Settlement of 292 to 316 AR]] |

The power that port councils and provincial commands gained during these wars survived Cassian. His successors inherited the need to supply frontier armies and keep grain moving alongside the new power to fund churches and settle their civil disputes.
