# Cassian and the Four Sees

## The reign in outline

Cassian won the civil war in 292 AR after seeing the White Flame at the Stone Bridge of Velos, where the Aurin has never frozen since. His reign was a long bargain over that river. He had the augur colleges enter the open water in their books as the emperor's sign, then traded a share of it to the bishops for peace: about 296 the **Velos Concord** gave the reach a chapel and a crown warden, and gave the congregations restitution of their confiscated property. He funded basilicas, granted the Caleran congregation the old judgement forum with its Pillar and the Redeemer's nails, and kept the augur colleges and Star offices that served his government. About 309 he executed his eldest son and the High Augur of Astaroth over a sealed answer about the succession. He was baptised in the unfrozen water as he was dying in 316. The daughter flame brought from Veridun Minor became the Eternal Ember during his reign. ## The river and its warden

The Aurin at Velos is a wide, slow river, and in an ordinary winter it freezes from bank to bank. Since 292 the reach under the Stone Bridge has stayed open. Carters crossing on the ice above the town can see the black water steaming below the arches, and every winter people walk out from the shore to look at it.

The sign was public and verifiable, and that raised the question of who it belonged to. The bridge and river were crown property. In imperial law a sign existed once an augur college had entered it with its witnesses and the Star that answered, so Cassian had the augurs of Caleran enter the reach as a sign answered to the emperor. The colleges had opposed him in the war. He kept them anyway, because they were the only body that could make his miracle official. The four sees would not commemorate an entry that called the Flame an "answer". The congregation at Velos kept Erom's prison chains, and claimed the sign for the city where he had walked four times into a burning orphanage.

About 296 the crown and the sees settled. Cassian built a chapel at the bridge and appointed a **Warden of the Reach**, a crown officer who sounded the ice with a weighted pole on fixed mornings each winter and entered the line of open water in a book kept at the chapel. The crown paid the clergy who accepted him. In return Cassian ordered restitution across the empire. Caleran's bishop signed first. Halmenth's signed last, after a winter of argument in which its stewards said that the bishops were selling a miracle for roof-tiles. The phrase has followed the Concord ever since.

## Who got the confiscated property back

Under the restitution decree, governors were to return property taken under anti-Redeemer orders to the local body that had held it: a meeting hall, a relief store, a burial ground. A bishop, several elders or the stewards of a burial ground could claim on that body's behalf. Governors weighed the seizure inventories against donor records, burial rolls and the memories of people who had carried bread to a meeting room. Earlier seizures, including those under Tervan, qualified where a congregation and its claim could still be identified.

Restitution took years and settled few quarrels cleanly. A ship's chandler who had bought a confiscated hall at a treasury sale might hand it back and wait a year for part of his money. A family who had let believers bury their dead in its garden kept the garden and lost the right to bar the gate. Where two successor congregations claimed the same room, the town held the keys until a hearing, and both kept burying their dead there in the meantime. The former Celestial Forum entered church use by a special grant; Star estates kept their land unless someone could prove an actual seizure. Qiryath's sanctuary lands and tribute stayed under the settlement imposed after the revolt, so a Redeemer assembly with Jirahar members could recover a confiscated meeting room while those same households still paid the tribute. The garrison and exile remained. Municipal councils resisted blanket exemptions that would put the same obligations on fewer households, and Cassian's grants funded specific buildings and services.

Cassian needed the same Star institutions for municipal continuity and military ceremonies that bishops sometimes petitioned against. He accepted a service oath before witnesses from soldiers who objected to an offering. At some forts the officer who administered the oath still joined the augurs at the traditional opening of the campaign season. Cassian also confirmed the contracts under which temple estates delivered grain. Provincial households kept supplying the state through familiar offices, and commanders gained a lawful way to retain Redeemer soldiers.

## The hearing at Halmenth, 297 AR

During the closing prosecutions an elder in Halmenth surrendered a chest after officers threatened his household. It held the congregation's letters and its book of signs, the record of healings and lamps that the capital's augurs called counter-augury. The surviving bishop restored him to fellowship. A group of confessors and the families of two arrested couriers disputed whether he could serve as steward again. They worshipped separately and claimed to represent the same pre-seizure assembly. Both groups petitioned for its hall, burial yard and relief chest. A ship supplier who had bought the hall from the treasury asked for the sale price and the cost of a new roof. Local mediation failed because the bishop's own judgement was part of the appeal.

Cassian issued travel warrants for bishops and witnesses, and sent an assessor to record the civil result. Halmenth hosted. Its own bishop gave evidence and stood aside from judging his earlier decision. Clergy and lay stewards spoke for the parties. The bishops who heard the appeal used testimony about the confiscation, election of elders and subsequent arrests. The sitting left each see with a different stake.

The bishops confirmed the elder's admission to worship and barred him from handling the community's correspondence or funds. They found enough evidence to establish the surrender of the chest. The surviving record leaves the cause of the two arrests uncertain. The governor restored the hall and relief chest to the continuing assembly through newly elected stewards and gave both groups access to the graveyard. The buyer surrendered the hall immediately and received partial reimbursement a year later. The confessors kept meeting in the rented room. A later petition asked Cassian to evict them. He refused the eviction, but assigned the next local building grant to the recognised assembly.

The four sees agreed to exchange decisions and invite outside bishops when a dispute put the local overseer on trial. The emperor could convene a hearing, enforce a property judgement and choose whom his treasury funded. Bishops still judged communion, discipline and suitability for office. In practice a grant could make one bishop's judgement easier to live by than another's.

## The forum after the court leaves

The Caleran congregation took on the pavement, drains and staff of a former judgement precinct. Families could bring the names of the condemned to a public vigil. The old Pillar stayed in the precinct where imperial officers had once read judgements. Leaded into it were the nails taken from Isfir's execution, which the governor at Qiryath had sent to Caleran in his case chest as proof of the sentence and which the Purge commission had fixed into the Pillar in the year it burned Jareth there. The crown had owned them for some two and a half centuries. With the grant it gave them away. Both later churches have used that change of hands against each other.

The keepers of Veridun Minor retained Amaryah's household lamp and brought its daughter flame and the vessel venerated as Solana's jar to Caleran. The court paid builders and put the emperor's name on the building. The keepers provided the flame, trained attendants and brought fuel from the congregation's income. At the dedication the court gave public thanks for peace and the congregation kept a vigil for the people killed at the site. Some clergy prayed for Cassian by name and declined requests to commemorate him beside the martyrs. Others read the victory at Velos as the start of a saint's life and later cited his baptism. Cassian is commemorated locally as a benefactor, and the question of his sanctity remains contested. [[The Making of Saints]]

## The sealed answer, c. 309

Cassian's own sign stood in an augural book, so his household learned to think of augural books as titles. About 309 his eldest son went privately to the High Augur of Astaroth and asked the Red Star for an answer on the succession. An entry naming him heir would have been the strongest claim in the empire. Asking about the emperor's life was a capital offence. The High Augur took the petition, the Star answered, and the entry was made and sealed.

Cassian learned of it. He executed his son and the High Augur together. The empress, whom court rumour had joined to her stepson's plan, was questioned and lived. Cassian never published the answer, and no one now knows what it said. Some chroniclers say it named Sevrin; others that it named no son of Cassian's at all; Sermoran letters of the period say it named a date. From then on a crown registrar sealed every answer that touched the throne. Sevrin inherited that sealed book and refused, for thirty-two years, to open it.

## Baptism at Velos, 316

Cassian was dying in the spring of 316. He had delayed baptism all his reign, as many great men then did, so that one washing would answer for everything. His household carried him by litter to Velos. No bishop of the four sees would baptise him in the crown's river, so the chaplain the crown paid to serve the bridge chapel did it, standing waist-deep in the open water below the arches while the ice cracked upstream.

The baptism gave rise to the **Cassian Wager**: whether a deathbed absolution answered a reign that included a son's execution. Confessors have condemned it ever since, deathbed-delayers have cited it ever since, and the dispute stays open because nobody knows whether it worked. The sees added a second question that they still argue: whether the crown's river, served by the crown's chaplain, could baptise at all.
