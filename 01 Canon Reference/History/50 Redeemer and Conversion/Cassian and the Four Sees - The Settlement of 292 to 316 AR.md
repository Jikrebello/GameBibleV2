# Cassian and the Four Sees

## The reign in outline

Cassian won the civil war in the winter of 292 AR without fighting its last battle. The White Flame stood on the frozen Aurin under the Stone Bridge of Velos, the ice went out beneath the arches, and the court's army crossed the bridge to him by companies. The river there has never frozen since. His reign was a long bargain over that water. He had the augur colleges enter it in their books as the emperor's sign, then traded a share of it to the bishops for peace: about 302 the **Velos Concord** gave the reach a chapel and a crown warden, and gave the congregations restitution of their condemned property. He funded basilicas, granted the Caleran congregation the old judgement forum with its Pillar and the Redeemer's nails, and kept the augur colleges and Star offices that served his government. About 304 he executed the High Augur of Astaroth over a sealed answer about the succession, and made the son who had asked for it chaplain of the bridge chapel. That son baptised him in the unfrozen water as he was dying in 307. The daughter flame brought from Veridun Minor became the Eternal Ember during his reign. ## The night on the ice, 292

The Aurin at Velos is a wide, slow river, and in an ordinary winter it freezes from bank to bank. In 292 the frost came early and hard. The court's army held the Stone Bridge and the north bank. Cassian, on the south bank with an army that had marched from Ironvale, meant to send his vanguard over the ice above the town in the dark and take the court's camp asleep.

His men were on the ice when the White Flame stood on the river under the arches of the bridge. Both camps saw it. The ice beneath the bridge went out with a noise that woke the town, and cracks ran upstream towards the men crossing. They came back to the bank. At dawn the reach under the Stone Bridge was open black water, steaming in the cold, and two armies stood on their own shores looking at it. Cassian said that he had seen the Flame, and so could several thousand men on the other side. Many of them had heard the Ironvale oath read in the Aurin towns that spring.

There was no battle. Over the next three days the court's companies came across the Stone Bridge to Cassian with their standards, the Redeemer soldiers first. The court's emperor rode back to Caleran with his guard and his augurs, and the port councils shut the river gate against him. Cassian's panegyrists later gave the Flame words to say that night. No two of them gave it the same words, and the soldiers who were on the ice remembered none.

## The river and its warden

Since 292 the reach under the Stone Bridge has stayed open. Carters crossing on the ice above the town can see the black water steaming below the arches, and every winter people walk out from the shore to look at it.

The sign was public and verifiable, and that raised the question of who it belonged to. The bridge and river were crown property. In imperial law a sign existed once an augur college had entered it with its witnesses and the Star that answered, so Cassian had the augurs of Caleran enter the reach as a sign answered to the emperor. The colleges had opposed him in the war. He kept them anyway, because they were the only body that could make his miracle official. For ten winters the four sees would not commemorate an entry that called the Flame an "answer". The congregation at Velos kept Erom's prison chains, and claimed the sign for the city where he had walked four times into a burning orphanage.

About 302 the crown and three of the sees settled. Cassian built a chapel at the bridge and appointed a **Warden of the Reach**, a crown officer who sounded the ice with a weighted pole on fixed mornings each winter and entered the line of open water in a book kept at the chapel. The crown paid the clergy who accepted him. In return Cassian ordered restitution across the empire. The settlement came with the grant of the Celestial Forum to Caleran's congregation, and Caleran's bishop signed first. Halmenth's signed last, three years later.

## Who got the condemned property back

Under the restitution decree, whatever had been taken from a congregation under the anti-Redeemer orders (a meeting hall, a relief store, a burial ground) was to go back to it, and a bishop, a few elders or the stewards of a graveyard could ask for it. Governors set the colleges' entries of condemned ground and the treasury's sale lists beside the burial rolls and the memories of old women who had carried bread to a meeting room. Seizures as old as Tervan's counted, if anyone was left to claim them.

Restitution took years and settled few quarrels cleanly. A merchant who had bought a condemned hall at a treasury sale might hand it back and wait a year for part of his money. A family who had let believers bury their dead in its garden kept the garden and lost the right to bar the gate. Congregations got their burial yards back empty and went digging in the common pits for their dead. Where two successor congregations claimed the same room, the town held the keys until a hearing. The former Celestial Forum entered church use by a special grant; Star estates kept their land unless someone could prove an actual seizure. Qiryath's sanctuary lands stayed under the settlement imposed after the revolt, and the Litter Debt stood, so a Redeemer assembly with Jirahar members could recover a meeting room while those same households still paid by the head for vessels lost in 72. The garrison and exile remained. Municipal councils fought any blanket exemption for church property, since the same taxes would then fall on fewer households, so Cassian paid for particular buildings instead.

Cassian still needed the Star temples the bishops petitioned against, because they ran the town festivals and opened every campaign season. He accepted a service oath before witnesses from soldiers who objected to an offering. At some forts the officer who administered the oath still joined the augurs at the traditional opening of the campaign season. Cassian also confirmed the contracts under which temple estates delivered grain. Provincial households kept supplying the state through familiar offices, and commanders gained a lawful way to retain Redeemer soldiers.

## The hearing at Halmenth, 305 AR

Halmenth would not sign. Its harbour stewards said that a see which took the emperor's restitution on the emperor's terms had agreed that the Flame's river was his, and that the bishops were selling a miracle for roof-tiles. The phrase has followed the Concord ever since, and in Halmenth it was meant exactly. The congregation's old hall on the ferry stairs had been condemned in 289 and sold by the treasury to a ship's chandler, who had put a new roof on it. Under the decree the governor could hand it back only to a see that had accepted the Warden. For three winters the hall stood locked. The chandler went unpaid, and the congregation buried its dead in rented ground while half its people asked the stewards what a river at the other end of the empire was to them.

Cassian wanted the fourth signature. He paid for the bishops and witnesses of the other three sees to travel and sent an officer to watch. Halmenth's bishop gave evidence and stood aside, since the quarrel was with his own stewards. Each see came with its own temper. Caleran wanted a named steward who could be held to account and was glad to carry the ruling to the treasury; the other sees valued that until Caleran began using its place at court to push its own favourites. Aurelium's readers laid the text of the Concord beside the augurs' entry and would let no one say what the sees had conceded until they could say what the entry claimed. Halmenth's harbour stewards insisted that the congregation go on burying its dead and drawing relief while the bishops argued. Sermora's merchants wanted clear rules for buyers like the chandler, and its bishop insisted that letters of communion carried their own authority into ports whose rulers owed Cassian nothing.

The bishops found that the Concord conceded a book and a building, and nothing about whose the sign was. Halmenth's bishop signed, with a line written under his name: the see accepted the Warden's book as a record of ice. The governor handed the hall to the congregation under newly chosen stewards. The chandler gave up the hall at once and got part of his money a year later. The stewards who had held out, most of them confessors who had come out of prison in 292, went on meeting in a rented room and would not set foot under the new roof. When a later petition asked Cassian to throw them out of the room, he refused, and gave Halmenth's next building grant to the congregation in the hall.

The four sees agreed to send one another their rulings and to call in outside bishops whenever a quarrel put a see's own judgement on trial. The emperor could call a hearing, enforce its ruling on property and choose whom his treasury paid; the bishops alone judged who could take communion and who was fit for office. In practice a building grant made one bishop's judgement much easier to live with than another's.

## The forum after the court leaves

The Caleran congregation took on the pavement, drains and staff of a former judgement precinct. Families could bring the names of the condemned to a public vigil. The old Pillar stayed in the precinct where imperial officers had once read judgements. Leaded into it were the nails taken from Isfir's execution, which the governor at Qiryath had sent to Caleran in his case chest as proof of the sentence and which the Purge commission had fixed into the Pillar in the year it burned Jareth there. The crown had owned them for some three centuries. With the grant it gave them away. Both later churches have used that change of hands against each other.

The keepers of Veridun Minor retained Amaryah's household lamp and brought its daughter flame and the vessel venerated as Solana's jar to Caleran. The court paid builders and put the emperor's name on the building. The keepers provided the flame, trained attendants and brought fuel from the congregation's income. At the dedication the court gave public thanks for peace and the congregation kept a vigil for the people killed at the site. Some clergy prayed for Cassian by name and declined requests to commemorate him beside the martyrs. Others read the night at Velos as the start of a saint's life and later cited his baptism. ## The sealed answer, c. 304

Cassian's own sign stood in an augural book, so his household learned to think of augural books as titles. About 304, two years after the Concord put a crown book at the bridge, his eldest son **Torian**, who had commanded the vanguard on the ice at Velos, went privately to the High Augur of Astaroth and asked the Red Star for an answer on the succession. An entry naming him heir would have been the strongest claim in the empire. Asking about the emperor's life was a capital offence. The High Augur took the petition, the Star answered, and the entry was made and sealed.

Cassian learned of it. He executed the High Augur. He would not kill the son who had stood on the ice, and he would not leave him an army. Torian was stripped of his command, ordained by the bishop of Caleran on the emperor's order and sent to Velos as chaplain of the bridge chapel, under the Warden's eye, with a guard on the road. The other three sees protested the ordination of a man who had not asked for it. Cassian never published the answer, and no one now knows what it said. Some chroniclers say it named Sevrin; others that it named no son of Cassian's at all; Sermoran letters of the period say it named a date. From then on a crown registrar sealed every answer that touched the throne. Sevrin inherited that sealed book and refused, for thirty-three years, to open it.

## Baptism at Velos, 307

Cassian was dying in the spring of 307, fifteen years after the night on the ice and with restitution half done. He had delayed baptism all his reign, as many great men then did, so that one washing would answer for everything. His household carried him by litter to Velos. No bishop of the four sees would baptise him in the crown's river. The chaplain the crown paid to serve the bridge chapel did it, standing waist-deep in the open water below the arches while the ice cracked upstream. The chaplain was Torian. Neither man's words were recorded.

The baptism gave rise to the **Cassian Wager**: whether a deathbed absolution answered a reign that included a civil war, an augur killed for reading what he was asked to read, and a son unmade. Confessors have condemned it ever since, deathbed-delayers have cited it ever since, and the dispute stays open because nobody knows whether it worked. The sees added two questions that they still argue. One is whether the crown's river, served by the crown's chaplain, could baptise at all. The other is whether a man ordained against his will was a chaplain.

Torian stayed at Velos. He served the bridge chapel for another thirty-four years, and died there about 341.
