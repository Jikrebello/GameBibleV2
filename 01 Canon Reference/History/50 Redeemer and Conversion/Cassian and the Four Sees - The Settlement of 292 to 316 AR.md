# Cassian and the Four Sees

## The reign in outline

Cassian won the civil war in 292 AR after seeing the White Flame at the Stone Bridge of Velos, where the Aurin has never frozen since. His reign was a long bargain over that river. He had the augur colleges enter the open water in their books as the emperor's sign, then traded a share of it to the bishops for peace: about 296 the **Velos Concord** gave the reach a chapel and a crown warden, and gave the congregations restitution of their confiscated property. He funded basilicas, granted the Caleran congregation the old judgement forum with its Pillar and the Redeemer's nails, and kept the augur colleges and Star offices that served his government. About 309 he executed his eldest son and the High Augur of Astaroth over a sealed answer about the succession. He was baptised in the unfrozen water as he was dying in 316. The daughter flame brought from Veridun Minor became the Eternal Ember during his reign. ## The river and its warden

The Aurin at Velos is a wide, slow river, and in an ordinary winter it freezes from bank to bank. Since 292 the reach under the Stone Bridge has stayed open. Carters crossing on the ice above the town can see the black water steaming below the arches, and every winter people walk out from the shore to look at it.

The sign was public and verifiable, and that raised the question of who it belonged to. The bridge and river were crown property. In imperial law a sign existed once an augur college had entered it with its witnesses and the Star that answered, so Cassian had the augurs of Caleran enter the reach as a sign answered to the emperor. The colleges had opposed him in the war. He kept them anyway, because they were the only body that could make his miracle official. The four sees would not commemorate an entry that called the Flame an "answer". The congregation at Velos kept Erom's prison chains, and claimed the sign for the city where he had walked four times into a burning orphanage.

About 296 the crown and the sees settled. Cassian built a chapel at the bridge and appointed a **Warden of the Reach**, a crown officer who sounded the ice with a weighted pole on fixed mornings each winter and entered the line of open water in a book kept at the chapel. The crown paid the clergy who accepted him. In return Cassian ordered restitution across the empire. Caleran's bishop signed first. Halmenth's signed last, after a winter of argument in which its stewards said that the bishops were selling a miracle for roof-tiles. The phrase has followed the Concord ever since.

## Who got the confiscated property back

Under the restitution decree, whatever had been taken from a congregation under the anti-Redeemer orders (a meeting hall, a relief store, a burial ground) was to go back to it, and a bishop, a few elders or the stewards of a graveyard could ask for it. Governors set the treasury's seizure lists beside the burial rolls and the memories of old women who had carried bread to a meeting room. Seizures as old as Tervan's counted, if anyone was left to claim them.

Restitution took years and settled few quarrels cleanly. A merchant who had bought a confiscated hall at a treasury sale might hand it back and wait a year for part of his money. A family who had let believers bury their dead in its garden kept the garden and lost the right to bar the gate. Where two successor congregations claimed the same room, the town held the keys until a hearing, and both kept burying their dead there in the meantime. The former Celestial Forum entered church use by a special grant; Star estates kept their land unless someone could prove an actual seizure. Qiryath's sanctuary lands and tribute stayed under the settlement imposed after the revolt, so a Redeemer assembly with Jirahar members could recover a confiscated meeting room while those same households still paid the tribute. The garrison and exile remained. Municipal councils fought any blanket exemption for church property, since the same taxes would then fall on fewer households, so Cassian paid for particular buildings instead.

Cassian still needed the Star temples the bishops petitioned against, because they ran the town festivals and opened every campaign season. He accepted a service oath before witnesses from soldiers who objected to an offering. At some forts the officer who administered the oath still joined the augurs at the traditional opening of the campaign season. Cassian also confirmed the contracts under which temple estates delivered grain. Provincial households kept supplying the state through familiar offices, and commanders gained a lawful way to retain Redeemer soldiers.

## The hearing at Halmenth, 297 AR

During the closing prosecutions an elder in Halmenth handed over a chest after officers threatened his household. It held the congregation's letters and its book of signs, the record of healings and lamps that the capital's augurs called counter-augury, and two couriers were arrested soon afterwards. The surviving bishop took the elder back. The confessors who had come out of prison, and the couriers' families, would not share a table with him. By 297 Halmenth had two congregations, each claiming to be the one that had lost the hall, the burial yard and the relief chest, and both burying their dead in the same yard. A ship's chandler who had bought the hall at a treasury sale wanted his money back and the price of the new roof he had put on it. No one in Halmenth could settle it, because the confessors' quarrel was with the bishop's own judgement.

Cassian paid for the bishops and witnesses to travel and sent an officer to watch. Halmenth's bishop gave evidence and stood aside. Each see came with its own temper. Caleran wanted a named steward who could be held to account and was glad to carry the ruling to the treasury; the other sees valued that until Caleran began using its place at court to push its own favourites. Aurelium's readers laid the seizure list beside the surviving copies of the letters and would let no one be blamed for an arrest until they could say what the chest had held. Halmenth's harbour stewards insisted that both congregations go on burying their dead and drawing relief while the bishops argued. Sermora's merchants wanted clear rules for buyers like the chandler, and its bishop insisted that letters of communion carried their own authority into ports whose rulers owed Cassian nothing.

The bishops let the elder back to worship and kept him away from the letters and the money. They found that he had handed over the chest; what caused the two arrests, the record leaves open. The governor gave the hall and the relief chest to the continuing congregation under newly chosen stewards, and let both groups use the graveyard. The chandler gave up the hall at once and got part of his money a year later. The confessors went on meeting in a rented room. When a later petition asked Cassian to throw them out of it, he refused, and gave Halmenth's next building grant to the other congregation.

The four sees agreed to send one another their rulings and to call in outside bishops whenever a quarrel put the local bishop's own judgement on trial. The emperor could call a hearing, enforce its ruling on property and choose whom his treasury paid; the bishops alone judged who could take communion and who was fit for office. In practice a building grant made one bishop's judgement much easier to live with than another's.

## The forum after the court leaves

The Caleran congregation took on the pavement, drains and staff of a former judgement precinct. Families could bring the names of the condemned to a public vigil. The old Pillar stayed in the precinct where imperial officers had once read judgements. Leaded into it were the nails taken from Isfir's execution, which the governor at Qiryath had sent to Caleran in his case chest as proof of the sentence and which the Purge commission had fixed into the Pillar in the year it burned Jareth there. The crown had owned them for some three centuries. With the grant it gave them away. Both later churches have used that change of hands against each other.

The keepers of Veridun Minor retained Amaryah's household lamp and brought its daughter flame and the vessel venerated as Solana's jar to Caleran. The court paid builders and put the emperor's name on the building. The keepers provided the flame, trained attendants and brought fuel from the congregation's income. At the dedication the court gave public thanks for peace and the congregation kept a vigil for the people killed at the site. Some clergy prayed for Cassian by name and declined requests to commemorate him beside the martyrs. Others read the victory at Velos as the start of a saint's life and later cited his baptism. ## The sealed answer, c. 309

Cassian's own sign stood in an augural book, so his household learned to think of augural books as titles. About 309 his eldest son went privately to the High Augur of Astaroth and asked the Red Star for an answer on the succession. An entry naming him heir would have been the strongest claim in the empire. Asking about the emperor's life was a capital offence. The High Augur took the petition, the Star answered, and the entry was made and sealed.

Cassian learned of it. He executed his son and the High Augur together. The empress, whom court rumour had joined to her stepson's plan, was questioned and lived. Cassian never published the answer, and no one now knows what it said. Some chroniclers say it named Sevrin; others that it named no son of Cassian's at all; Sermoran letters of the period say it named a date. From then on a crown registrar sealed every answer that touched the throne. Sevrin inherited that sealed book and refused, for thirty-two years, to open it.

## Baptism at Velos, 316

Cassian was dying in the spring of 316. He had delayed baptism all his reign, as many great men then did, so that one washing would answer for everything. His household carried him by litter to Velos. No bishop of the four sees would baptise him in the crown's river, so the chaplain the crown paid to serve the bridge chapel did it, standing waist-deep in the open water below the arches while the ice cracked upstream.

The baptism gave rise to the **Cassian Wager**: whether a deathbed absolution answered a reign that included a son's execution. Confessors have condemned it ever since, deathbed-delayers have cited it ever since, and the dispute stays open because nobody knows whether it worked. The sees added a second question that they still argue: whether the crown's river, served by the crown's chaplain, could baptise at all.
