# The Temple That Would Not Burn

The Temple That Would Not Burn is an Underground-era tradition concerning a damaged Star temple at Cyrian which local believers had adapted for clandestine Redeemer worship. About 246 AR, commissioners enforcing Emperor Tervan Marcellan's edict against unlicensed grave- and shrine-lamps tried three times to destroy the building by fire. The structure survived, while soldiers and guards died during the attempts. The commissioners' clerks entered each failure in the Cyrian augurs' book of answers, and those entries are the oldest record of the event. Eclessian, Thalmyric and surviving imperial accounts disagree about its cause and meaning.

> _“They lit the flame. But the flame refused them.”_  
> —Inscription carved into the last standing wall of the Temple at Cyrian

### **Date**: c. 245–247 AR

The enforcement years of Tervan Marcellan's Edict of Public Sacrifice, which required every household to offer before the Stars and to license the lamps it kept for its dead.

### **Location**:

**Cyrian**, a mid-sized provincial city in the western empire, known for its marble quarries and observatory-temple to **Lyraxis**, the Muse-Star.

---

### **Overview**

The Temple at Cyrian was among the first Star Pantheon temples taken over by Redeemer believers for clandestine worship. Records preserved in both **Eclessian ecclesiastical histories** and **Thalmyric chronicles of early miracles** agree on the outline.

An earthquake had partly collapsed the temple, which many in the faith took as a sign of divine displeasure with the Stars. Local believers then turned its inner sanctum into a **shrine to the Redeemer**, hanging chains over the shattered altar and kindling a fire there each night in the name of mercy.

To the commissioners who arrived with Tervan's edict, a nightly fire in a ruined Star temple, tended by people who would show no licence and would not say for whom it burned, looked like the Kept Lamps. That cult had set lamps over the graves of unpaid soldiers in the eastern garrisons, lamps that leaned towards mourners and burned without oil, and its keepers had fed the mutinies that frightened Tervan into his edict. The commissioners at Cyrian were not careless men. They treated the shrine as an Idol's nest and ordered the whole complex burned as a purgation and a public warning.

But **it would not burn.**

---

### **Recorded Attempts to Destroy the Temple**

1. **First Attempt**:  
    Pitch-soaked tapestries, dry wood, and sacred oils were poured through the nave and ignited.  
    The flames erupted, then **snuffed out instantly**, leaving the star-forged pillars **glowing cold**.
    
2. **Second Attempt** (Three days later):  
    A pyre of manuscripts and carved effigies was constructed inside. Imperial witnesses swore the fire **rose up**, but **would not consume the stone**—and instead **burned one of the soldiers**, though no embers touched him.
    
3. **Third Attempt** (Festival of Astaroth):  
    The local Prefect ordered a bonfire lit in each corner of the temple and recited curses against the Redeemer. That night, **a lightning storm struck**, killing two of the prefect’s guards.  
    When dawn broke, the temple still stood—charred, but intact.

The commissioners' clerks took each failure to the Cyrian augurs, as the law required for any wonder, and the college entered all three in its book of answers. It could name no Star that had answered, so the entries stand without an author: three refusals of fire, witnessed, dated and unexplained. When the bishops took over the colleges' books in 383, their readers found the Cyrian entries and copied them. They are the earliest official record of the event and the oldest evidence behind the pilgrimage that grew there.

### **Interpretations and Scholarly Debates**

####  **Eclessian View (Ecclesia Aurelia, High Council Histories)**:

The Temple at Cyrian is considered one of the **Three Silent Miracles** of the Underground Centuries, which the late Eclessian [[Codex of The Era of Blood and Ash]] counts among its "Confirmed Early Miracle Sites". It represents **divine resistance**: the Holy Flame cannot be wielded by tyranny, and the sanctuary of the Redeemer cannot be destroyed by injustice. Eclessian writers make much of the fact that the first record of the miracle was kept by its enemies.

It is often cited in **sermons about spiritual fortitude** and appears in religious art as a **ruined colonnade bathed in gold firelight**, often with a single unburned candle atop a cracked altar.

---

#### **Thalmyric Interpretation (Order of the Returning Standard)**

The Thalmyric Church interprets the event not as preservation—but as **transmutation**. The temple’s refusal to burn is seen as the **first proof that the Redeemer’s flame is purifying, not destructive**. It burned away unworthy intentions and false worship—but preserved that which had been consecrated anew.

For Thalmyric mystics, Cyrian is symbolic of **inner sanctification**—that the body may suffer, but the soul, if bound to the Flame, cannot be consumed.

---

####  **Imperial Secular Records (Codex Aurelianus, Volume IX)**:

One archivist dismisses the incident entirely, claiming the temple was built with **anomalously resistant quarry stone** and **high humidity** from nearby springs. The "failed burnings," according to this version, were due to **logistical incompetence** and poor weather.

This view is echoed in some modern Concord universities as an example of **“miraculous embellishment through oral transmission.”** Its critics answer that the augurs' entries were written within days of each burning by men who wanted the temple gone.

---

### **Legacy**

- The **site of the Temple of Cyrian** became a **pilgrimage destination** after the faith was legalised.
- A cathedral now stands beside the ruins, built from the original stone—its altar still crowned with **unchanging flame** in some accounts.
- Redeemer iconography often shows the temple as **a heart-shaped structure encircled by fire**—a symbol of **endurance through trial**.
