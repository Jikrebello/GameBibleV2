# The Conversion of the Empire (292–398 AR)

> _“They tried to silence the Flame. Then they tried to own it.”_
> —later summary of the Ember Years

The conversion of the Atherian Empire ran from Cassian's vision at the Stone Bridge of Velos in 292 AR to the death of Vestian the Pious in 398. In that century the Redeemer's faith went from hunted to tolerated, from tolerated to favoured, and from favoured to compulsory. Beneath the changes of ruler ran a quarrel as old as the empire: who had the right to say what a wonder meant. For centuries the augur colleges had decided which signs existed in law. Cassian won his throne under a sign anyone could check in winter, Astrion answered it with a sign of his own and spent twenty years as a crippled rival emperor waiting for another, and Vestian gave the power of naming signs to the bishops, then watched thousands of lamps refuse to kindle at the ceremonies of his law. Believers held the miracles of the age to be genuine. By its end they had also become instruments of coercion.

## Prelude: The Underground Centuries (1–291 AR)

The empire that Isfir's followers entered was governed by answers. Every chartered augur college kept a book of what the Stars had said: which campaign the Red Star had blessed, which ground was cursed, which election was void. Augurs watched the sky from temple roofs and read the livers of sheep on the steps below, and their answers were read aloud in the forums on market days. A wonder the colleges had never entered was, in law, either a fraud or a cult feeding something.

The colleges' own books open the age with a gap. From the hour of the Binding at Qiryath to the third morning, no college in the empire recorded any answer at all. High Augur Severan, who had urged Isfir's execution, never read again. The colleges sealed those three days. Tyros, a Companion who had been an astrologer-priest, learned of them from former colleagues and talked about them in public, and junior augurs in several provincial colleges followed him with copies. The High Augurate answered with the commission of the **Great Purge** (c. 36–45 AR), which burned Jareth in the Celestial Forum, beheaded Tyros and Aquilan, and charged its prisoners with forging or stealing sacred records. Its edict made it a crime to publish a wonder the colleges had not entered, and congregations learned to write their miracles down as weather and good sense. [[The Twelve Companions of the Redeemer]] · [[The First Church - From Qiryath to the Tower Revolt|The First Church]]

Redeemer communities were then illegal or precariously tolerated for more than two centuries. Enforcement varied by emperor, governor, city and crisis. Quiet decades let households, workshops, burial societies and local clergy take root; purges destroyed congregations and made martyrs whose memory travelled farther than official proclamations. Caleran, Aurelium, Halmenth and Sermora became the principal regional sees by the third century.

Magistrates had their own reasons to fear the faith. In the unpaid eastern garrisons of the 240s a cult called the **Kept Lamps** promised that comrades who had died owed their wages were "kept" by lamps over their graves. The lamps leaned towards mourners and burned without oil, and the garrisons that tended them mutinied. Augurs broke the cult's central image about 243. When Emperor Tervan Marcellan issued his Edict of Public Sacrifice in 245, every household had to put out the lamps it kept for its dead before the Stars in the market and relight them from the college fire, and the colleges' salted oil made them burn green. For three years the empire's burial grounds glowed green at night, and the watch walked the graves looking for yellow flames. The Redeemer burial fraternities, who kept lamps for executed men, were the first it found. The green came from verdigris scraped off the temple bronzes, and his successor suspended the edict in 248 after the High Augur of Caleran refused to open the year before a scraped image. In the forty years that followed the colleges sold the opening of campaign seasons to the commands that could pay. During the civil war of 288–291 the court holding Caleran turned the same books on the congregations, whose members filled Cassian's ranks. Its augurs entered their meeting halls and burial yards as cursed ground. The treasury sold the halls, and the dead were carted out of the yards to common pits. The empire's succession wars, fighting with Vashar and the southern Folk, and the rising of enslaved workers in Sarqen explain how the provincial commands and ports gained leverage over Caleran. [[Atheria between Qiryath and Cassian - Emperors Wars and Revolts|Atheria between Qiryath and Cassian]]

## The River That Will Not Freeze (292–316 AR)

In 292 AR the usurper **Cassian**, acclaimed by his frontier army four years earlier, marched on Caleran. Many of his soldiers had heard the Ironvale oath read aloud in the Aurin towns that spring. The frost came early that winter, and at Velos the court's army held the Stone Bridge while the Aurin froze from bank to bank. Cassian sent his vanguard over the ice in the dark to take the court's camp asleep. The White Flame stood upon the river under the arches of the bridge, the ice beneath it went out with a noise that woke the town, and his men came back to the bank. At dawn two armies looked at the same open water. No battle was fought. Over three days the court's companies crossed the bridge to Cassian with their standards, and he entered Caleran and legalised the Redeemer's faith. His panegyrists later gave the Flame words to say that night, and no two gave it the same ones. The river at the bridge has not frozen since. Every winter the Aurin above and below Velos carries ice thick enough for carts, and every winter the reach beneath the bridge runs black and open, steaming in the cold.

Anyone could check the sign, so the quarrel at once turned to who owned it. River and bridge were crown property. Imperial law still knew only the answers in the augurs' books, so Cassian had the augurs of Caleran enter the reach in their books as a sign answered to the emperor. That was why he kept the colleges: they made his miracle official. The four sees refused to commemorate an entry that called the Flame an "answer". The congregation at Velos, keeper of the martyr Erom's prison chains, claimed the sign for its own city.

About 296 the crown and the sees settled in the **Velos Concord**. Cassian built a chapel at the bridge, appointed a **Warden of the Reach** to measure the ice line each winter and keep its book, and paid the clergy who accepted him. In return he ordered restitution across the empire: condemned meeting rooms, relief stores and burial grounds were to go back to the congregations that had held them. Caleran's bishop signed first. Halmenth's stewards said the bishops were selling a miracle for roof-tiles, and their see held out for a year. Critics in every later century have said the same.

Restitution took years. Governors weighed household deeds against burial rolls and the memories of old women who had carried bread to a meeting room. Buyers who had bought condemned halls in good faith at treasury sales could petition for their money back. Families who had let believers bury their dead in a garden kept the garden and lost the right to bar the gate. Congregations got their burial yards back empty and dug in the common pits for their dead. At Qiryath the older settlement stood: the garrison, the Litter Debt and the seizure of sanctuary land remained in force.

Cassian accepted a witnessed service oath from soldiers who objected to an offering, renewed grain contracts with Star estates, restored southern market treaties, renewed Elmaric convoy contracts and secured a prisoner exchange with Vashar. In 297 he paid for the other three sees to travel to Halmenth, which still refused the Concord while its condemned hall stood locked and its buyer unpaid. The bishops found that the Concord conceded a book and a building and nothing about whose the sign was, and Halmenth signed with the words "as a record of ice" under its bishop's name. Bishops judged communion and eligibility for church office. Governors enforced property rulings, and the treasury could favour one congregation through its grants. [[Cassian and the Four Sees - The Settlement of 292 to 316 AR|Cassian and the Four Sees]]

Cassian granted Caleran's former Celestial Forum precinct to the local congregation and paid for a basilica. With the precinct came the Pillar of Concordance, where judgements had once been read, and the Redeemer's nails, which the Purge commission had leaded into it. The crown had held them for some three centuries before it gave them away. Keepers from Veridun Minor brought a daughter flame from Amaryah's household lamp. Public worship brought the capital's commemoration of Jareth and its other martyrs together with the household tradition from Qiryath. The sanctuary flame became the Eternal Ember. [[Caleran and the Eternal Ember]]

Because the crown's own sign stood in an augural book, Cassian's eldest son **Torian**, who had led the vanguard onto the ice at Velos, went to the High Augur of Astaroth about 309 and asked for an answer on the succession. An entry naming him heir would have been the strongest title in the empire. Asking about the emperor's life was a capital offence. The answer was given and sealed. Cassian executed the High Augur. He had his son ordained by the bishop of Caleran, over the protests of the other sees, and shut him into the bridge chapel at Velos as its chaplain. What the sealed entry said has never been published. From then on a crown registrar sealed every answer touching the throne.

Cassian was dying in 316. His household carried him to Velos, and the crown-paid chaplain of the bridge baptised him in the unfrozen water, since no bishop of the four sees would do it there. The chaplain was Torian. The baptism gave rise to the **Cassian Wager**, the unresolved dispute over whether one final absolution answered a reign that included a civil war, an augur executed for answering and a son unmade. The sees still ask two further questions: can the crown's river baptise, and was a man ordained against his will a chaplain?

## Contested Coexistence (316–348 AR)

For a generation after Cassian, Stars and Flame shared the empire unevenly. Old temples retained estates and municipal obligations. Bishops competed over confiscated property and imperial access. Mixed households kept both calendars and lit lamps for both. Local officials could protect one congregation, persecute another, and call both acts public order.

This was the generation in which a hunted Church learned to keep accounts. Witnesses of the martyrdoms still lived, but their successors argued about courts, schools, basilicas, military chaplains, and whether imperial favour was providence or a subtler trial.

In 316 AR, Cassian's younger son **Sevrin** secured Caleran and confirmed his cousin **Adran** in the eastern command in return for recognition, on terms that put Adran's line next in the succession after Sevrin's sons. Sevrin kept the restitution judgements, the witnessed service oath and the grain contracts with Star estates. He refused every petition to hand the wardenship of the reach to the bishops, whose chapel held his elder brother until about 341, and he refused to open the sealed answer that had unmade Torian. Sevrin's only son died c. 343. Adran's son Astrion gained command of the northern desert approaches and succeeded Sevrin in 348 under the terms of 316. [[The Reign of Sevrin]]

The augurs still held the legal power to say what a sign was, and they used it. About 338 the wardens of the Red Star loaded a ruined shrine of Astaroth at Veridun Minor before the town built on it, and the altar step cracked open on a chamber holding a woman's bones and a broken sword hilt. The four sees claimed them for Calira; the Star college, whose own test had found them and which was still the lawful judge of such finds, entered them as the remains of a Star-priestess. [[The Apostle’s Bones]]

Along the quarry roads a worse dispute ran for two generations. A prison worker who had carried bread and the names of the dead between the quarry cells and their families had been executed in the last persecutions, and his body thrown into a lime trench. After legalisation two shrines claimed him: the quarry town's crowned icon of an unbroken confessor, and a weeping image at a road sanctuary several days away. Both worked signs. Relic hunters broke bones in the lime trench, custodians killed his cousin for refusing to sell the dead man's work shirt, a man who had recanted beside him was murdered and thrown into a quarry pit, and the family's house was burned. Pilgrims of the two shrines fought on the road between them. Investigators sent by the sees later found that each image went quiet when its quarrel with the other was taken away, and wrote that neither had the man. His family still lines his empty grave with uncarved stones. [[G-70 - The Empty Grave and the Two False Shrines|The Empty Grave and the Two False Shrines]] · [[The Hunger Wars]]

## Astrion's Restoration (348–351 AR)

**Astrion the Apostate**, Cassian's great-nephew, restored temple stipends and had the Purge's old edict read again in the forums. Under the order the towns called **the Entering**, every wonder the Church had claimed since 292 was to be brought before an augur college and entered or struck. Redeemer worship and Cassian's restitution judgements remained lawful. He had the augurs re-enter the reach at Velos as an answer of Hecatara, the Star of crossings. The bishops called the entry a forgery, and the crowds at the bridge went on calling the open water the Flame's. Astrion also authorised survey work at Qiryath after Jirahar petitions for access, water and relief from the Litter Debt. When the wardens of the Red Star set up their frame over the galleries that a broken frame had brought down in the siege, the Jirahar crews put down their tools, the garrison flogged forty of them, and the work stopped. The survey's lists stayed in the provincial archive.

In 350 his augurs staged a public test at a Caleran mustering ground, meant to give the Stars a sign as public as the river. They loaded a bronze standard and its stone socket. The Red Star answered once, by exposing a weakness in the loaded support before engineers cleared the ground. Soldiers took it as a promise of victory, engineers as a warning and Redeemer clergy as proof that a created power could answer. Astrion wanted a second test; the augurs would give no terms for it. Their signed account eventually reached the Sanctum and was sealed.

After a quarrel over requisitions, Astrion took a punitive column beyond the northern depot line. Local defenders closed secondary wells and drove off animals. On the retreat the baggage jammed at a dry wash, and the army's carpenters bridged it in a night with roof beams from a captured store. There was no time to load the span before the Red Star, as every public work was meant to be loaded, and emperors had excused themselves from standing beneath new works since Velen I. Astrion refused the excuse, refused the master carpenter who offered to stand there for him, and stood under the span while the wagons crossed. It held for the wagon carrying the augurs' register and broke under the wagons of wounded behind it. He was dug out with his hips and legs crushed, at thirty-four. The surgeons expected him dead by morning, and a courier rode for Caleran to say that he was. The field record gives him one question from that night: had the baggage with the register crossed? The commander of his guard, **Savel Orren**, was offered the throne beside the wreck and answered that the guard did not bury living men. Astrion lived, and never stood again. Preachers said the Stars had broken their own priest-king and had not troubled to finish him. Sestaran humanists keep the story as tragedy. ## The Water-Bringer and the Two Standards (351–377 AR)

The column was dying of thirst when **Tellan**, Astrion's younger brother, came up the eastern depot road with water bought from the Well-Tarkhan carriers who had kept their contracts. The skins came on camels under the bronze standard of the 350 test. The eastern army has called it the **Answered Standard** ever since, and called Tellan the Water-Bringer.

The first courier reached Caleran weeks ahead of the truth. The capital believed the emperor dead, and the bishops, the port councils and the guard made **Lucar Aster** emperor: Sevrin's son-in-law and commander of the southern reserve, whose elder brother Corlan held the Aurin approaches for him. When the second courier brought word that Astrion lived and would never walk, Caleran kept the emperor it had. Lucar repealed the Entering and restored church stipends.

The east kept Astrion. Its units carried him to every muster in a litter and set him down under the Answered Standard. About 357, after two seasons of stand-off on the road garrisons, the two courts settled in the **Accord of the Two Standards**. Astrion kept the name of emperor east of the depot line for his lifetime and could name no heir to it. The east kept its revenue share on Sevrin's old terms, its augurs and its standard. Eastern units swore a Star oath beneath it, and western units swore Cassian's service oath. Each spring Astrion's augurs asked the Red Star for terms on which it would answer a second time, and each spring they wrote down honestly that it had given none. He died at Arcelon about 372. Tellan kept the command and never claimed the throne.

Corlan ran the southern defences for his brother until he died inspecting them about 366, and Lucar took the south into his own hands. He kept the wardenship at Velos in crown hands and sold the chapel's revenue to bishops who accepted his terms for army chaplains. The court increasingly decided which bishops could draw public revenue. Star temples still held estates in some districts.

The southern army had crossed the river-courts' fords for eighty years behind Folk guides from the fort districts, who waded first with their households' small iron, bone and hair tokens tied at the wrist while the column followed on a rope. From about 368 Lucar's chaplains took the tokens as idols, from the guides and from every Folk trader at the fort markets, and the officers sold the silver ones. Then came the winters of 369–371, the hardest in living memory, and Grobi war-bands came down the frozen rivers. With no guides the garrisons could reach the far banks only by the bridges, and they watched allied villages burn. A ford would still pass a column that delivered a traveller: one person who walked in ahead of the rope and came out on the far bank unable to return. The officers took a child from each allied household of the fort districts and walked the children into the fords. Stewards sold some of the children they had been given for the crossings and wrote them down as delivered. From 372 the fort-district households slipped away across the fords at night on whatever tokens they had hidden, towards the children standing on the far banks, and the forts hanged the token-carriers at the water. In the spring of 377 the last of them gathered below Kellovar, the hill where Casvilar had starved the Folk seven and a half centuries earlier, and crossed out of the empire in the night. Lucar came south to bring back the guides his forts could not do without. At dawn his army followed them into the ford behind its chaplains' blessing, with no token and no one delivered, and the river took it.

Lucar himself crossed dry, with a confiscated silver token on his horse's breast-strap, and came out of the water among the households whose children his officers had walked into the fords. The house whose token it was received him at its fire under hearth-law for three days and sent him back across the ford. On the near bank the survivors of his rearguard, who had seen him ride dry out of the river that drowned their comrades, pulled him from the saddle and killed him. **Vestian** had kept the inner depots open, paid the surviving units and been recognised at Caleran while Lucar was still across the river. He read the ford as a judgement on an empire that served two kinds of sacred authority, and put no one on trial for Lucar's death. ## Vestian and Compulsory Faith (377–398 AR)

**Emperor Vestian the Pious** came to power convinced that signs were real and must be held by one authority. As the price of Caleran's recognition and the sees' help in feeding the towns, he gave the bishops the chapel at Velos and its winter book. River and bridge stayed crown property. Folk communities that negotiated settlement under their own elders were settled by 379, and investigators began tracing the children the fort stewards had sold.

In 383 Vestian dissolved the augur colleges, beginning at Caleran. The last High Augur, **Ilios Marrant**, asked leave for one final petition to the Red Star on behalf of the college that had made it answer in 350. Vestian granted it before a crowd in the Celestial Forum, in front of the Pillar that holds the Nails. The augurs set no load and named no price. They asked the Star to show that the college still deserved its office. The crowd waited through the appointed time, nothing answered, and people went home saying the Stars had been beaten. An augur who later converted wrote down Marrant's own reading of the silence: the Red Star had never answered a petition without a field, and would not begin for a college with nothing left to test. Then the college seal was broken, and its sealed books, the **Deep Register**, went to the bishop's readers. By imperial statute the right to say which signs existed in law passed to the bishops of recognised communion. The provincial colleges followed by 384.

The eastern command, eleven years after Astrion's death, refused to give up its augurs. In the **War of the Two Standards** (383–384) Vestian cut the east off from port grain and called on southern contingents under the new Folk agreements. At the decisive crossing the eastern augurs asked the Red Star for terms. None came. The Answered Standard went into the field unanswered, and the eastern Redeemer units negotiated their own surrender. Tellan ended his life a pensioned captive in a house above the Aurin, visited by his old soldiers on the anniversary of the water. Caleran's inventory says the standard was melted for a church bell; eastern veterans' families said for generations that it lay buried under a depot road. The augurs' account of 350 went to Aurelium. Vestian had won his war because the Stars would not answer his enemy.

In 385 he made the oath that had ended the eastern war into imperial law. Under **the Profession**, every officeholder and then every household head in the directly governed provinces professed the faith before recognised clergy and lit a reception lamp from a church flame. Congregations had kept such lamps since the first century. Now and then a newly baptised believer's lamp took a white, unconsuming fire before the assembly, rarely enough that Underground letters said only that "the lamp took well". The chancery announced that the ceremonies would show the Flame confirming the realm. Vestian expected the confirmation as a sign that the Redeemer's return was near.

The lamps burned yellow. In town after town, in squares hung with imperial banners, thousands of reception lamps burned like any oil, while in the parish churches of the same towns willing baptisms went on kindling at the old uneven rate. The court circulated a handful of white flames at state ceremonies; the confessors noted that most had fallen to people who had sought baptism before the decree. The bishops divided. Aurelium's readers compiled a register of the unkindled professions district by district and sealed it. Caleran ruled that a properly witnessed profession had civil force whether or not anything kindled. Halmenth refused to use the lamp at any profession made under compulsion. In Sermora and the gateway ports, congregations of Jirahar descent said aloud that they had heard this silence before, at Qiryath, and called it the **Second Silence**. The court called that slander.

Vestian's court blamed pollution: Star images hidden in cupboards, offerings to household spirits, and what its preachers called "Jirahar blindness" in the descendants of the old council. About 387 it banned household rites altogether and quickened its orders against the eastern towers. About 389 the state professions changed to a sealed written oath. The lamp went out of the ceremony, and with it the public record of the silence. [[Vestian Persecutions and the Long Diaspora]]

The closing of the temples did damage of its own. A temple was an employer, and sometimes the keeper of a bargain older than the empire. Some sanctuaries, the bishops' readers reported, held Masks that had fed on their worshippers; some held only frightened priests, and some kept compacts with the powers of a river or a spring that were still in force. On the Weiss, a temple whose priests had paid a river's court each spring was closed in the 380s, and the next flood took the lower town. The church built on its foundations still has a crypt where something is paid every spring, and its clergy will not say what. The old-cult families called these years the **War of the Closed Doors**.

In the emptied sanctuaries a counter-church grew. The **Preacher of the Second Gospel**, a former temple copyist whose bishop had removed him from teaching, gathered displaced temple workers and abandoned fever households and offered them real minor wonders as proof that what works is true. He presided over captive sacrifice, ordered a child used in a possession trial and had a former companion murdered for the record of his own discipline. Once the colleges had gone, his were the only wonders in the empire that no bishop had licensed, and they drew converts away from the lawful Church. [[G-25 - The Ragged Gospel in the Hospital Apron|The Ragged Gospel in the Hospital Apron]] · [[Saints and Sinners of the Chronicles]]

Vestian's reign welded sanctity to state power. Sincere bishops gained the means to end old persecutions and also the means to persecute. Court converts used heresy and temple law to settle property claims, destroy rivals and rewrite family histories. The old augur families kept some of their wealth by selling their archives, their offices and their good names to the new order. Vestian died in 398.

## The Vestian Persecutions of the Jirahar (377–398 AR)

Second Qiryath had already been destroyed by Atheria in 67 AR. During the Underground Centuries, Jirahar and Redeemer communities sometimes sheltered one another and sometimes denounced one another. Under conversion-era polemic, the specific guilt of Ezrahel's council was turned into inherited accusation against an entire people.

Under Vestian, riot became policy. The yellow lamps gave his court a culprit, and the old rolls of the Litter Debt and Astrion's survey lists told officers where Jirahar households lived and what they held. Governors treated descent as evidence even against baptised families. At Raveth, about 389, baptised families of Jirahar descent had lodged their sworn accounts of the unkindled lamps in a Jirahar hearing house when the bishops' courts would not hear them. A crowd with its own grievances over rents and debts burned the house, urged on by clergy who wanted the accounts destroyed, and Vestian withdrew the order to rebuild it. Remaining eastern towers were razed or confiscated between about 388 and 396. About 396 Qiryath's last inhabitants were cleared under its Empty Charter. The chancery draft forbade rebuilding "in perpetuity"; Vestian struck the phrase out and wrote *before the Mesakha* in his own hand. Church canonists read the clause as "never". The custodians read it as a promise in their persecutor's handwriting. The roads, harbours and foreign quarters of the older diaspora absorbed another great displacement. Jirahar residents and jurists later influenced Covenant scholarship.

## Inheritance

By Vestian's death in 398 AR, the empire was officially Redeemer-faithful. Star rites survived in households, rural estates, philosophical schools, eastern military customs and the offerings people still left at fords and crossroads; veterans of the Answered Standard swore by a Star that had answered once. The Church inherited the colleges' books, the lawful power to say which signs existed, imperial legal habits, a written oath with the lamp taken out of it, and the temptation to treat successful custody as ownership of the Flame.

## Workers of the old temples

A sanctuary was an employer as well as a cult centre. Its singers, cooks, guards, prostitutes, metalworkers, cleaners and dependants remained when the altar was broken. Some converted sincerely, some continued old rites, and some sold knowledge to new patrons. [[G-11 - The Children of the Closed Temple|The Children of the Closed Temple]] remembers this dispersed population.

Old objects caused different problems. Some images were judged empty frauds; others were held to have fed Masks, and destroying such a statue was said to expose theft without ending the appetite which had used it. Relics venerated as authentic were no less dangerous to people who treated them as imperial equipment.

## Repentance without erasure

The Penitent Governor tradition remembers a ruler who ordered torture, judicial murder, forced conversion and confiscation, and whose purification policy killed his own heir, a child in some tellings. The tradition holds that his repentance was real. His laws stayed in force, the families he had injured stayed injured, and a predatory likeness of his public face went on receiving petitions after his change of heart.

## Burial, song and fracture

Persecution had left bodies in the wrong graves and Lives stitched together from several people. Legalisation let families dig for their dead and gave rival churches reasons to claim them. The Red-Passage Witness, whose martyrdom the churches keep, had earlier abandoned prisoners and helped hide killings; the Life tells the service and repentance that came after. Some congregations remembered their dead by murdering the people they believed had informed on them.

Church choirs took over the old temple melodies and dropped the names of temple custodians who had been executed. The custom of keeping a silence for five remembers one such absence. In the Profession years a converted hymn guild took over an old temple choir's house after one of its singers informed on five custodians, who were tortured and killed. The choirs that name the dead say that at the dedication of the new sanctuary a figure of black and white stood above the choir rail carrying a coal that gave no smoke, and touched the informer's mouth; he spoke all five names, confessed to the governor and went to prison. [[G-41 - The Silence Kept for Five|The Silence Kept for Five]]

Coastal fishers meanwhile told of [[G-75 - The Empty Oar of the Winter Tithe|the Winter Tithe]], a bargain with a court of the sea under which pulling one person from the water left another exposed, until someone faced the bargain itself. Later polemic made every captive in the tale either a martyr or a pagan fiction, and both versions were false.

## Towards the frontier

After the Conversion the Church built monasteries, hospitals and schools, and it learned to speak the language of arrest. About 404, under Vestian's young son and his regents, the custodians of a new reliquary began giving orders in the voice of a dead prison clerk, and a bishop's readers certified the voice. It named enemies of the converted state, and men were arrested on its word. The clerk had been a prisoner in Tervan's time who gave a dyer's name under torture, spent the rest of his life copying the names the prison courts struck from their rolls, and died sealed beneath a civic court for refusing to become the living mouth of whatever was bound there. The prison districts' chronicles claim that he came back once, in 404, to say that the voice was not his. He held the old chamber shut until its keepers were out, and spoke the dyer's name before his own. The reliquary cracked, and the arrests it had guided stopped in that province. Prison families still say that a bishop's seal sent their kin to the cells and a wonder no bishop had licensed let them out. One church still reads the dyer's name first at the clerk's feast; another reverses the order. The Council of Halmenth in 411 condemned Bishop Nasriel of Qabir, who as a young reader had kept his city's count of the unkindled lamps and carried the copies to Raveth. His party had first said of the state's lamp, *the lamp is made by hands; the Fire comes after*, and within a generation said the same of the Redeemer himself and of the reliquaries whose estates were buying freedom from the land tax. At Halmenth he read the count aloud and was charged under the augurs' old law for publishing a sign no recognised authority had entered. When the assenting bishops lit their lamps from a daughter of the Eternal Ember brought by warship from Caleran, Qabir's deacons kept the fire they had brought from home and went out of communion with it burning. Halmenth's ferrymen rowed Nasriel out to a Sea Fleet ship that night. The regency struck the gateway sees off its roll instead of pursuing him, and since their clergy could certify no relic estate, the condemned cities went on paying the land tax the rest of the empire was escaping; the regents left them alone. Nasriel's communion did not go into exile. It gathered in those cities and became the Velian Church, which still prays in the Jirahar dialect. [[Amaryah the Fire-Bearer]] · [[The Velian Church]]

The following centuries lead to [[The Long Frontier and the First Unsealing]], where faith, imperial citizenship, everyday cooperation and violence no longer follow one border.
