# The Conversion of the Empire (292–398 AR)

> _“They tried to silence the Flame. Then they tried to own it.”_
> —later summary of the Ember Years

The conversion of the Atherian Empire ran from Cassian's vision at the Stone Bridge of Velos in 292 AR to the death of Vestian the Pious in 398. In that century the Redeemer's faith went from hunted to tolerated, from tolerated to favoured, and from favoured to compulsory. Beneath the changes of ruler ran a quarrel as old as the empire: who had the right to say what a wonder meant. For three centuries the augur colleges had decided which signs existed in law. Cassian won his throne under a sign anyone could check in winter, Astrion answered it with a sign of his own, and Vestian gave the power of naming signs to the bishops, then watched thousands of lamps refuse to kindle at the ceremonies of his law. Believers held the miracles of the age to be genuine. By its end they had also become instruments of coercion.

## Prelude: The Underground Centuries (1–291 AR)

The empire that Isfir's followers entered was governed by answers. Every chartered augur college kept a book of what the Stars had said: which campaign the Red Star had blessed, which ground was cursed, which election was void. Augurs watched the sky from temple roofs and read the livers of sheep on the steps below, and their answers were read aloud in the forums on market days. A wonder the colleges had never entered was, in law, either a fraud or a cult feeding something.

The colleges' own books open the age with a gap. From the hour of the Binding at Qiryath to the third morning, no college in the empire recorded any answer at all. High Augur Severan, who had urged Isfir's execution, never read again. The colleges sealed those three days. Tyros, a Companion who had been an astrologer-priest, learned of them from former colleagues and talked about them in public, and junior augurs in several provincial colleges followed him with copies. The High Augurate answered with the commission of the **Great Purge** (c. 36–45 AR), which burned Jareth in the Celestial Forum, beheaded Tyros and Aquilan, and charged its prisoners with forging or stealing sacred records. Its edict made it a crime to publish a wonder the colleges had not entered, and congregations learned to write their miracles down as weather and good sense. [[The Twelve Companions of the Redeemer]] · [[The First Church - From Qiryath to the Tower Revolt|The First Church]]

Redeemer communities were then illegal or precariously tolerated for more than two centuries. Enforcement varied by emperor, governor, city and crisis. Quiet decades let households, workshops, burial societies and local clergy take root; purges destroyed congregations and made martyrs whose memory travelled farther than official proclamations. Caleran, Aurelium, Halmenth and Sermora became the principal regional sees by the third century.

Magistrates had their own reasons to fear the faith. In the unpaid eastern garrisons of the 240s a cult called the **Kept Lamps** promised that comrades who had died owed their wages were "kept" by lamps over their graves. The lamps leaned towards mourners and burned without oil, and the garrisons that tended them mutinied. Augurs broke the cult's central image about 247. When Emperor Tervan Marcellan issued his Edict of Public Sacrifice in 249, he demanded a witnessed offering from every household and a licence for its graves and lamps. The Redeemer burial fraternities, who also kept lamps for executed men, fell under the licence. His successor suspended the edict in 253. During the civil war of 288–291 the court holding Caleran seized meeting places and arrested church officers. Its searchers hunted above all for the congregations' books of signs, which the capital's augurs called counter-augury. [[The Church after Qiryath - From Exile to Legalization]]

The empire's succession wars, fighting with Vashar and the southern Folk, and the rising of enslaved workers in Sarqen explain how the provincial commands and ports gained leverage over Caleran. [[Atheria between Qiryath and Cassian - Emperors Wars and Revolts|Atheria between Qiryath and Cassian]]

- **[[The Ember Underground]]:** believers move people, letters and relics along the Via Celestis while informers map the same routes.
- **[[The Fireless Trial]]:** a Redeemer-faithful senator refuses both recantation and the theatrical miracle his supporters expect.
- **[[The Temple That Would Not Burn]]:** about 250, commissioners enforcing Tervan's lamp licences try three times to burn a ruined Star temple at Cyrian where believers kept a nightly fire, and three times fail.
- **[[The Forty Flames]]:** in winter 290 AR a three-day rising at the Ironvale Ashforges ends in assault and reprisals. Forty prisoners die after three nights in an unlit furnace, and witnesses see a flame on the dead when it is opened. In 291 a guard and a freed script-keeper swear before Cassian's officers that the furnace had been cold.

## The River That Will Not Freeze (292–316 AR)

In 292 AR the usurper **Cassian**, acclaimed by his frontier army four years earlier, marched on Caleran. Many of his soldiers had heard the Ironvale oath read aloud in the Aurin towns that spring. At the Stone Bridge of Velos he saw the White Flame stand upon the Aurin and heard, _“By this Flame, hold.”_ He won under its sign and legalized the Redeemer's faith. The river at the bridge has not frozen since. Every winter the Aurin above and below Velos carries ice thick enough for carts, and every winter the reach beneath the bridge runs black and open, steaming in the cold.

Anyone could check the sign, so the quarrel at once turned to who owned it. River and bridge were crown property. Imperial law still knew only registered answers, so Cassian had the augurs of Caleran enter the reach in their books as a sign answered to the emperor. That was why he kept the colleges: they made his miracle official. The four sees refused to commemorate an entry that called the Flame an "answer". The congregation at Velos, keeper of the martyr Erom's prison chains, claimed the sign for its own city.

About 296 the crown and the sees settled in the **Velos Concord**. Cassian built a chapel at the bridge, appointed a **Warden of the Reach** to measure the ice line each winter and keep its book, and paid the clergy who accepted him. In return he ordered restitution across the empire: confiscated meeting rooms, relief stores and burial grounds were to go back to the congregations that had held them. Caleran's bishop signed first and Halmenth's last. Critics in every later century have said that the bishops traded a miracle for property.

Restitution took years. Governors weighed household deeds against burial rolls and the memories of old women who had carried bread to a meeting room. Buyers who had bought confiscated halls in good faith at treasury sales could petition for their money back. Families who had let believers bury their dead in a garden kept the garden and lost the right to bar the gate. At Qiryath the older settlement stood: the garrison, the tribute and the seizure of sanctuary land remained in force.

Cassian accepted a witnessed service oath from soldiers who objected to an offering, renewed grain contracts with Star estates, restored southern market treaties, renewed Elmaric convoy contracts and secured a prisoner exchange with Vashar. In 297 he called the four sees to Halmenth to hear a quarrel over a hall whose elder had surrendered the congregation's chest, and its book of signs, during the prosecutions. Bishops judged communion and eligibility for church office. Governors enforced property rulings, and the treasury could favour one congregation through its grants. [[Cassian and the Four Sees - The Settlement of 292 to 316 AR|Cassian and the Four Sees]]

Cassian granted Caleran's former Celestial Forum precinct to the local congregation and paid for a basilica. With the precinct came the Pillar of Concordance, where judgements had once been read, and the Redeemer's nails, which the Purge commission had leaded into it. The crown had held them for some two and a half centuries before it gave them away. Keepers from Veridun Minor brought a daughter flame from Amaryah's household lamp. Public worship brought the capital's commemoration of Jareth and its other martyrs together with the household tradition from Qiryath. The sanctuary flame became the Eternal Ember. [[Caleran and the Eternal Ember]]

Because the crown's own sign stood in an augural book, Cassian's eldest son went to the High Augur of Astaroth about 309 and asked for an answer on the succession. An entry naming him heir would have been the strongest title in the empire. Asking about the emperor's life was a capital offence. The answer was given and sealed. Cassian executed his son and the High Augur; the empress, whom rumour had made the heir's ally, lived. What the sealed entry said has never been published. From then on a crown registrar sealed every answer touching the throne.

Cassian was dying in 316. His household carried him to Velos, and the crown-paid chaplain of the bridge baptised him in the unfrozen water, since no bishop of the four sees would do it there. The baptism gave rise to the **Cassian Wager**, the unresolved dispute over whether one final absolution answered a reign that included a son's execution, and to a second question that the sees still ask: can the crown's river baptise?

## Contested Coexistence (316–348 AR)

For more than half a century, Stars and Flame shared the empire unevenly. Old temples retained estates and municipal obligations. Bishops competed over confiscated property and imperial access. Mixed households kept both calendars and lit lamps for both. Local officials could protect one congregation, persecute another, and call both acts public order.

This was the generation in which a persecuted Church learned administration. Witnesses of the martyrdoms still lived, but their successors argued about courts, schools, basilicas, military chaplains, and whether imperial favour was providence or a subtler trial.

In 316 AR, Cassian's younger son **Sevrin** secured Caleran and confirmed his cousin **Adran** in the eastern command in return for recognition. Sevrin kept the restitution judgements, the witnessed service oath and the grain contracts with Star estates. He refused every petition to hand the wardenship of the reach to the bishops, and he refused to open the sealed answer that had killed his brother. His intended heir died c. 343. Adran's son Astrion gained command of the northern desert approaches and succeeded Sevrin in 348 with the backing of officers whose pay and households depended on the frontier command. [[The Reign of Sevrin]]

The augurs still held the legal power to say what a sign was, and they used it. In the 320s builders at Veridun Minor found a woman's bones beneath a ruined shrine of Astaroth, with a broken sword hilt beside them. The four sees claimed them for Calira; the Star college, still the lawful judge of such finds, entered them as the remains of a Star-priestess. [[The Apostle’s Bones]]

Along the quarry roads a worse dispute ran for two generations. A prison worker who had carried bread and the names of the dead between the quarry cells and their families had been executed in the last persecutions, and his body thrown into a lime trench. After legalization two shrines claimed him: the quarry town's crowned icon of an unbroken confessor, and a weeping image at a road sanctuary several days away. Both worked signs. Relic hunters broke bones in the lime trench, custodians killed his cousin for refusing to sell the dead man's work shirt, a man who had recanted beside him was murdered and thrown into a quarry pit, and the family's house was burned. Pilgrims of the two shrines fought on the road between them. Investigators sent by the sees later found that each image went quiet when its quarrel with the other was taken away, and wrote that neither had the man. His family still lines his empty grave with uncarved stones. [[G-70 - The Empty Grave and the Two False Shrines|The Empty Grave and the Two False Shrines]] · [[The Hunger Wars]]

## Astrion's Restoration (348–351 AR)

**Astrion the Apostate**, Cassian's great-nephew, restored temple stipends and required publicly paid teachers of the classics to honour the Star rites. Redeemer worship and Cassian's restitution judgements remained lawful. He had the augurs re-enter the reach at Velos as an answer of Hecatara, the Star of crossings. The bishops called the entry a forgery, and the crowds at the bridge went on calling the open water the Flame's. Astrion also authorised survey work at Qiryath after Jirahar petitions for access, water and relief from tribute. A wall collapse stopped the work.

In 350 his augurs staged a public test at a Caleran mustering ground, meant to give the Stars a sign as public as the river. They loaded a bronze standard and its stone socket. The Red Star answered once, by exposing a weakness in the loaded support before engineers cleared the ground. Soldiers took it as a promise of victory, engineers as a warning and Redeemer clergy as proof that a created power could answer. Astrion wanted a second test; the augurs would give no terms for it. Their signed account eventually reached the Sanctum and was sealed.

After a quarrel over requisitions, Astrion took a punitive column beyond the northern depot line. Local defenders closed secondary wells and drove off animals. He died at thirty-four on the retreat, struck by a spear no witness saw thrown. The field record gives him no last words, only a question: had the baggage with the register crossed? Preachers read that as an apostate clinging to a Star answer, and the eastern soldiers as loyalty to the men who had stood under the standard. Church tradition later gave him the words _“You have won, Carpenter,”_ which the Church remembers as surrender and Sestaran humanists as tragedy. [[Astrion and the Road to Vestian - 348 to 377 AR|Astrion and the Road to Vestian]]

## The Water-Bringer and the Two Standards (351–377 AR)

The column was dying of thirst when **Tellan**, Astrion's younger brother, came up the eastern depot road with water bought from the Well-Tarkhan carriers who had kept their contracts. The skins came on camels under the bronze standard of the 350 test. The eastern army has called it the **Answered Standard** ever since, and called Tellan the Water-Bringer. **Savel Orren**, the guard commander acclaimed in the field, restored church stipends, reopened public teaching to Redeemer teachers and abandoned two exposed road forts on the way out. When Caleran declared for Corlan Aster in 352, Savel gave the crown back at the depot line and lived out his life on an estate.

**Corlan Aster** held Caleran and the west; his brother **Lucar** held the southern and northern commands under the same imperial title. The east stayed with Adran's house. Tellan had a paid command, a sign he could show and soldiers whose fathers he had saved. He never claimed the throne: in all the years that followed, the Red Star never answered the east a second time, and its augurs said so honestly. About 357 the brothers and Tellan settled in the **Accord of the Two Standards**. The east kept its revenue share on Sevrin's old terms, its augurs and its standard. Eastern units swore a Star oath beneath it, and western units swore Cassian's service oath.

Corlan died about 366 while inspecting the southern defences, and Lucar ruled alone. He took the wardenship at Velos back into crown hands and sold the chapel's revenue to bishops who accepted his terms for army chaplains. The court increasingly decided which bishops could draw public revenue. Star temples still held estates in some districts.

The winters of 369–371 were the hardest in living memory, and Grobi war-bands came down the frozen rivers behind them. Folk households fled to the southern fort line, and Lucar admitted them in 371–372. His officers diverted the grain meant for them and sold dependants into slavery. His chaplains confiscated the small iron, bone and hair tokens that every Folk household carried to pay the river-courts at fords, calling them idols, and the officers sold the silver ones. Armed resistance spread from 372. In 377 Lucar marched south before his reserves and wagons were ready. His army came to the ford below Kellovar, the hill where Casvilar had starved the Folk four centuries earlier, and crossed without paying. The river took the crossing. Folk fighters who still carried their tokens crossed dry, and Lucar's army broke in the water and he died with it. **Vestian** kept the inner depots open, paid the surviving units and secured recognition from Caleran. He read the ford as a judgement on an empire that served two kinds of sacred authority. [[Astrion and the Road to Vestian - 348 to 377 AR|Astrion and the Road to Vestian]]

## Vestian and Compulsory Faith (377–398 AR)

**Emperor Vestian the Pious** came to power convinced that signs were real and must be held by one authority. As the price of Caleran's recognition and the sees' help in feeding the towns, he gave the bishops the chapel at Velos and its winter book. River and bridge stayed crown property. Folk communities that negotiated settlement under their own elders were settled by 379, and investigators began tracing the dependants Lucar's officers had sold.

In 381 Vestian dissolved the augur colleges, beginning at Caleran. The last High Augur, **Ilios Marrant**, asked leave for one final petition to the Red Star on behalf of the college that had made it answer in 350. Vestian granted it before a crowd in the Celestial Forum, in front of the Pillar that holds the Nails. The augurs set no load and named no price. They asked the Star to show that the college still deserved its office. The crowd waited through the appointed time, nothing answered, and people went home saying the Stars had been beaten. An augur who later converted wrote down Marrant's own reading of the silence: the Red Star had never answered a petition without a field, and would not begin for a college with nothing left to test. Then the college seal was broken, and its sealed books, the **Deep Register**, went to the bishop's readers. By imperial statute the right to say which signs existed in law passed to the bishops of recognised communion. The provincial colleges followed by 384.

The eastern command refused to give up its augurs. In the **War of the Two Standards** (381–384) Vestian cut the east off from port grain and called on southern contingents under the new Folk agreements. At the decisive crossing the eastern augurs asked the Red Star for terms. None came. The Answered Standard went into the field unanswered, and the eastern Redeemer units negotiated their own surrender. Tellan ended his life a pensioned captive in a house above the Aurin, visited by his old soldiers on the anniversary of the water. Caleran's inventory says the standard was melted for a church bell; eastern veterans' families said for generations that it lay buried under a depot road. The augurs' account of 350 went to Aurelium. Vestian had won his war because the Stars would not answer his enemy.

In 385 he made the east's peace oath imperial law. Under **the Profession**, every officeholder and then every household head in the directly governed provinces professed the faith before recognised clergy and lit a reception lamp from a church flame. Congregations had kept such lamps since the first century. Now and then a newly baptised believer's lamp took a white, unconsuming fire before the assembly, rarely enough that Underground letters said only that "the lamp took well". The chancery announced that the ceremonies would show the Flame confirming the realm. Vestian expected the confirmation as a sign that the Redeemer's return was near.

The lamps burned yellow. In town after town, in squares hung with imperial banners, thousands of reception lamps burned like any oil, while in the parish churches of the same towns willing baptisms went on kindling at the old uneven rate. The court circulated a handful of white flames at state ceremonies; the confessors noted that most had fallen to people who had sought baptism before the decree. The bishops divided. Aurelium's readers compiled a register of the unkindled professions district by district and sealed it. Caleran ruled that a properly witnessed profession had civil force whether or not anything kindled. Halmenth refused to use the lamp at any profession made under compulsion. In Sermora and the gateway ports, congregations of Jirahar descent said aloud that they had heard this silence before, at Qiryath, and called it the **Second Silence**. The court called that slander.

Vestian's court blamed pollution: Star images hidden in cupboards, offerings to household spirits, and what its preachers called "Jirahar blindness" in the descendants of the old council. About 387 it banned household rites altogether and quickened its orders against the eastern towers. About 389 the state professions changed to a sealed written oath. The lamp went out of the ceremony, and with it the public record of the silence. [[Vestian Persecutions and the Long Diaspora]]

The closing of the temples did damage of its own. A temple was an employer, and sometimes the keeper of a bargain older than the empire. Some sanctuaries held Masks that had fed on their worshippers, some held only frightened priests, and some kept compacts with the powers of a river or a spring that were still in force. On the Weiss, a temple whose priests had paid a river's court each spring was closed in the 380s, and the next flood took the lower town. The church built on its foundations still has a crypt where something is paid every spring, and its clergy will not say what. The old-cult families called these years the **War of the Closed Doors**.

In the emptied sanctuaries a counter-church grew. The **Preacher of the Second Gospel**, a former temple copyist whose bishop had removed him from teaching, gathered displaced temple workers and abandoned fever households and offered them real minor wonders as proof that what works is true. He presided over captive sacrifice, ordered a child used in a possession trial and had a former companion murdered for the record of his own discipline. Once the colleges had gone, his were the only wonders in the empire that no bishop had licensed, and they drew converts away from the lawful Church. [[G-25 - The Ragged Gospel in the Hospital Apron|The Ragged Gospel in the Hospital Apron]] · [[Saints and Sinners of the Chronicles]]

Vestian's reign welded sanctity to state power. Sincere bishops gained the means to end old persecutions and also the means to persecute. Court converts used heresy and temple law to settle property claims, destroy rivals and rewrite family histories. The old augur aristocracy preserved parts of its wealth by selling archives, offices and legitimacy to the new order. Vestian died in 398.

## The Vestian Persecutions of the Jirahar (377–398 AR)

The Second Qiryath had already been destroyed by Atheria in 70 AR. During the Underground Centuries, Jirahar and Redeemer communities sometimes sheltered one another and sometimes denounced one another. Under conversion-era polemic, the specific guilt of Ezrahel's council was turned into inherited accusation against an entire people.

Under Vestian, riot became policy. The yellow lamps gave his court a culprit, and the old tribute rolls and Astrion's survey petitions told officers where Jirahar households lived and what they held. Governors treated descent as evidence even against baptised families. At Raveth, about 389, baptised families of Jirahar descent had lodged their sworn accounts of the unkindled lamps in a Jirahar hearing house when the bishops' courts would not hear them. A crowd with its own grievances over rents and debts burned the house, urged on by clergy who wanted the accounts destroyed, and Vestian withdrew the order to rebuild it. Remaining eastern towers were razed or confiscated between about 388 and 396. About 396 Qiryath's last inhabitants were cleared under its Empty Charter. The chancery draft forbade rebuilding "in perpetuity"; Vestian struck the phrase out and wrote *before the Mesakha* in his own hand. Church canonists read the clause as "never". The custodians read it as a promise in their persecutor's handwriting. The roads, harbours and foreign quarters of the older diaspora absorbed another great displacement. Jirahar residents and jurists later influenced Covenant scholarship.

## Inheritance

By Vestian's death in 398 AR, the empire was officially Redeemer-faithful. Star rites survived in households, rural estates, philosophical schools, eastern military customs and Menhir encounters; veterans of the Answered Standard swore by a Star that had answered once. The Church inherited the colleges' books, the lawful power to say which signs existed, imperial legal habits, a written oath with the lamp taken out of it, and the temptation to treat successful custody as ownership of the Flame.

## Workers of the old temples

A sanctuary was an employer as well as a cult center. Its singers, cooks, guards, prostitutes, metalworkers, cleaners and dependants remained when the altar was broken. Some converted sincerely, some continued old rites, and some sold knowledge to new patrons. [[G-11 - The Children of the Closed Temple|The Children of the Closed Temple]] remembers this dispersed population.

Old objects caused different problems. Some images were judged empty frauds; others were held to have fed Masks, and destroying such a statue was said to expose theft without ending the appetite which had used it. Relics venerated as authentic were no less dangerous to people who treated them as imperial equipment.

## Repentance without erasure

The Penitent Governor tradition concerns a ruler who authorized torture, judicial murder, forced conversion, confiscation and a purification policy which killed his own child or heir. The tradition holds his repentance real; laws, injured families and a predatory likeness of his public identity remained afterward.

The [[GS-02 - Preacher of the Second Gospel|Preacher of the Second Gospel]] began with charitable work, was removed from teaching and then chose hostile revelation. He personally authorized captive sacrifice, a child possession trial and the murder of a former companion.

## Burial, song and fracture

Persecution had left misidentified bodies and composite Lives. Legalization made their recovery possible and gave rival institutions reasons to claim them. The Red-Passage Witness's own history included abandoned prisoners and concealment of killings before later service, martyrdom and repentance. Commemoration sometimes repeated the old guilt by murdering alleged informers.

Singers inherited temple melodies while suppressing the names of executed custodians. The custom of keeping a silence for five remembers an absence inside a successful choir. In the Profession years a converted hymn guild took over an old temple choir's house after one of its singers informed on five custodians, who were tortured and killed. The choirs that name the dead say that at the dedication of the new sanctuary a figure of black and white stood above the choir rail carrying a coal that gave no smoke, and touched the informer's mouth; he spoke all five names, confessed to the governor and went to prison. [[G-41 - The Silence Kept for Five|The Silence Kept for Five]]

Coastal fishers meanwhile told of [[G-75 - The Empty Oar of the Winter Tithe|the Winter Tithe]], a court bargain in which rescuing one person left another vulnerable until the obligation itself was confronted. Later polemic falsely made every captive a martyr or a pagan fiction.

## Toward the frontier

After Conversion, new monasteries, hospitals, schools and household practices mattered. So did the transfer of coercive power into the language of the Church. About 404, under Vestian's young son and his regents, the custodians of a new reliquary began giving orders in the voice of a dead prison clerk, and a bishop's readers certified the voice. It named enemies of the converted state, and men were arrested on its word. The clerk had been a prisoner in Tervan's time who gave a dyer's name under torture, spent the rest of his life copying the names the prison courts struck from their rolls, and died sealed beneath a civic court for refusing to become the living mouth of whatever was bound there. The prison districts' chronicles claim that he came back once, in 404, to say that the voice was not his. He held the old chamber shut until its keepers were out, and spoke the dyer's name before his own. The reliquary cracked, and the arrests it had guided stopped in that province. Prison families still say that a bishop's seal sent their kin to the cells and a wonder no bishop had licensed let them out. One church still reads the dyer's name first at the clerk's feast; another reverses the order. [[Saints and Sinners of the Chronicles]] · [[G-39 - First the Betrayed, Then the Witness|First the Betrayed, Then the Witness]]

The Council of Halmenth in 411 condemned Bishop Nasriel of Qabir, who as a young reader had kept his city's count of the unkindled lamps and carried the copies to Raveth. His party had first said of the state's lamp, *the lamp is made by hands; the Fire comes after*, and within a generation said the same of the Redeemer himself. The exile of Nasriel's communion created a further divide, forming part of the later Velian world. [[Amaryah the Fire-Bearer]] · [[The Velian Church]]

The following centuries lead to [[The Long Frontier and the First Unsealing]], where faith, imperial citizenship, everyday cooperation and violence no longer follow one border.
