# The Long Frontier and the First Unsealing

## Atlas narrative

The following account distinguishes established history from the received stories named within it.

## Decadence before the new frontier

Between c. 400 and 540 AR, weak imperial governments faced sea-raids, Folk incursions, regional ambition and the loosening of Sestaran dependence. Provincial people repaired bridges and irrigation while the center struggled to command the work. Some districts prospered under local protection; others paid soldiers and raiders in the same season.

The Council of Halmenth in 411 condemned Bishop Nasriel's party. Refuge northward helped form the Velian communion and, later, a distinct border people. This was not merely a theological label pasted onto unchanged Thalmyrics. Congregations acquired their own patterns of schooling, married clergy, craft and household loyalty.

## The First Unsealing

Mujalib's revelation in 541 AR and tribal unification in 545–567 transformed older Ahdari oath and water societies. Al-Qahur's foundation, bound labour and the Covenant's military machinery joined religious conviction to state formation. Mujalib remained sincere; authorial knowledge of the Creditor's hidden part does not make him knowingly fraudulent.

Conquest under Qatada Sayf-al-Ahd, 568–612, took northern provinces. The seizure of the Nine Wells in 558 had already wounded Tarkhan communities and cannot be rewritten as voluntary submission. Zanthir's emirate dates to 601; the Velian States came under Banu Mizan protection around 610. The First Crusade held the advance at ruinous cost.

## Three centuries of ordinary coexistence

The Long Frontier proper runs from 612 to 884 AR. Peace meant the absence of general imperial conquest, not the absence of raiding, ransom, slavery or persecution. Border households negotiated irrigation, pasture and marriage while soldiers prepared for renewed war. Translators and physicians crossed boundaries that preachers denounced. A mixed town might use several laws without agreeing that all religions were true.

Banu Mizan patronage sustained libraries, translation houses and water administration. Rab Yequtiel of Zanthir, c. 748–831, wrote the *Guide for the Wandering*. Workshops produced medicines, engines, cloth and copies of books. The same routes transported slaves and supplied garrisons. Material accomplishment and moral judgement remain separate.

## People rather than one fellowship

The eight principal frontier exemplars in [[Great Saints and Sinners - Lives and Afterlives]] began independently. The older generation included a mason who turned ignored work into tyranny, a rebel who reproduced the captivity he hated, an administrator who had to end his own emergency power and a healer who made dependence permanent. The younger generation included a secretive hunter, two very different smiths and a reader whose attempt to master a closure endangered other people.

Their contacts were limited and historical. They did not form a council, sacred roll or army which later split in one Heresy. Some encountered one another only after death under a particular heavenly commission or infernal project. Other physicians, translators, caravan guards and mixed families lived important lives without entering their rivalries.

## Particular towns and disasters

A Zanthiri-facing Patron port traded neutrality for coerced witness and replacement captivity. A smaller furnace refuge admitted frontier refugees and turned shelter into hereditary maintenance debt; revolt destroyed much of it rather than founding a lasting metropolis. A translation company produced an elegant edition whose omitted warnings injured its users. The Physician and Interpreter cycle follows a friendship damaged when each mistaken certainty worsened a patient's injury.

These stories belong to different communities. They do not prove a single invisible mechanism underneath every frontier institution. [[Patron Cities and the Long Rent Histories]] and [[Constructed Shell Settlements]] explain their respective settings.

## The peace ends

The failed Second Crusade of 874–878 exposed military weakness and hardened rival claims. Qahir ibn Ruzbeh's resurgence, c. 885–905, rebuilt levies and siege-golem foundries. It belongs to the transition out of the long peace, culminating in [[The Fall and the Long Reconquest]]. The frontier's achievements survived unevenly through that catastrophe; neither golden-age nostalgia nor a story of uninterrupted oppression is sufficient.
