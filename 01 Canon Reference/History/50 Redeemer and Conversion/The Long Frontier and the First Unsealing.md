# The Long Frontier and the First Unsealing

The Long Frontier is the age between the death of Emperor Vestian in 398 AR and the fall of Caleran in 905, when the Atherian Empire lost its northern provinces to the new Covenant and then lived for nearly three centuries beside its conqueror along a line of fortified towns, monasteries, ferries and watch-fires. Four houses of emperors ruled from Caleran in these centuries. Under them the palace's hymns stopped kindling while the frontier hermits' still did; a war over a toll road and a cattle plague broke the old well-country of the north and fed the rise of Mujalib's Covenant; and Covenant armies took half the northern towns by reading them their own charters before they stormed the rest.

The peace of 612 was made by two frightened armies, each of which had just watched its own wonder fail. It held until an emperor broke it in 874 and handed the Covenant the argument it needed. Thalmyria, Eclessia, Zanthir, the Velian States and the Qahirid Covenant all trace living quarrels to this age.

## Four houses after Vestian

Vestian left the throne in 398 to a son still in the schoolroom, and the court bishops and paymasters who had made the father ruled through the boy. They needed money and had little to sell except holiness. From the first years of the regency a monastery or a bishop's estate that kept a relic could buy freedom from the land tax and from the auditors' visits. Provincial landowners deeded their fields to relic estates and leased them back, and the empire's wealth went into the sanctuaries to hide. It was this regency that summoned the bishops to Halmenth in 411 and condemned Bishop Nasriel of Qabir. It then struck his gateway sees off the roll instead of pursuing him. No recognised cleric would certify their relics, so the condemned cities went on paying the land tax the rest of the empire was escaping, and the regents left them alone.

Vestian's son reigned for forty-nine years. In the summer of 446 a harbour barge carrying his only son and heir overturned in a squall at the mouth of the Aurin, in sight of the quay at Caleran, and the young man drowned with his oarsmen. The old emperor died the next spring. From 447 to 452 the army of the southern forts fought the Aurin fleet and the port councils for the throne. Its commander won because the great sees and monasteries acclaimed him, and he paid them in more immunities. He was the first of the **Saldane** house, and in his war the court first hired cohorts of Sestaran warlocks to throw Shell-fire for it.

The Saldanes ruled from the southern fort-line for 135 years, through Ättar sea-raids on the Kedravan coast, Folk pressure on the southern forts and the slow drift of the Sestaran cities, which dug out charters older than the empire (Vardessa's is the famous case) and sold ships and warlock contracts to Caleran in place of tax. Aurelium's historians call the years from 398 to 514 the Decadence. **Teomar Saldane** (r. 509–531), a fort-line soldier with a quartermaster's eye, ended them by going to war for a road. His successors paid for that war through the worst famine of the age, and in the north the Saldane emperor of the famine years is still called the Granary Thief. When the northern provinces fell to the Covenant, the remnant of the field army and the frontier bishops deposed the house in 587.

**Leovar I** (r. 587–624) was a frontier general who travelled with a hermit-bishop in his baggage and knelt in the mud at the gate of every town he re-sealed. He held what was left of the empire, signed the peace of 612 and built a smaller state around it. His house, the **Leovarids**, ruled until the summer of 709, when a fever took **Leovar II** (r. 681–709) and his heir nine days apart. Caleran remembers it as the Fever Week.

A Leovarid cousin held the capital, the fleet and the court clergy. **Corvenas**, who commanded the march facing the Velian belt and had married Leovar II's daughter, held the frontier. Every march oath had to be renewed in person within a year and a day of a new reign, and the cousin could not leave Caleran without losing it. Corvenas spent 710 in the saddle. At march after march he knelt while a frontier monk read the old oath over him, and the lamps took the Fire. The marches chose him, and after four years of the War of the Two Oaths he entered Caleran as **Corvenas I** (r. 713–746). His **Corvenid** descendants ruled until the Fall.

Wary of the marches that had made them, the later Corvenids built a counterweight in the capital: a chancery of sworn jurists called the Sealing Office, a household guard of Ättar who swore to the emperor's person, and gold contracts with Sestaran and Vortharan warlock guilds. **Corvenas II** (r. 861–884) launched the Second Crusade and was forced to abdicate. **Corvenas III** (r. 884–905) inherited the result; his last years are told in [[Prelude to Collapse]].

## The Guttering

The reception lamps of Vestian's Profession had already burned yellow in the town squares in 385. After his death the palace's own rites followed them, in plain sight. The accession relics that had wept oil at Vestian's crowning stayed dry at his son's. The palace choir sang the battle hymns over the army that marched out in 447, and the lamps on the altar burned yellow. Year after year the great feasts in the cathedral of the Celestial Forum passed without a kindling. In the passes and the frontier hospitals the old signs went on: a hermit's lamp took the white fire over a dying shepherd, a keeper of the sick kindled at a deathbed, and the lay brothers at the remount studs sang over their foals as they always had. Those foals' descendants are the horses the Tarkhan now price above provinces.

The court met the failure in three ways, one after another. First it hid it: the palace calendar entered failed rites as *postponed*, and in some years the postponements outnumbered the feasts. Then it hired: the Shell-fire cohorts of 447 became a permanent arm, licensed warlock fire became the imperial army's standard incendiary, and Ättar crews and Folk auxiliaries filled ranks the church-backed muster no longer filled. Then it faked. At the accession of Leovar II in 681 the lamp in the emperor's hands flared white before the whole cathedral, and Caleran rang its bells for a day. Two years later a Sestaran warlock, unpaid for eighteen months, sold a brass fire-cup and a written account of the night to a Covenant factor at Sermora. The Patriarch of Caleran called the cup a forgery and the account a slander. The factor's masters kept both. The scandal of **the Copper Lamp** began a file that Covenant jurists added to for two hundred years.

The name *the Guttering* comes from a Thalmyric refuge chronicler of the 930s: *in the palace the lamps guttered, and in the passes they burned.* Eclessian historians call the same years **the Unexamined Centuries**. In their account the yields of the Fire never fell; only the number of wonders anyone checked did, and they point to a palace register of kindlings for 794–812 in which a later hand has turned *postponed* into *answered* thirty-one times. Covenant jurists say the Fire will not witness a breached title. The Velian Church says it warned of exactly this at Halmenth. Sestaran humanists call the whole affair a treasury's story told in incense.

## The Toll War, the Thin Floods and the end of Vashar, 514–560

In 500 the highland Crown of Roads and Dawns still held the Ubar riverlands and the roads to the grain of the Sacred Basin, and its road commanders had become hereditary lords of their stations. Vashar took its toll on every bale that crossed the Ubar. Teomar Saldane wanted it. The **Ubar Toll War** of 514–523 was fought for relay stations, bridges and wells. Atherian columns took the western stations of the Avesh corridor and then found that the treasury could not pay to garrison them; Vashar's western commanders, beaten and unpaid, stopped answering their king. Both crowns borrowed against future tolls, and both stopped the stipends that had kept the Ahdari well-houses of the dry country as their clients for a thousand years. The well-country fell into feud. Wells were fouled, guest-law broke down, and caravans paid twice and arrived with half their camels. Mujalib, born about 510, grew up in it. His revelation came in 541.

Five years later the rain failed. In the **Thin Floods** of 546–552 the Basin's flood failed in five summers out of seven and the highland snowmelt came thin. From 548 an ox murrain the north called **the yoke-sickness** spread from the Basin through the Vasharan valleys and into the Atherian north. Plough oxen coughed, lay down in the furrows and did not rise; road mules died in their traces at the relay stations. Camels were largely spared. The settled world lost its traction while the desert clans kept theirs.

The Atherian north lost its harvests too. The Saldane treasury, still paying the war's debts and the warlocks' contracts, kept Caleran's bread dole by sending requisition columns north for the stores. The north remembers that the capital ate its seed grain. Along the grain road the dead of the famine travelled in the same carts as the bread. Graves were found opened, and villages along the road began keeping grave-watches that some of them keep still.

In the Basin, through the worst of the famine, the 4,444 bound devils of the Covenant dug. They cut the channels and the oasis at Al-Qahur day and night while children starved in the canal towns below. The grain the new channels watered went to those who sealed their names into the Covenant, written down one by one. The canal councils of the Basin entered for bread as much as for belief. A Covenant jurist of the next century wrote that the Vault of Names began as the famine ration roll, where to be named was to be fed; Witnessed-Seal teachers call that a slander on the Prophet.

Vashar ended in the same years. Its inscriptions had repeated one promise in three languages for twelve centuries: the king keeps the roads. By 554 couriers on the Dawn Road found station after station with cold hearths, empty stalls and doors banging in the wind; the horses had been eaten and the keepers had walked home. Covenant preachers rode the empty road reading the old saying of Mazhara of Daryam back to the road commanders: a king's lie remains a lie when carved in three languages. In 555 Covenant jurists declared the last King at Dawn forsworn. Road commanders and subject rulers, many unpaid since the Toll War, sealed with the Covenant on terms that kept their estates and their horses. Among them were the horse-lords of **Ruzhan**, Vashar's high pasture country, who entered Covenant service as road and cavalry officers; Qahir ibn Ruzbeh would descend from one of their houses. A few highland valleys fought and were reduced by ordinary armies by 560. The last King at Dawn was pensioned at the oasis and died there.

The Tarkhan of the [[Nine Wells of Tergai]] held their water by labour, burial and use, with nothing sealed that a jurist could void. In 558, the worst year of a drought that held the dry country long after the Basin's floods came back, Covenant forces took the wells by force and killed those who held them. The later tradition of a First Submission covers a conquest that had none.

## The First Unsealing, 568–612

In 568 Qatada Sayf-al-Ahd led the Covenant into the empire's north with an army that marched behind its lawyers. Each column carried a breach-court: Covenant jurists with their scribes, seal-boxes and an escort of horsemen.

Many northern towns had sworn their founding charters under the Stars, before augurs, and for centuries had kept a charter-day when the old oath was read at the gate under the town's protector-star. Vestian had closed the temples and dissolved the colleges; the readings stopped, and nobody in the empire thought the charters weaker for it. The Covenant's jurists did. A breach-court before such a town asked to see its charter and the witness who had sealed it. The witness was a college that no longer existed and a star nobody had asked anything of in two centuries. The exiled augur families had sold their archives piece by piece, and a jurist could buy a dead college's list of charters in a Sestaran market for the price of a horse.

When a court ruled a charter void, the town felt it like a fever breaking. Garrison oaths, guild privileges and debts all hung from the charter. Soldiers who had sworn on it found themselves released, and several garrisons walked home with their pay chests; creditors besieged the counting-houses; some gates were opened from inside. The Covenant offered terms: tribute, the surrender of arms, limits on testimony in court, and protection for worship. Half the north went this way. Towns that refused were stormed by Qatada's horsemen and levies. *The Unsealing was served, not fought* is the Covenant's proudest sentence and the conquered's bitterest. Many northern magnates took terms willingly. The Covenant confirmed their estates for tribute, and they had not forgotten the requisition columns.

In the spring of 575 the commander of the northern field army rode out from his walls to destroy the escort of a breach-court at the **Dry Ford**, believing that a court which could not protect its own escort could not lawfully sit. Qatada was waiting, and by evening the northern field army was gone.

**Leovar I** answered with seals. From 587 he sent hermit-bishops from the frontier monasteries to every town still holding, to read its charter aloud again before a living witness. Where the Fire came, a white flame standing on the wax before the whole town, the charter was re-sealed in the Fire, and no breach-court ever voided one of those. Where a court cleric officiated and nothing came, the seal was only wax. Where a governor hid a warlock behind the screen, the fraud came out sooner or later, and the town lost its terms. Honest bishops read the charters through and stopped at the lies. Bought immunities and forged boundaries were left out of the new seals, and more than one magnate who lost his exemptions preferred Covenant terms.

In the **First Crusade** of 594–598 Leovar's armies held the western field line at ruinous cost while bishops re-sealed the towns behind it. The bearers who kindled at sealing after sealing broke under it, and some went blind; the rule later Thalmyric armies keep, that bearers be rotated, begins with them.

In 597 the Covenant split over the [[The Disputed Fourth Seal|Fourth Seal]]. From then on every breach-court sat under an instrument half the Covenant called forged, and defendants learned to say so at their gates: *your court sits under a seal nobody may open.* Sealed-Blood jurists refused to sit in Uthar's name. In 598 Uthar, whose seal could be questioned, dismissed Qatada, whose authority was victory. Qatada obeyed without a word, and the field advance stopped. In 603 Mujalib's grandson Husan, travelling to arbitration under safe conduct, died with forty of his household at the station well of Bir Qassim, where the bound keeper would not draw for them once a flood had pushed them off the named road; the caliph's escort killed those the thirst had left.

On the Velian road the conquest went on where the crusade could not reach. Bishops ordained in Halmenth's communion would not witness charters for congregations Halmenth had condemned, and Nasriel's clergy held that the Fire crowns a vessel and does not seal an empire's deeds. The Velian cities were taken under Uthar's seal. Zanthir became an emirate in 601. About 610 the Velian cities accepted the protection of the Banu Mizan, the House of the Scales, without surrendering their charters, which went into fireproof chests. They are there still.

**Tamsar, 611.** In the autumn of 611 a Covenant army and its breach-court came before Tamsar, a march town on the Velian road whose charter a hermit-bishop had read before its people four years earlier while the Fire stood on the wax. The court read its ruling at the gate. The gates stayed shut, the garrison stayed on the walls, and the jurists' clerks were seen comparing papers. Two days later Leovar's relief army arrived with singers of the palace chapel and a reliquary from Caleran. The choir sang the battle hymn before both armies, and the reliquary's lamps burned yellow. That evening a Sealed-Blood jurist climbed onto a wagon in the Covenant camp and told the soldiers that their court sat under a seal nobody dared open. Neither commander gave battle. Each had watched his enemy's wonder fail and his own, and neither cared to learn which would fail next.

Both sides had reasons beyond Tamsar to stop. The Covenant's garrisons in the conquered north had to be fed from country the war had stripped bare, the Sealed-Blood families who mourned Bir Qassim were gathering across the western strait at Qazmarid, and the Banu Mizan wanted their new cities quiet. Leovar's bearers were going blind and his treasury could not pay the next campaign. In 612 the Banu Mizan brokered a peace between Leovar I and Uthar's successor. Along the line neither side would send its jurists to the other's gates, storm its towns or re-seal its charters, and every disputed charter was held *neither void nor in force*. Atherians called it the Long Frontier Peace. Covenant law called it the Suspension.

## The frontier as a country, 612–872

The line ran for hundreds of miles through dry hills, river country and the desert's edge, and it was seldom quiet. About a dozen **kindled marches** faced the Velian belt, the desert and the western strait. Each was held under a single sworn charter that had to be renewed at every new reign, in person, by the emperor kneeling before a frontier monk on whom the Fire came. Land in the marches was held for service and paid no tax. The frontier monasteries kept the standing witnesses, the hospitals and the remount studs, and a march commander's first duty on the morning after a raid was to find out whether the monks had survived it. Thalmyria's military provinces descend from these marches.

On both sides of the line the watch-towers lit signal fires for the same raiders. Ransom brokers worked both banks of the border rivers, and a coastal trader might sell prisoners one season and ransom his own cousin the next. Ferrymen kept two scales of tolls and two calendars of saints' days. Funeral parties carried the dead across the line to old family burial grounds under safe conduct when no living traveller could cross without papers; frontier people called these routes the corpse roads and kept grave-watches along them, for reasons the songs disagree about. Mixed Velian, Ahdari, Jirahar and provincial households married across lines the preachers sharpened, and a child might take a language from one parent, a craft from the other and obligations to both.

The Banu Mizan took the Velian belt's tribute while Caleran could not lawfully reclaim it, and they spent it. Their translation houses at Qabir and in the belt towns were working households where apprentices ground ink and cooked for the copyists, and a captive who knew a rare language might be worth more than a paid scholar. Their water-courts sat at the canal heads at dawn and measured each village's share by the hour-cup. Their libraries kept Atherian learning in the conqueror's custody. Banu Mizan law required a witness at every bound person's deathbed before the custodian could enter the release, and in the bond-hospitals and salt pans of Zanthir's dry margin the witnesses were often Jirahar physicians, whom Covenant courts and Velian families both accepted because they belonged to neither. **Rab Yequtiel of Zanthir** (c. 748–831) sat at such deathbeds for fifty years and kept a record of each: what the dying asked for, whom they cursed, whether they feared that the register would hold them after death. His book, *What the Register Keeps*, argued from those beds that a register keeps a name and never a person, and that the bound died as the free died. Custodians quoted it to quiet the dying; Keepers of the Path denounced it as a licence for bondage, since a custody that cannot touch the soul is easier to defend; Velian copyists carried it into the belt's churches. Jirahar physicians and Velian copyists worked across the line, and Sestaran notaries sold opinions to both sides. When Atheria tore itself apart in 709–713, Covenant commanders watched from the line and did nothing; their jurists warned that crossing it would open the peace of 612 and every question buried in it.

Inside the empire the money thinned. The palace registers carried every northern province lost between 568 and 612 as though it still paid tax, because striking one off would concede the Covenant's ruling in writing, and the chancery went on issuing orders to the governors of provinces that had been Covenant country for two hundred years. Gold stayed heavy, because warlocks and foreign captains would take nothing else. Soldiers' silver was debased in the eighth century, and a soldier would rather hold a march tenure than draw pay.

The east drifted. The immunity sales had made the great eastern monasteries rich, and in the Thin Floods its bishops handed out grain where governors had none. In the 640s the treasury ruled that a relic estate kept its exemption only while its relic answered a test; at Caleran the tests were bought. In 653 a Thalassian squall sank the fleet carrying the eastern tax grain to Caleran, and the court, unable to replace it, let the eastern bishops collect and spend the revenue at home "for one year". Nobody ever ended the year. From then on the eastern bishops kept the revenue whichever way a test fell, so they tested honestly, and Aurelium, a see of readers and copyists since the Underground, kept the results in a **register of answered fire**: which relics answered, which never had, which clergy kindled. Bishops began to promote from it.

In 711, during the War of the Two Oaths, the claimant in Caleran sent the **Velos Standard**, the battle standard the court held Cassian had carried at the Stone Bridge, east to rally the provinces. **Simeon**, bishop of Aurelium, had it set up in his cathedral and examined before the people for three days. The lamps beside it burned yellow. He recorded it silent, refused the summons and held the eastern governors' oaths "in trust for the emperor who proves lawful". In 713 Corvenas I confirmed Aurelium's right to examine relics presented in the east for imperial purposes, because the precedent had served him. Eclessians call it **Simeon's primacy**, and in 1015 Bonifac III would demand that Veridun acknowledge it. Caleran never accepted the verdict; its clergy said Simeon had lied for a rebel.

## Saints and sinners of the frontier

The frontier produced the age's most famous lives, and nearly all of them are remembered as saint and sinner at once by somebody. Their chroniclers name them by epithets, and [[Saints and Sinners of the Chronicles]] gathers the lives as they are told.

**The Clerk of a Hundred Wells** (c. 641–708) was a junior clerk who slept in the archive because his wages would not pay for a room. In a drought and a siege he broke the seals on rich households' storehouses and wells and fed whole districts under a common water register. When the rain came back he kept the seizures going, signed warrants for armed men against villagers guarding their harvest and sent a petitioner to execution under a procedure he had written himself. The well women who had blessed his wagons ended it. They put out his counting candle and carried every chair in his office into the street, and the guard captain whose mother stood among them refused to arrest them. The Clerk spent his last years restoring hearings and writing expiry clauses. Courts still ask whether an emergency order contains "the Clerk's ending", a date after which it must justify itself again. Banu Mizan jurists claimed him as a founder of their water law; the descendants of the people he seized never forgave him.

**The Mason of the Ninth Bastion** (c. 646–716) began as a quarry measurer who kept tunnels from collapsing and watched the honours for the work go to noble patrons. The Mason recorded every death and every slight, and came to believe that only those willing to spend lives understood building. When sabotage delayed a course of wall, one worker in ten from every surviving crew was killed by lot, including the workers who had exposed the saboteurs. On the ninth bastion of a great frontier fortress the Mason cut the evacuation short to keep a perfect foundation, and a workers' quarter collapsed with prisoners sealed inside. Three ordinary fortresses still contain sealed ninth chambers that engineers refuse to open. An engineering school founded on the Mason's measurements still keeps a "necessary loss" appendix it does not teach.

**The Chain-Bearer of Sarrat** (c. 670–727) was an attendant in a slave infirmary who could tell another captive's pain before it was spoken. His owners fitted him with a chain engine that turned the suffering around him into strength and uncontrollable pain. He helped lead a revolt, and officials who wanted the engine for frontier war took him from the last stand; his companions died believing he had abandoned them. Free at last, he killed surrendering overseers, fitted new soldiers with copies of the engine and massacred a neutral household accused of hiding former owners. Abolitionists still wear broken chain links with an open gap. States still cite the massacre against every revolt.

**The Bitter Reaper** (c. 676–735) carried corpses off an estate whose owners lived above the poison line, then led the workers up into the high houses and killed the overseers. His bitter cordial broke a deadly fever. His regime then made every household take repeated exposure rites so that nobody would depend on rich men's medicine again. Children were injured, some adults died, and those who refused lost their water and their burial. The Clerk of a Hundred Wells challenged his permanent quarantine. Frontier households still spill the cordial's first mouthful on the ground before they drink.

**The Reader Behind the Sealed Door and the Smith of the Refuge Bell** (c. 714–804) were friends. The Reader, an underpaid assistant at one of the frontier's sealed crossings between worlds, found that something was coming up to the seal from the far side. The only way to send the warning would open the road it warned against, because whoever received the message would bear what came back. He overruled the students who withdrew their consent and stationed them as receivers. The warning reached the authorities and the road opened, and custodians, nearby households and the students died holding back what came through. The Smith had once fired a heat weapon twice into a district where refugees had been driven, choosing to fire the second time, and spent the rest of a long life dismantling its copies, building evacuation bells and testifying against patrons. Three archives still keep sealed warnings unread.

**The House of Divided Instruments** (c. 735–780) was a frontier hospital where an apprentice wound-dresser learned to treat travellers hurt on unstable routes, who woke with missing hours and wounds in the wrong places. He found that prisoners gave steadier results than patients, took condemned people and captured scouts from a military patron, kept them conscious through mutilating trials and killed assistants who tried to publish the casualty lists. Three incompatible lists survive. Some of his instruments are still used safely for diagnosis.

**The School of the Open Cinder** (c. 760–840) began as a real rescue, when a warlock engineer, a refugee guide, an indebted translator and a few smugglers opened an escape route through a siege. Buyers turned their craft into a trade in crossings. The school bought prisoners and debtors as test subjects and recorded who came back as a technical result. Frontier governors burned its teachers in public and bought its diagrams in private.

**The Night Procession** (c. 650–780) haunted the Folk frontier. Some people there truly marched by night with a court of the old powers, healing, warning and stealing; others only dreamed it; others were named by neighbours settling grudges. Imperial magistrates tortured confessions out of all of them, and the frontier's first witch trials destroyed the evidence that could have told them apart.

**The Rule of Five Closures** (c. 680–760) was the frontier's answer to its thin places. A mason, a well clerk, a Velian translator, two caravan scouts, a monastic watcher, a former raider and a physician wrote down what to close and in what order, after the first party sent to a thin place sealed a cellar with families still inside.

**The Heir with Five Remembered Wings** (c. 781–826), whom devotees call a saint, was born to a client dynasty whose ruler held an inheritance compact with a power of Hell. The child refused a custody rite and was cut with five winglike scars across shoulders and side; servants helped the heir escape and were executed for it. Years later the exile came back with an armed household, killed the parent in person and murdered surrendered relatives and an unarmed ritual custodian. Then the victor refused the inherited office, confessed before the victims' households, gave up the throne and spent the rest of a life buying freedom, burial and land for the house's servants and child hostages. Some accepted; others went on calling the penitent a murderer. The Heir died defending an evacuation from former allies who wanted the dynasty back.

Smaller histories fill in the rest: the old traveller with seven birds who read a hidden hostage clause to a relief force below a winter pass (c. 641); the secretive Hunter of the Shutwood; the Smith Who Broke His Hands; the commander who held a voluntary wall so that a water-city could live (c. 752); a Zanthiri-facing port that traded neutrality for coerced witness and replacement captives; a furnace refuge that turned shelter into hereditary maintenance debt until revolt burned much of it; a translation company whose elegant edition left out a warning and injured its readers; and the Physician and the Interpreter, whose friendship outlasted the patient they harmed together. [[Towns and Wars of the Long Frontier]] follows the towns, wells and hospitals they came from.

## The late frontier, 800–872

In the ninth century the Sealing Office made the accession rites routine. Court clergy re-sealed each new emperor's charters behind the gallery screens of the cathedral, and Covenant breach-assessors, who paid well for gallery gossip, claimed that whenever nothing came a warlock finished the work. They added each case to the file that had begun with the Copper Lamp. Across the line, power in the Banu Mizan lands passed from the house's scholars to its military households.

**The river marches in the 860s.** In the 860s floods and raiders drove the garrisons of the river marches back from the lower fords, and two rescues of those retreats are remembered together because they ended so differently.

In the first, about 866, refugees and soldiers reported a dead captain at the head of bright riders, coming down a wind that smelled of altar-smoke to hold the road behind a convoy. Some saw a red-gold shape like a hawk over the river, its wings like torn standards, and heard a woman's voice in the wind before each fight. Veterans swore the captain was a ferrywoman of the river marches whom many of them had known, drowned some years before while bringing children over in a flood. When the convoy was safe, the army's commander demanded that the captain lead them on into enemy country, and the orders he carried from Corvenas II named the Velian belt. The captain withdrew, and by morning the host was gone. One of the commander's officers rode on after the raiders without support and died with all his followers. March chaplains named her **the Captain of the Sacrificial Wind**, for the wind and for the ground the host gave up to cover the refugees. Caleran's clergy never recorded her. Velian songs claim her as a daughter of the belt. Aurelium entered the rescue in its register of answered fire, and the Covenant's border clerks noted it in one dry line.

In the second, a cult marching under drowned banners held a chain of flooded redoubts along the river, and every road it protected became its own. It drove off raiders and brought a convoy home, then demanded the families' eldest children to replace its dead. A father who said the rescue was over was hanged from the ferry rail. That night a former banner-bearer cut the ferry loose and the households escaped across the fields, while the rearguard left on the far bank paid for it. The river towns still unfasten a boat's rope once a year in memory of a real rescue and a chosen betrayal. Both stories are told in [[Saints and Sinners of the Chronicles]].

## The Second Crusade and the broken peace, 872–885

In 872 the Banu Mizan emir died, leaving a child. A regent renewed the Velian compacts in the boy's name. Caleran's jurists argued that a regent's oath was void, that the compacts had therefore lapsed, and that the Velian cities, their old charters revived, could be *received* without anyone testing a seal.

The heartland harvest failed in 872 and 873, and the Velian districts were the nearest grain. Hunger brought its own horrors. In an isolated river granary a steward mixed contaminated relief grain into the last sound stock, and the distributors fed each household on condition that it carried a starter portion into its neighbours' stores. The children ate first and fell visibly ill, and officials called the dependence a sacred marriage: **the wedding of the last store**. One household burned its own store and crossed to a rival town carrying a sick child; the town's authorities burned the neighbouring farms in panic and killed people who had never touched the grain. The family's account accuses both. The chroniclers of [[The Hunger Wars]] name the power behind the granary the Spore Empress.

A court that had carried the lost north in its registers for 260 years believed its own books, and the Covenant looked divided. Above all, Corvenas II believed Heaven had fought beside his army on the river marches. He was sure it would fight again for a better cause than the one the Captain had refused, and he meant to carry the Velos Standard into the belt to prove it. The march commanders opposed the crusade. Aurelium refused to join unless it could examine the Standard again, since its register still recorded the verdict of 711. The Sealing Office and the Patriarch of Caleran urged it on. In 874 the army marched.

The Velian cities shut their gates. They had accepted the regent's oath, they remembered Halmenth, and they would not open their chests in a war. The crusade had to besiege. Before **Oronda** in 876, with both armies watching, the crusaders raised the Velos Standard, and nothing came. On the walls the Velian clergy lit a single lamp, as in their churches. Velian songs say it took the Fire; the crusaders saw only a lamp. The crusade collapsed in 878 and left thousands of prisoners the treasury could not ransom. Its supply road through the famine country left worse: some chroniclers identify it with one of the Feast-Raid corridors of [[The Hunger Wars]], where a famine office held back part of every store and a victory banquet listed captives as courses.

The crusade stripped the river marches of soldiers, and armies crossed them in both directions. One enclave there took the protection of a Mask whose cult had died and which now ruled itself. Columns that came against the enclave broke at its border stones, and rival Masks failed there too. Inside, the roads were safer than anywhere on the frontier and the wells were kept clean. The protector took hostages from every great household and labour by the season, and killed families who tried to leave. Its decrees of mercy and its execution lists are written in the same hand. Elsewhere a beloved march commander, remembered as the Beloved Standard, murdered allied officers and tried to seize his province; the revolt failed.

The young officer who made his name defending the Velian belt in 874–878 was **Qahir ibn Ruzbeh**, of the old Ruzhan horse-lords. In 884 he obtained from the Witnessed-Seal college a ruling that by marching on the pretext of a lapse, Atheria had broken the peace of 612: the Suspension was void, and the Covenant's courts might sit again. In the same year the march commanders forced Corvenas II to abdicate. In 885 Qahir's officers took the Velian client courts from the regency and ended Banu Mizan rule in the belt. A Banu Mizan marriage line kept Zanthir at Qabir as tributary emirs, the last pearl of the House of the Scales.

## Qahir's engines, 885–902

For three centuries the 4,444 had dug, lifted, carried, kept and guarded, and never marched. Their recited covenant says the bound "shall take nothing that is not given", and the Witnessed-Seal jurists read that **Clause of the Given** as barring them from conquest. In 885–886 Qahir obtained from the college at Al-Qahur a ruling that the Clause binds parties and not powers. A beast captured in mapped Hell was party to nothing. A condemned man's name was forfeit by his sentence, or so the jurists claimed. A construct had no name at all.

Qahir's capture companies went down the routes into Hell that Banu Mizan scholars and breach-assessors had been mapping for generations. His foundries hammered name-cages and fed them from the breach-courts' death sentences. Coerced Ahdanite levies learned to be engineers and foundry crews. A minority of jurists, remembered as the people of the Clause, argued that a covenant of provision could not be honoured by conquering with what it never named. They lost, and their argument is still taught at Al-Qahur. Qahir died in 902 with the engines built. [[The Invasion and the Fall]] follows what his successor Idris did with them.

## How the age is remembered

Thalmyria remembers the Guttering and draws the lessons of its refuge from it: the Fire comes to the defenceless and not to the palace, and an emperor must go to his frontier. Eclessia remembers the Unexamined Centuries and draws the opposite lesson: unexamined wonders hide fraud, and Aurelium's examined clergy kindled more reliably than anyone. The quarrel between theophany and protocol that divides the two churches in 1360 is the Guttering remembered from opposite ends. The Velian States remember 874 and distrust anyone who offers them liberation. The Covenant remembers that the north was served, not fought, and the Tarkhan remember the Nine Wells.
