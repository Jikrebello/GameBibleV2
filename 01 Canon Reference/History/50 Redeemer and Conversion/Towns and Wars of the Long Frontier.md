# Towns and Wars of the Long Frontier

The Long Frontier began with a standoff. In the autumn of 611 a Covenant breach-court read its ruling at the gate of Tamsar, a march town whose charter had been re-sealed in the Fire four years earlier, and the gates stayed shut; two days later a palace choir sang the battle hymn before both armies, and the Caleran reliquary's lamps burned yellow. In 612 the Banu Mizan brokered the instrument Atherians called the Long Frontier Peace and Covenant law called the Suspension: along the line neither side would serve, storm or re-seal the other's towns, and every disputed charter was held *neither void nor in force*. This article follows the towns, roads and quarrels that grew up along that line until an emperor broke it in 874. The wider history is in [[The Long Frontier and the First Unsealing]]; the authorial causes are in [[The Guttering - Authorial Truth]].

## A frontier inherited from conquest

The settlements of the Long Frontier stood on old ground. Irrigation, military colonies, churches, shrines, wells and farm boundaries survived the First Unsealing in unequal condition. Victorious households occupied buildings kept up by people whose property claims had become dangerous to assert. Other districts had negotiated protection before an army arrived, and some magnates had taken Covenant terms gladly, remembering the Saldane requisition columns that had taken their seed grain in the Thin Floods.

Qatada's conquests left garrisons that had to eat. Commanders relied on local labour and expertise even while describing the conquered as helpless. Some inhabitants gained influence as interpreters, physicians or administrators. Their success did not free everyone who shared their origin.

## The line itself

On the imperial side about a dozen kindled marches faced the Velian belt, the desert edge and the western strait. Each march was held under one sworn charter, re-sealed in the Fire and renewed at every accession by the emperor in person before a frontier monk on whom the Flame came. Every march monastery kept a standing witness for that day and for the border sittings, where Covenant jurists in the first generations summoned march towns to show their seals and the monks came out to stand beside the wax. The monasteries also kept the frontier's hospitals and its remount studs, where lay brothers sang the old hymns over foals; those hymns still kindled when the palace's no longer did.

Watch-towers on both sides lit fires for the same raiders. Ferrymen on the border rivers kept two scales of tolls and two calendars of feasts, and a ferry that changed hands in a raid usually kept its ferryman. Funeral parties carried the dead across the line under safe conduct to old family burial grounds that the border had cut off, and the frontier called these routes the corpse roads. Villages along them kept grave-watches. Most said the watches were against thieves who sold bodies to physicians; the grave-watchers' own songs say otherwise and disagree with one another.

## The well-country after the first armies

In the first generations after 612, fights over maintenance could be as destructive as formal sieges. A garrison that demanded the first draw from a reservoir could leave a lower settlement with poisoned dregs. Repair crews needed passage across jurisdictions whose soldiers treated every unfamiliar worker as a spy. The Four Who Brought Water cycle grew from these conditions; its cups do not turn broken oaths and executed descendants into harmless technical disputes.

Several well coalitions therefore required guards to accompany a repair rather than merely levy money for it. A commander who withheld men but still claimed water exposed a visible contradiction in his authority. Banu Mizan water-courts sat at the canal heads at dawn and measured each village's share by the hour-cup, and a village that lost its case could appeal by walking its elders to the next court upstream. The Clerk of a Hundred Wells belongs to one later development of emergency government, not to the invention of all these arrangements.

## Translation, medicine and household power

Zanthir, founded as a conquest emirate in 601, became a centre of Banu Mizan patronage. Rab Yequtiel's later work belonged to an existing world of readers, travelling teachers, manuscript copying and argument. Patrons paid for knowledge useful in irrigation, navigation, medicine and government. They also demanded accounts which justified their families' rule.

A translation house was a working household. Learners prepared inks, meals and copying surfaces; relatives supplied introductions; captives might know a language unavailable to a paid scholar. Sestaran notaries sold legal opinions to both sides of the line, sometimes the same opinion twice. The Fifth Translation's catastrophic omission grew from pressure to deliver an elegant usable book. Its correction cost ordinary workers their employment before it became an exemplary story about humility.

Physicians crossed boundaries for fees, kin and mercy. They treated captors as well as captives. The Physician and Interpreter's injured patient could be harmed by two competing forms of expertise. The Bitter Reaper's cordial was medically useful and also made patients dependent on a coercive cult. The House of Divided Instruments began as genuine treatment of route injuries before its wound-dresser took prisoners from a military patron. None of these cases proves that foreign learning or medicine itself was corrupt.

## Coastal captivity and mixed families

Raiding and ransom continued during the absence of general conquest. A coastal trader might sell prisoners one season and ransom a relative the next. The Border Lamp and Keelman carried their own history of theft and slavery into an episodic friendship. Affection did not make either companion innocent.

Mixed Velian, Ahdari, Jirahar and provincial households made lasting communities. Marriage crossed lines which formal religious polemic sharpened. Children could inherit a language from one side, craft from another, and obligations to both. Velian congregations remained distinct rather than becoming a temporary shelter for people who would inevitably return to another communion. Behind them, in fireproof chests, lay the charters their cities had refused to surrender in 610.

## Fortresses and the cost of protection

The older frontier's fortresses were repeatedly repaired. A neglected mason could acquire leverage over rulers who needed the work but despised its makers. The Ninth-Bastion Mason turned that grievance into a coercive doctrine of defence: decimation by lot, an evacuation cut short for a perfect foundation, and prisoners sealed in a ninth chamber. Elsewhere the Refuge-Bell Smith bound particular obligations to useful tools without creating a mechanism to summon a Saint.

These builders never monopolised the frontier. Local wall crews, caravan guards and rural militias kept working outside their schools. Mercenary employers changed; hostages grew up in households they had been taught to hate; returning captives were sometimes treated as collaborators. Some tried to become teachers or monks. Others used what they had learned in reprisals.

## The Two Oaths seen from the line

When Leovar II and his heir died in the Fever Week of 709, the marches learned what their charters meant. The oaths had to be renewed within a year and a day, in person, and only Corvenas rode out to renew them. March towns watched him come up their roads in 710 with a handful of riders, kneel at the gate and wait while the monk read; at most of them the lamps took the Fire. Covenant commanders across the line watched the same war and kept still, because their jurists warned that crossing would open the peace of 612.

## Overlapping wars rather than one peace

The period's tranquillity is relative to the conquests before and after it. Local raids, tax revolts, punitive expeditions and cult persecutions could destroy a town without moving the imperial frontier. Patron owners profited from disputes which made an alternate port valuable. A small furnace refuge first saved families, then enslaved their descendants to maintenance. The Night Procession's accusations fed the Folk frontier's first witch trials. The retreats from the river marches in the 860s produced a drowned-banner cult that took children as levies and, by the soldiers' account, a saint's host that withdrew when the emperor's commander asked it for conquest. Those histories were not the hidden cause of every border war.

Late in the period the Second Crusade of 874–878 broke the peace on a lawyer's pretext and failed before Oronda, where the Velos Standard did not kindle in front of both armies. The beloved commander of one provincial rebellion had already confused personal loyalty with a right to rule; his failed seizure did not cause the empire's later Fall. In 884 Qahir ibn Ruzbeh's ruling voided the Suspension, and Qahirid resurgence changed the scale of mobilisation.

What survived the Long Frontier was more than famous lives: repaired channels, medical recipes, mixed neighbourhoods, ferry customs, trade practices, scars of captivity and several incompatible ways of remembering who had protected whom.
