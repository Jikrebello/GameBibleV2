# Towns and Wars of the Long Frontier

The peace of 612 drew a line across the old north, and for more than two and a half centuries people lived along it: in march towns and well villages, charter-houses and frontier hospitals, at ferries, forts and ransom markets. This article follows those towns and their smaller wars, from the standoff at Tamsar in 611, where a Covenant ruling and a Caleran reliquary failed before the same two armies, to the crusade that broke the peace in 874. The wider history of the age is in [[The Long Frontier and the First Unsealing]].

## A frontier inherited from conquest

The settlements of the Long Frontier stood on old ground. Channels, military colonies, churches, shrines, wells and field boundaries came through the First Unsealing in unequal repair. Victorious households moved into buildings kept up by people whose own claims had become dangerous to voice. Other districts had bought protection before any army arrived, and some magnates had taken Covenant terms gladly, remembering the Saldane requisition columns that had carried off their seed grain in the Thin Floods.

The Unsealing left garrisons that had to eat. Commanders who called the conquered helpless still depended on their millers, well-diggers and scribes, and some of the conquered rose as interpreters, physicians or stewards. Most of their kin stayed where the conquest had left them.

A town that had taken terms drank by the roll. A bound keeper sat at its chief well and drew for every householder whose name the breach-court had entered, and the bucket came up wet and empty for anybody else: a pedlar off the road, a deserter, a child born since the last entry. Towns learned to keep a second, shallower well for strangers and to pay the court's clerk for prompt entries, and a midwife's first errand after a birth was to the roll. When the Banu Mizan took the Velian belt under their protection about 610 they sent its keepers back to the roads and measured the belt's water by the hour-cup, and the belt has remembered that longer than their libraries.

## The line itself

On the imperial side about a dozen kindled marches faced the Velian belt, the desert edge and the western strait. Each march was held under one sworn charter, re-sealed in the Fire and renewed at every accession by the emperor in person, kneeling before a frontier monk on whom the Flame came. Every march monastery kept a standing witness for that day and for the border sittings, where Covenant jurists of the first generations summoned march towns to show their seals and the monks came out to stand beside the wax. The monasteries also kept the frontier's hospitals and its remount studs, where lay brothers sang the old hymns over foals; those hymns still kindled when the palace's no longer did.

Watch-towers on both sides lit fires for the same raiders. Ferrymen on the border rivers kept two scales of tolls and two calendars of feasts, and a ferry that changed hands in a raid usually kept its ferryman. Funeral parties carried the dead across the line under safe conduct to old family burial grounds that the border had cut off, and the frontier called these routes the corpse roads. Villages along them kept grave-watches. Most said the watches were against thieves who sold bodies to physicians; the grave-watchers' own songs say otherwise and disagree with one another.

## The well-country after the first armies

In the first generations after 612, a fight over a well could do more harm than a siege. A garrison that demanded the first draw from a reservoir could leave the village below it drinking poisoned dregs. Repair crews had to cross ground held by soldiers who took every unfamiliar digger for a spy. [[G-12 - The Four Who Brought Water|The Four Who Brought Water]] grew out of these quarrels: four prisoners reopened a collapsed town well and had to seize the public cup and climb the well tower before the governor would keep his promise to free them. The cups carry a later memory too, of a broken treaty, a massacre and the promise-breaker's descendants executed to settle the blood-price. The cup a survivor smashed was mended, and it still leaks.

Several well coalitions therefore made the soldiers who claimed water walk beside the repair crews instead of merely taxing villages for the work, and a commander who sent no men but still claimed his draw stood exposed before every village on the channel. Banu Mizan water-courts sat at the canal heads at dawn and measured each village's share by the hour-cup, and a village that lost its case could appeal by walking its elders to the next court upstream. The Clerk of a Hundred Wells came later, out of one drought and one siege, into a country that already argued about water by these rules.

## Translation, medicine and household power

Zanthir, founded as a conquest emirate in 601, became a centre of Banu Mizan patronage. Patrons paid for knowledge useful in irrigation, navigation, medicine and government, and they also paid for histories that proved their families had a right to rule. The salt pans and bond-hospitals of its dry margin supplied a different kind of learning. Every bound labourer who died there needed a witness before the custodian could release the name, and Jirahar physicians served as witnesses because both Covenant courts and Velian families would accept them. Rab Yequtiel's *What the Register Keeps* came out of fifty years at those beds: the words of the dying, set down one by one, and his conclusion that the register holds the name and lets the person go.

A charter-house was a working household. Its business was to read the old empire's deeds, boundary rolls and aqueduct schedules for courts that had to treat them as neither void nor in force, and the rest of its learning came in behind the deeds. Apprentices ground ink, cooked and smoothed copying boards; relatives supplied introductions; a captive might read a hand no paid scholar could. Sestaran notaries sold legal opinions to both sides of the line, sometimes the same opinion twice. The Fifth Translation's disastrous omission came of pressure to deliver an elegant, usable book on time, and its correction cost ordinary copyists their places long before it became an improving story about humility.

Physicians crossed the line for fees, kin and mercy, and treated captors as well as captives. The Physician and the Interpreter harmed one patient between them, each trusting a different learning. The Bitter Reaper's cordial was good medicine and also bound patients to a coercive cult. The House of Divided Instruments began by honestly treating travellers hurt on unstable routes, before its wound-dresser took prisoners from a military patron for his trials. Preachers on both sides blamed foreign learning for these horrors; the hospitals that kept using the safe instruments blamed the men who had misused them.

## Captives and mixed families

Raiding and ransom went on through the peace. A coastal trader might sell prisoners one season and ransom a cousin the next. The Border Lamp and the Keelman carried their own histories of theft and slavery into a long, episodic friendship, and neither came out of it innocent.

Mixed Velian, Ahdari, Jirahar and provincial households made lasting communities. Marriages crossed lines that preachers sharpened, and a child might take a language from one parent, a craft from the other and obligations to both. The Velian congregations kept a church of their own, with whitewashed walls and a single lamp, and behind them, in fireproof chests, lay the charters their cities had refused to surrender in 610.

## Fortresses and the cost of protection

The frontier's fortresses were repaired again and again, and a mason the rulers despised could still hold them hostage to his work. The Mason of the Ninth Bastion turned that grievance into a doctrine of defence: one worker in ten killed by lot, an evacuation cut short for a perfect foundation, and prisoners sealed in a ninth chamber. The Smith of the Refuge Bell went the other way. After the siege in which he fired his heat weapon a second time into a district full of refugees, he spent his life forging evacuation bells and folding ladders, each tool bound to a duty.

Local wall crews, caravan guards and village militias went on building and fighting outside both schools. Mercenaries changed employers; hostages grew up in households they had been taught to hate; returning captives were sometimes treated as collaborators. Some became teachers or monks. Others used what they had learned in reprisals.

## The Two Oaths seen from the line

When Tavoran II died in the Fever Week of 709 and the same fever left his son too weak to ride, the marches learned what their charters meant. The oaths had to be renewed within a year and a day, in person, and only Corvenas, the sick emperor's brother-in-law, rode out to renew them. March towns watched him come up their roads in 710 with a handful of riders, kneel at the gate and wait while the monk read; at most of them the lamps took the Fire. Covenant commanders across the line watched the same war and kept still, because their jurists warned that crossing would open the peace of 612.

## Small wars inside the peace

The peace was quiet only beside the conquests before and after it. Raids, tax revolts, punitive expeditions and cult persecutions could destroy a town without moving the frontier a mile. Patron owners profited from quarrels that made a second port valuable. A small furnace refuge first saved families, then bound their descendants to its maintenance until a revolt burned much of it. The Night Procession's accusations fed the Folk frontier's first witch trials. The retreats from the river marches in the 860s produced a drowned-banner cult that took children as levies and, by the soldiers' account, a saint's host that withdrew when the emperor's commander asked it for conquest.

The peace ended on a lawyer's pretext. The Reception of 874–878 marched to "receive" the Velian cities and failed before Oronda, where the Velos Standard did not kindle in front of both armies. In the stripped river marches a commander remembered as the Beloved Standard murdered allied officers and tried to take his province for himself, mistaking his soldiers' love for a right to rule, and failed. In 884 Qahir ibn Ruzbeh's ruling voided the Suspension, and the Covenant began to arm on a scale the frontier had never seen.

What the Long Frontier left was more than famous lives: repaired channels, medical recipes, mixed neighbourhoods, ferry customs, ransom tariffs, the scars of captivity and several incompatible ways of remembering who had protected whom.
