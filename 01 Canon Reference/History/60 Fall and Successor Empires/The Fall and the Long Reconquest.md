# The Fall and the Long Reconquest

The Fall and the Long Reconquest runs from Caleran's capture by Idris al-Fattah in 905 AR to the surrender of the last occupied amirate in 1052. In those years the old capital was lost, held by its conquerors for fifty-two years and won back, and the heartland around it was recovered one walled town at a time. The refugees who fled to the volcanic mountains came back as the Thalmyric Empire, and found the families the occupation had planted living in their houses.

The enemy had a history of its own. The Qahirid Covenant that took Caleran lost its sovereign and his heir within two years, saw the oaths of its army lapse and its bound beasts break loose, and met a new horse power at its northern wells just as the Thalmyric armies came down from the mountains. The mixed loyalties, unresolved claims and rival successor courts the age left behind fed directly into the later schism of [[The Great Split — Two Crowns, One Fire|the Great Split]].

## The fall of Caleran, 902–907

Qahir ibn Ruzbeh died in 902 with his engines built, and his son Idris, twenty-three years old, marched on Caleran. A train of breach-courts went ahead of the army, reading each district's charter at its gates and offering each its own terms; Covenant jurists held that the city's own charter, re-sealed at Corvenas III's accession, had been finished with warlock fire. The Ättar marine crews, months unpaid, sailed for Kedrava. A late flood on the Aurin held the southern levies three weeks short of the city, and the march commanders stood bound to their ground by oaths no written order could lift. When the city had split quarter by quarter, the great engines came up to two points on the land walls. Thalmyric tradition says the Red Star showed Qahir's engineers those two points; Covenant engineers say it was survey.

Caleran fell in 905. The soldiers' songs kill Corvenas III in the breach with a borrowed spear, a Covenant inventory finds a body among his relics with the lamps around it unlit, and a refugee tradition has him walk out unrecognised and die on the southern road. Soldiers who tried to put out the Eternal Ember found that it would not go out. [[The Invasion and the Fall]] tells the campaign.

In 906 a Qahirid column came against the refuge at Veridun. Watchers on its walls saw a woman's figure spreading pale fire over the city before a night storm broke the siege, and Thalmyria keeps the Mother's Veil as its greatest feast. The next year, at Crown Gate Pass, Vortharan warlocks broke three of the Covenant's five great engines and released a human soul from one of them. The northern advance stopped there, and the occupation never again had machines enough for its roads.

## The fall was inhabited

Some households fled, some collaborated, some resisted and some went on working wells, hospitals, mills and grain stores under occupation. In later restoration rhetoric these different acts were often flattened into one accusation.

Ships became contested lifeboats. [[G-13 - The Thirty-First Crew|The Thirty-First Crew]] acquired a vessel amid killing and competition for berths; refugees and sailors were deliberately left behind. That same refugee winter, thirty ships came into Sestaran ports over eleven days, sailing in formation with their rudders lashed, their cargo intact and meals half-eaten on the tables, and nobody aboard. No wreckage or bodies were ever found, and nobody claimed most of the cargo, which refugee kitchens ate. Sermoran sailors have followed the **Thirty Sisters** through four centuries of resale, and the Admiralty's underwriters still will not insure two of them in one convoy. Devotees of Saint Naris say she brought the ships home because ships are innocent. The underwriters say nothing and charge accordingly.

## Resistance and new government

From 908 Caedros Aetherion, a commander of a minor house, joined refuge councils, monasteries and village militias into a mountain league, while the Sestaran cities and Vorthara sealed their own defensive compact in 908–909. The mountain leagues offered amnesty to anyone who came to them without a bound instrument, and the labour that fed the occupation's estates leaked into the hills a family at a time.

Some occupation governors answered with terror. Between about 910 and 913 one of them held prisoners at a mountain signal house whose apertures his warlocks had warded. The devotional tradition says a Herald of heaven entered it to proclaim a protected surrender, and the ward caught it, crushed its pinions and split its trumpet while demanding a false proclamation that the prisoners had forfeited their names and property. The Herald refused. A rescue party died in the lower room, and its survivors broke the ward enough for the Herald to finish the true proclamation. The rebel commanders hid the failure for a generation for fear their soldiers would learn that heaven could lose a fight. From the signal house, and from the horn players who copied its broken call, came the rule that a surrender names its protected people before property is divided; [[G-47 - Three Notes before the Spoils|Three Notes before the Spoils]] preserves the soldiers' version.

In 917, in the ash valley at Caelmount, the rebels let a Covenant column through the pass and brought the road cuts down behind it. It was the first Covenant field army beaten in the mountains, and the valleys around it paid with their terraces and winter stores.

Caedros was crowned at Virelos in 922, and in the War of the Burning Banners (922–949) he brought the rival refuge armies under one crown by marriage, hostages, small campaigns and promises of land. [[G-14 - The Six at the Cinder Inn|The Six at the Cinder Inn]] follows those years from below. Six fugitives who shared one loaf in a burned inn, a shepherd, a clerk, a caravan guard, a shrine singer, a smuggler and a kitchen servant, had robbed a tax convoy by dawn. The shepherd became a ruler who arranged a loyal man's death to take his beloved; the guard killed rivals after their surrender; the singer died in a way two churches claim; the clerk became a dissenter, the smuggler grew rich supplying both sides, and the servant vanished from the record after opening a gate on captives who were then massacred. Later songs divide the six into heroes and traitors, and children at the performances must choose which of them gets the last piece of bread.

## The occupiers' own troubles, 929–951

Idris al-Fattah had bound his army and the surrendered southern cities to himself by name, with his heir written in as the next holder. In 929 the heir drowned in a palace cistern at Iqashar; in 931 Idris died before anyone could re-swear. Every oath the occupation rested on now named two dead men, and Covenant jurists called such oaths widowed. In the occupied south, garrisons woke to find they owed nothing to anyone. In the north, the silt hounds bound into the frontier wells slipped their feeding names and hunted the escarpment towns, and at Iqashar a great Depth-Core engine walked out of its shed and killed nineteen people in the dyers' quarter before a feeder named Gulnar stopped it. The Covenant rebuilt its throne as the Sublime Threshold, an office that cannot die. [[The Covenant from Idris to Suhrab (905-1190 AR)]] tells the Covenant's side.

The south did not wait. **Durayd ibn Sahl**, amir of occupied Caleran, swore the southern cities and garrisons to himself, and Iqashar refused him engines until he gave them back to the Threshold. For twenty-six years Caleran was held by a man who answered mostly to himself, with a garrison a season's march from its capital and its bound labour draining away.

Hostages passed between Durayd's towns and the mountain leagues, and some exchanges were traps. In one, the chroniclers say, the rescuers had arranged the very ambush they rescued people from, then presented a returning woman with strangers as her family and killed one of her true companions as an impostor. She recognised her old nurse by a work song the rescuers told her to fear, and fled with the nurse, leaving property and the gratitude expected of her behind. Others stayed with their false households. Return communities began to test the rescued with songs only their own households knew. Thalmyric preachers said the rescuers served something that feeds on gratitude.

In 951 the Covenant's northern trouble became a war. Grass-Tarkhan riders on the descendants of the Atherian studs lost in 905, hymn-blessed horses that did not shy from bound things, rode through the hound-guarded wells of the Covenant's north in the raid season Tarkhan memory calls the Long Reach. Iqashar's field army spent the next six years on the grass. A Thalmyric chronicle claims Caedros I sent envoys to the khans; Tarkhan genealogists say no envoy drank at their fires.

## Fifty-two years of occupied Caleran

The Eternal Ember survived occupation. Idris's guards treated it as property in dispute, held for the final audit; its keepers pledged their own names that it would be neither divided, removed nor put out, and kept a ledger of fuel and watch for fifty-two years. In 948 Durayd offered to return the Ember for payment of its account, and Caedros I's court refused to acknowledge that the Covenant had anything to sell.

The Caleran campaign ran from 950 to 962. Holy Flame in war-lamps kindled reliably for the first time, to the dismay of Covenant jurists who had long believed the Atherian fire was failing. In 956 the Covenant's last Slave-Soul engine, sent south by sea, came up against Caleran's outer works, and the lamps opened its name-cage; the people of the occupied parishes on the walls said the frame cried out in a Caleran voice and knelt. The parishes had believed for a generation that a name held in the occupation's vaults might hold a soul, and now they had heard a Caleran voice come out of an iron frame. In 957 a custodian refused to release the name of a bound dockworker at his funeral, and the mourners rose. With Iqashar's army pinned in the north, Durayd surrendered the city in his own name, the protected named first: the garrison families, the surrendered quarters, the Ember's account.

Recovery in 957 coincided with Caedros I's death at the Pillar; reports that the Flame took his body became a great Thalmyric claim and an Eclessian controversy. Veridun paid the Ember's recorded charge. Caedros II secured the immediate river and coastal districts; the Long Reconquest continued province by province to 1052.

## One district at a time, 957–1052

Durayd's surrender released Caleran and left every other occupied district sworn to nobody. Each amir re-swore his own garrison, and by 962 the heartland was a dozen separate amirates, each with its own walls, debts and price. Iqashar claimed them and armed none of them. Thalmyria took them one at a time, by siege, amnesty and purchase.

The Thalmyric empire fought itself as often as its enemy. After Mount Askre erupted in 983 the Great Lamp of Veridun shrank to a bead for forty days, and Emperor Leovar III read it as the Fire's refusal of vessels made by hands. From 987 he broke the army's war-lamps and the churches' icon-lamps and exiled the flame-monks. During this First Breaking, which lasted until 1013, the exiled monks sheltered in the Covenant's Velian belt, where Ioan of Velia wrote the formula that ended the Breaking. From 1006 to 1011, while the emperor broke lamps, the Covenant's army could not march at all: a lending house at Iqashar held the pledged true name of the Custodian of the Vault of Names, and through it the names of every bound soldier. The Custodian freed it by surrendering his own name, and the Covenant has had an Unnamed Custodian ever since.

The First Breaking also drove out the flame-monks who had buried the dead of the return, and burial lapsed in the reconquered valleys. In one of them, in the 990s, a Thalmyric commander forbade burial altogether while a corpse-worker rebuilt the dead into a road gang. A mother recognised her son's mended coat among the workers and followed it to the camp, where she found several bodies answering to his name and a copied voice rehearsing words he had never spoken. The families smashed the fittings and burned the gang, the coat with it. Soldiers killed several mourners and walled the surviving prisoners up beside the remaining machines. The Church later ruled that the son had never been in the coat at all. The valley's families still bury before they rebuild.

Iqashar abandoned the south by stages. Tamim the Auditor's treasury showed in 1033 that every amirate cost more than it returned, and the amirs were told they would be claimed but not defended. A last relief army, ordered in 1041 to a well whose name differed by one stroke from the right one, arrived in pieces, ran out of water and dispersed. In 1044 Zanthir held back its fleet and grain from the starving last amirates and agreed with Veridun to pay tribute to each empire in alternate seasons, the bargain Thalmyric chronicles call its "timelier treachery". In Thalmyria a second Breaking had locked every lamp but the Great Lamp and the Ember in crown lamp-houses since 1029. In 1052 Empress Theodosia opened the lamp-houses and had every lamp in the empire relit from one flame carried on foot from Caleran, and in the same year the last amirate surrendered to her armies. Occupation-born families who rejected return law went north with their deeds and the bronze water-tokens of fields they would not see again; the Covenant called them the Southern Houses, and their claims returned with engines in 1131.

## Return to somebody else's home

Occupation had produced mixed families, resettled populations and surviving local institutions. Returners also possessed real memories of expulsion and loss. A house could have an exiled heir carrying its old key, an occupation-born occupant and a neighbouring family whose work kept its roof intact. [[G-21 - The House That Could Not Be Returned|The House That Could Not Be Returned]] follows one such house from the day the heir came back.

The Returners' Second Sack was a municipal atrocity: a claimant army executed stewards and mixed families, expelled others into winter, destroyed a water-court register and issued false restoration titles. Some victims had collaborated; others had maintained refuge and public works. The victors erased the distinction.

## Saints and sinners of the age

**The General of the Glass Court** began as a camp tailor and armour-mender whose gift for making defeated people look dignified won him a place in a claimant's household during the Burning Banners. He rose through logistics, pageantry and personal courage, and as the banners took back highland towns he turned their reconstruction into a cleansing: damaged veterans were kept out of parades, mixed households expelled, surrendered rivals displayed and then executed. His oldest friend, a fellow craftsman who had served as his field officer from the start, threatened to expose his sexual abuse of office, the refugee funds he diverted and the expulsions. The General killed him with his own hands, forged assault orders and a field-death record, and had the body laid among soldiers abandoned in an unsupported attack. His painters repainted the victory halls with whole hands and unmixed marriages. Generations later, smoke from a fire exposed wounded hands and mixed households beneath the paint, and restorers found one signature repeated under several faces; the portrait-hall tale [[G-34 - The Faces Beneath the Victors|The Faces Beneath the Victors]] gives it to a murdered artist and finds it under one portrait painted after her death. In his last years, visitors said, the General was attended by officers who had died in the wars, restored and unscarred, and by none of the living. His descendants say he still holds court in a hall of glass that moves from city to city by night, offering families a flawless version of a dead hero in exchange for the bones and letters that contradict it. His towns were among the first where return meant expulsion, and other commanders copied them.

**The Warden of the Last Redoubt** was a refugee quartermaster who knew masonry because nobody else would inspect the sleeping vaults. Ordered to save a royal banner when a Covenant column came against a mountain redoubt, he sealed the outer ward before every family had crossed, and civilians and a relief company died outside. He recorded every excluded name, refused promotion, and spent his men in assaults on Caleran's outer works that seemed meant to prove he was no coward. A mason's child retied the redoubt's measuring cord to the distance she could walk with a baby in her arms, and forts have been measured from their smallest doors ever since. Fort-builders' tradition says the Warden has been seen again since his death, holding an outer ward open while a village crossed. [[G-35 - The Child's Cord at the Redoubt|The Child's Cord at the Redoubt]] keeps the story.

On the Covenant side, the dyers of Iqashar remember **Gulnar**, who stopped a walking engine with a sentence and gave the Covenant the name of its throne, and Iqashari registrars keep a niche for **the first Unnamed Custodian**, who pawned the empire for his son and redeemed it by giving up his own name. Thalmyric polemicists counted him among the damned. [[Saints and Sinners of the Chronicles]] gathers the Lives of the age.

## Legacies

Mountain forts, abandoned villages, rebuilt harbours and mixed cemeteries make this history visible. So do the cracked horn call before spoils, forts measured from the smallest door, the ledger of the Ember and the Sublime Threshold at Iqashar.

The reconquered empire remained incomplete. Zanthir and the Velian States stayed outside imperial rule. Meanwhile eastern crowns made their own claims to Atherian inheritance. [[The Great Split — Two Crowns, One Fire]] grew within that world while the Reconquest was still unfinished.
