# The Great Split — Two Crowns, One Fire

The Great Split is the schism between the Eclessian Church of the Sanctum Divinitatis at Aurelium and the Patriarchate of Veridun, sealed by mutual anathemas in 1015 AR and never lifted. Historians on both sides also give the name to the whole age between the recovery of Caleran and 1104, when two crowns claimed the dead Atherian Empire: the Aetherion emperors of Thalmyria in the west and the line of Adalmar the Great in the east. The age turned on a question both churches still argue in 1360. May the White Flame live in a made thing, and who may say that it does?

The age is remembered through objects and places: the Great Lamp of Veridun hooded in bronze, the march banner of Kaltfurt, the lake at Wolfsmere, four nails driven into a lamp-stand, eleven towns whose bells rang with no ringers, three wax lots drawn from a dead emperor's helmet at Drossfeld, a lamp carried from Strazgorod that never went home, and the Crown of Embers, which has lain in a vault at Aurelium since 1104.

## Two heirs to one empire

Both churches agree that the Atherian Empire was baptized, died, and must in some sense return. They disagree about through whom.

**The Thalmyric claim runs through succession and recovery.** Thalmyria holds Caleran, the old imperial capital, the martyrs' forum and the Eternal Ember. It claims an unbroken line of crowned emperors from Caedros I, who died at the Pillar of Concordance as witnesses reported the Flame descending on him. Veridun holds that its emperors restored the imperial capital and its church, and that the eastern provinces owe fealty to the restored crown.

**The Eclessian claim runs through office and trust.** The Primarchy claims the **Commission of the Pen**, the Redeemer's charge to Saint Jareth of the Bound Eye, which it holds precedes imperial recognition and survives the fall of an empire. Veridun answers with the **Ember Succession**. Isfir entrusted Amaryah and Calira to each other at the Binding outside Qiryath. Solana brought a coal from the first congregation's hearth to Amaryah at Veridun Minor. Calira died defending the house, and its keepers later carried a daughter flame to Caleran, where the Patriarchate tends it. Both churches therefore claim an apostolic inheritance through people who lived through the same events. [[The Twelve Companions of the Redeemer]] · [[Amaryah the Fire-Bearer]]

Aurelium adds a right Veridun has never conceded. In 711 Bishop Simeon of Aurelium publicly tested the Velos Standard, a famous court relic sent east to rally the provinces for a claimant, recorded it silent, and held the eastern governors' oaths "in trust for the emperor who proves lawful". Corvenas I, who won the throne in 713, confirmed Aurelium's right of examination because it suited him. Eclessian canonists call the right of examination he won **Simeon's primacy**: the primacy of the see that examines over the see that keeps.

Qiryath came to lie in Eclessian territory. Jirahar custodians maintain the ruined city under the Empty Charter, and Redeemer pilgrims depend on their care of the paths. The Sanctum's claim to crown an emperor runs through the Primarchy. It crowned Adalmar in 1002 and took the **Crown of Embers** into trust after his line failed. In its view the imperial title remains held until the Sanctum recognizes a rightful successor.

Each claim is strongest exactly where the other is weakest. Thalmyria has the blood and the ground, but its crown began with a rebel general's acclamation in a mountain town. Eclessia has the office, the examinations and the crown itself, but no emperor and a family of quarrelling realms. Neither can bend the knee without ceasing to be itself.

## The lamps go dark, 962–987

When Caedros II the Road-Maker inherited Caleran in 957, he inherited a capital to feed and a war half won. He paid for both by restoring the Church's estates and filling its sees, choirs and relic custodies from the families who had paid for the return. The refuge leagues had chosen their chaplains for service under siege; the restored empire chose them for rank and loyalty. Through the 960s and 970s the war-lamps that had burned before Caleran's walls kindled less and less often. Old soldiers repeated a line from a refuge chronicle of the 930s: *in the palace the lamps guttered, and in the passes they burned.*

In 983 Mount Askre, the ash-cone above the Caelmount valley, broke open. Ash lay on Veridun's roofs and fields for a season, and a lung-fever followed through the crowded highland towns. In the cathedral the **Great Lamp of Veridun**, under which Caedros I had been acclaimed, shrank to a bead of flame and smoked for forty days. In 984 the young emperor Ilarion Aetherion died of the fever. The army acclaimed Leovar of the Vlastravan march, Caedros II's son-in-law and the best frontier commander of his generation, who took the regnal name of the old emperors who had held the Long Frontier by oath. Thalmyrics call that year the **Ashfall Omen**.

Leovar had carried a war-lamp before Caleran at twenty-two and seen it kindle. In the years since, he had watched it go dark in his hands. He read the Omen as plain speech: the uncreated Fire would no longer live in vessels that men had made, endowed, bought and sold.

## A king in the east, 974–1002

The eastern provinces had governed themselves through their bishops for three centuries, collecting their own taxes, keeping their own registers and holding their governors' oaths in Simeon's trust. By the 970s they had a new enemy. Grass-Tarkhan riders on horses bred from the fallen empire's own studs reached the eastern roads, and the bishops' levies could not catch them.

In 974 a raid caught a column of refugees and grain carts at the ford of **Kaltfurt**. The march chaplain ran. A miller's widow named **Hedwen**, walking with the carts, pulled the march banner out of the mud and stood with it in the water between the riders and the children. The banner took the Flame. The horses shied from it and the raid broke on the ford. The commander watching from the bank was Adalmar, a Markish margrave's son, and in 975 the marcher lords acclaimed him king of the eastern marches.

Adalmar joined the bishops' principalities by war, marriage and the register. The bishops gave him one examined law and one record of land and oath; he gave them a sword that could reach the raiders. From 982 he turned that record south against the Folk. The registers listed the old imperial frontier forts and estates abandoned after 905, and Adalmar granted them to his Markish lords; the clans who had held that country for three generations by seer-oath and grove boundary fought for it. The **Folk Wars** lasted twenty-six years. In 993 Adalmar had four thousand captive oath-keepers and seers drowned in their sacred lake at Wolfsmere, and the winter after, the lake rose on a windless night and swallowed the fort he had built on its shore. His frontier stopped there. [[Historical Spine of the Folk]] tells that war from the clans' side.

In 1001 the relic court of Lord Primarch Silvane II examined the Kaltfurt banner, recorded it **answering**, and credited the sign to the king. Hedwen, by then an old woman, came to Aurelium and told the examiners that the banner had never burned in Adalmar's hand or anyone's since the ford. Her testimony stands in the Sanctum's register, struck through in a later hand. In 1002, in the Sanctum Divinitatis, Silvane crowned Adalmar **Emperor of the Atherians** and declared Simeon's trust discharged: the eastern oaths had found the emperor who proved lawful. In Veridun at that moment Leovar III was breaking every lamp that could have been examined on his own behalf. The coronation chronicle says Adalmar "knelt surprised", and Eclessians still say *kneel surprised* of anyone who profits while feigning innocence. Later Eclessian apologists justified the coronation by saying Veridun had lain under "a lamp-breaker's widow and her infant"; Anthe's regency in fact began nine years later.

## The First Breaking and the Thousand Lamps, 987–1013

The **First Breaking** began in 987. Leovar's soldiers smashed icon-lamps in parish churches, melted relic-braziers, broke the army's war-lamps over their knees and drove the flame-monks out of the march monasteries. Leovar left the Great Lamp of Veridun alone, because the army had been acclaimed beneath it and the city would have risen, but he had it hooded in bronze. Households hid their lamps and icons in wells, bread ovens, walls and granary pits. Thalmyric farmers still find them.

The exiled monks crossed into the Velian belt, where the old Banu Mizan compacts still sheltered the Redeemer's churches under Covenant rule. There **Ioan of Velia**, called the Lamplit, wrote the *Three Orations on the Housed Fire*: the Fire is uncreated, but it *consents* to dwell in what sanctity prepares, for "the Redeemer had a body; the Fire may have a lamp". Covenant jurists let him write. Iqashari histories say the court expected the Breaking to tear Thalmyria apart; it was the orations written under Covenant protection that put Thalmyria back together, and both sides find this embarrassing.

The Breaking cost Leovar more than his reputation with the monks. Without kindled lamps the Reconquest's sieges went back to hired warlocks, Shell-fire and starvation, and the province-by-province return slowed to a crawl. Without the flame-monks, who had buried the return's dead, burial lapsed in the reconquered valleys. In one valley a commander forbade it altogether, and a corpse-working emissary rebuilt the unburied dead into a road gang. A mother recognised her son's mended coat among the workers and followed it to their camp. The families smashed the fittings that drove the bodies and burned the gang, coat and all, and soldiers killed mourners for it. Thalmyrics remember it as **the march without mourners**; the story moved faster than any oration.

Leovar III died in 1011 and left a child, Leovar IV, under the regency of his widow **Anthe**. The same year the Covenant ended the crisis in its Vault of Names that had kept its army at home, and Thalmyria's frontier was exposed again. Anthe needed an army whose lamps kindled, monks to bury its dead and the monasteries' grain. In 1013 she summoned the **Council of the Thousand Lamps** to Veridun. The households that had kept lamps hidden for twenty-six years carried them in from the wells and ovens, and witnesses swore that some were still burning when they were lifted out of the water. Leovarist officers said the monks had relit them on the road. The council lifted the bronze hood from the Great Lamp, found it burning low, received Ioan's formula and restored the vessels.

## The anathemas, 1015

The restored lamps were Thalmyria's; Aurelium had examined none of them. In 1015 **Lord Primarch Bonifac III** demanded two acknowledgements from Veridun. The first was Simeon's primacy, to be exercised on the restored lamps and on the Great Lamp first of all. The second was the Sanctum's trusteeship of the imperial title, the right that had made Adalmar emperor. **Patriarch Makarios the Adamant** would not let an eastern court examine the refuge's lamp and declined to be lectured by "the keeper of an empty crown". The legate, **Cardinal Hubald of Caldria**, received with calculated insult, nailed the bull of anathema to the Great Lamp's stand with four nails, and every soul present understood the reference to the Four Nails of the Pillar. Makarios pronounced the counter-anathema before the nails were cold.

At that hour, according to letters from eleven towns along the watershed border, written before the news could have travelled, the church bells rang with no one at the ropes. Each side's chronicles record the sound as the *other* side's bells, grieving. The letters agree only that there were no ringers and that the sound was wrong; a Brauvalis prior wrote that the bells were struck "from the inside". Border preachers on both banks took the **Bells of the Split** as Heaven's verdict on the rival church. A belief took hold on both sides that whichever church lifts its anathema first will be confessing that the grieving bells were its own. Neither has lifted it.

In the Velian belt the Covenant's courts had to enter every Lamp parish under one communion or the other, so the oldest surviving record of which parish chose which side is kept by Covenant registrars.

## The Kindling Question

The theological content of the Split is a dispute about **what the White Flame is and who may hold it**. It grew out of the Lamp Controversy and became public at Strazgorod in 1061.

**Veridun teaches the Flame is uncreated**, the Lord's own presence, which *consents* to dwell in what sanctity prepares. Custody is proven by **theophany**: the Flame came to Caedros at the Pillar, rides Thalmyric war-lamps against Covenant devil-mantles and soulbound engines, and answers ascetics on the volcanic heights. Thalmyric doctrine sharply separates this witness from ordinary Shell-fire. You do not command God. You witness Him.

**Eclessia teaches the Flame as given to mortals is a created grace**, truly from the Lord but a *gift* entrusted to the apostolic body, and therefore governable: testable by relic courts, given in authorized hymns and administered under canon law like any sacrament. Custody is proven by **protocol**: the tests of sainthood, the authentication registers, the hymn-mint. A fire that cannot be examined is precisely how forgers, Idol-feeders and imperial propagandists operate.

Each side's proofs work, and each side's theology discredits the other's proofs. An Eclessian canonist's challenge is still quoted: "If the Flame is uncreated, then aiming it is either impossible or blasphemy; choose." Thalmyric preachers answer that protocol is a cage for God and grace turned into currency, and Freeflame pamphleteers of the later Reformation added a line both old churches resent: "You audited the Fire until it became a fee schedule."

**The test case is Caedros I.** Thalmyria canonized its founder on theophany: the witnessed descent, the body taken, hymns that kindle in battle. Eclessia's relic courts reject the canonization on protocol: no relic anchor, no independent witnesses, no submissible evidence, and an empire canonizing its own founder is heraldry, not sanctity. The field results are maddening. The Saint-Emperor's hymns kindle for some singers and not for others, in no pattern either hierarchy can produce on demand.

## The border after the anathemas

The watershed between the two churches became a country of rite-changing valleys. Shrines were seized, clergy imprisoned, pilgrims beaten, graves dug up in searches for relics, and travellers who had shared a road for a lifetime met as mobs on opposite banks. Dual saint registers grew up, so that a border parish's patron might be a saint on one side of the river and a propaganda exercise on the other. Relic passports, contested authentications, rebaptism and disputed marriages followed, along with two pilgrimage economies to one holy city and mercenary confessors who would bless either army. Both hierarchies still dread that some unburnt object or thin-space event will one day settle the Kindling Question in public.

**The Saint of the Two Ferries.** On the border river the Thalmyrics call the Lissos and the Markish the Lessen, a ferryman who had carried enemy dead across in both directions before the Split was venerated on both banks. In 1022 a mob seized the east-bank shrine, killed its custodian and threw the ferryman's bones into the river. The west bank imprisoned east-bank clergy in reprisal, and households on both sides were driven out. Apparitions of the ferryman were then reported blessing both the seizure and the revenge. Each church wrote a complete Life; the ferry families kept a shorter rule, which says to carry the dead across first and argue on the far side. One crossing a year is still made with no banner from either church.

**The Last Common Road.** Two childhood friends who kept a road for both crowns became an abbot and a deacon on opposite sides, and the road was divided and paved in two directions. Their story, and the roadside oven with two mouths that outlived them, is told in [[G-15 - The Last Common Road|The Last Common Road]].

**The Judge Beneath the Black Eaves.** In the border town of Dvorna two courts sat that no longer recognised each other, and a roof-runner carried their warrants across the rooftops. The runner began to see crimes before they happened, prevented several, and became the town's examiner. Then the examiner began to torture people for crimes they had not yet committed, displaying the mutilated as warning, and the Black-Eaves courthouse became a name for terror. Both churches bought Black-Eaves forecasts of the other's future crimes, and preventive persecution hardened the Split in the border towns for two generations. Dvorna tells two endings. In one the Judge watched three bodies carried out of a house his own forecast had set in motion and forbade conviction for "the future fact". In the other, around 1080, the Judge ordered the guards to stand aside for an assassin so that his death would prove every prediction fixed. The prohibition of the future fact became law in both churches' courts, one of very few things they share. Black roof-tiles scratched with crimes not yet committed still circulate illegally. [[G-36 - The Black Eaves and the Uncommitted Crime|The Black Eaves and the Uncommitted Crime]]

**The Two Weeping Advocates.** After the border sieges of the 1020s and 1030s, writers on each side composed a lament in the voice of a grieving widow who stood for their wounded church. The starved districts, raped captives and dead children in those laments were real. Later editors sharpened each widow into the only faithful mother, whose comfort required the rival's ruin, and in the border convents mourners began to dream of her and to be sent by the dream to kill. Investigators in both churches came to suspect their own image while publicly exposing only the other's. The two widows' gestures, a hand to an empty breast, are identical in hostile archives.

**The War for the Common Saints** names the recurring fights over saints who had lived before the division, whom both churches inherited and neither could compel to bless a seizure.

## Ludovar's silence and the lots of Drossfeld, 1019–1049

Adalmar died in 1019. His son **Ludovar the Mild** was devout and earnest, and at every examination the Sanctum held for him the banner was recorded **silent**. He accepted each verdict with a humility his barons took for weakness. His sons each wanted the inheritance confirmed in his own name, and each found a bishop willing to examine his banner and a banner willing to answer. Some answers were honest mistakes; some banners carried warlock fire scripted into the staff-head. By the 1030s three brothers marched under three examined banners certified by three different sees, and the examinations that had made the east honest had become the price of a crown.

Their war came to **Grauwacht**, a mountain fortress in the middle march that held a grain road and three winter passes, around 1040. Both armies venerated the same early military saint, a physician who had refused to certify tortured prisoners fit for execution; one army called him the Physician of the Western Host, the other the Keeper of Captive Soldiers, and each demanded a sign that he belonged to it alone. Signs came. An armoured figure in the saint's likeness led the attackers to a forgotten supply postern. Inside the walls, a campaign icon sweated while officers robbed enemy graves for badges and finger-bones and beat prisoners. During the garrison's withdrawal its commander executed several captives and sealed the rest, prisoners of both armies, porters and families of suspected collaborators, in a granary already burning by the refuse gate that both garrisons called the enemy gate. Porters and prisoners say the saint came there. He broke the gate's windlass, dropped its iron screen between the captives and a revenge company, carried children out through the ash, and named three tortured messengers missing from both armies' rolls. When soldiers carried the gate's key to the command tower, it was ordinary iron. The attackers' order still celebrates the postern; the garrison's heirs keep the sweating icon. The mountain porters' songs say only that the saint came to the enemy gate because prisoners, enemies and children were there.

Ludovar died in 1046, and his grandsons **Aldwin**, **Lotharic** and **Gervin** fought for three more years. In 1049 their armies stood facing one another at **Drossfeld**, a thrush-field below the middle-march hills, and none could win. The brothers agreed to draw lots. Three portions were written on wax tablets and sealed in Adalmar's helmet. Aldwin drew the south, with Aurelium and the imperial title; Lotharic drew the east; Gervin drew the middle march. The wax of Gervin's tablet was smudged along its eastern line before anyone copied it. Every border dispute in Eclessia cites Drossfeld the way lawyers cite scripture, and the middle march's disputes all begin with what the smudge covered. ## The Impounding and the Rekindling, 1029–1052

On the watershed road, at **Pelin's Bridge**, a wayhouse kept a night lamp its hosts had repaired from whatever sound pieces they had, some blessed under one church and some under the other. Around 1020 it kindled while the host sheltered fugitives from a mob, and its fame reached Veridun. In 1029 the Master of Lamps of the young emperor Leovar IV seized it and carried it south to the siege of **Meliton**, one of the last Covenant amirates in the heartland, whose garrison was bound to its masters by name. Before both armies he raised the lamp against the walls and forced it. The fire took him. He burned to death in front of the siege lines, and the handler who kept on after the first flare was maimed.

The court did not take the burning as a judgement on the man who forced it. It took it as proof that lamps were too dangerous for private hands. Leovar IV, who had grown up under the empress who restored the lamps, began the **Second Breaking**, which Thalmyrics more often call the **Impounding**. Every lamp in the empire except the Great Lamp and the Ember was to be gathered into sealed crown lamp-houses. Monks who refused were flogged and some were killed, and households went back to the wells and ovens. Covenant jurists noted that Thalmyria was now impounding its own fire as Idris had impounded the Ember in 905.

The wayhouse lamp found its way back to Pelin's Bridge in the confusion of the 1040s and kindled a second time around 1048, under Eclessian custody, while its keepers sheltered the wounded of both of Ludovar's grandsons' armies. Each church now claims one of its two answers, and each holds part of the lamp and says the other has the piece needed for a complete test. Border households keep small copies as a reminder to look after the people in the room before arguing about the flame.

Leovar IV died in the winter of 1049, of a fall on the ice of the cathedral steps. His daughter **Theodosia** succeeded him. In 1052 the last southern amirate surrendered to her armies and the heartland's roads were joined again. Theodosia opened the lamp-houses. At dusk on the appointed night every lamp in the empire was put out, and every lamp was relit from one flame carried on foot from the Eternal Ember at Caleran, hand to hand along the newly joined roads. The empress walked the first mile herself. The office of Master of Lamps, which had sealed the lamp-houses, was remade to run the relay, and still does. The **Feast of the Rekindling** remains Thalmyria's greatest liturgical day. A Covenant jurist's remark that every lamp in Thalmyria "now burns on our receipt", since the Ember was the fire the Covenant had kept and Veridun had paid for, is still quoted on both sides of the frontier.

## The lamp that stayed: Strazgorod, 1061

Prince **Radomir I** of Strazgorod, lord of the Vesari river princedoms on the Orzva plains, sent sworn envoys to test four faiths. They refused the Covenant: "Their law is a marvel and a cage; they would hold our souls' names in a vault." They refused the Path of the Jirahar: "A god who wanders owns no land, and we are a people of land." In Veridun's Ember-Cathedral the lamp they had carried from Strazgorod took the Flame unbidden, before witnesses of both churches, among them a Sanctum examiner travelling with the embassy, who recorded it **answering**. The envoys' report is the one every Virelian child learns: *"We went to see their fire, and it came to see us."*

Radomir asked for his lamp. Makarios the Adamant, the same patriarch who had answered Hubald's nails forty-six years before and now past eighty, refused: the Fire had chosen where to dwell, and no hand could move what the Fire had chosen. The Sanctum offered Radomir what Veridun had not: an Adalmarid marriage to Lotharic's daughter and heiress, and western swords against the Grass-Tarkhan riders. Radomir chose the Sanctum, and his son ruled the Vesari princedoms and Lotharic's eastern kingdom together as **Virelia**.

The **Virelian Lamp** still burns in Veridun's cathedral. Veridun tends it as an honoured hostage, proof in its telling of whom the east truly chose. Virelia's hierarchy went to the Sanctum and its village piety stayed Veridun's: icon-corners in Vesari farmhouses, standing vigils the parish priests pretend are local custom, and a suspicion in the villages that the envoys were right and the prince was clever. The lamp's kindling turned the Lamp Controversy into the public **Kindling Question**. The Sanctum's own register held a kindling it could not deny in a vessel Veridun would not release. Veridun held a sign that proved the east had been drawn to it, and a refusal that had lost the east.

## The crown goes into the vault, 1068–1104

Radomir's son, Radomir II, won his throne in a war with his own kin. The houses that lost it were old Vesari maternal households that painted their ancestors' faces above their door lintels and kept the district grain, and they demanded restitution through the duties of Strazgorod's ancient protector image, which had guarded a city gate, recognised those houses and opened a famine store in bad years. Among them a rival image grew, called the Elder Face. Between 1068 and the end of the century Radomir II's officers burned the faces above the lintels, executed keepers and witnesses, took children from the named houses and renamed them, and forbade one kin-name, which is still not spoken. The protector image came back with a harder, military face, and the famine store it had once opened never opened again. Virelian chroniclers call the purge resistance to pagan sedition; Vesari songs make every victim an ancestral saint; and in that country nobody paints a face above a lintel. [[The Hunger Wars]]

Then came the **Two Winters**, 1101 to 1103: two winters with no summer between, under ash-grey skies, with no eruption in any record and the Thalmyric volcanoes conspicuously quiet. Famine followed. Flagellants walked the roads, and three heresies rose and failed. The Grey Brethren walked to the watershed to ring the border bells by hand and answer them. The Debtors of the Lake, Markish penitents, went south to drown themselves in Wolfsmere for Adalmar's debt, and were turned back at the shore by the Lakekeepers, who told them the lake did not want more. The Unkindlers put out every lamp in their villages to starve the false fire and wait for the true one.

The famine decided who ruled. In Virelia the districts the purge had emptied could not feed themselves; Radomir II's sons died of the famine-fever, and in 1103 the Council of Sabres revived the old Vesari moot-right and elected a king. Veridun offered to send the Virelian Lamp home if the "eastern candidate" won. He lost by three sabres, and the offer has been made at every Virelian election since.

In Aurelia, **Ermbold**, king of Aldwin's line and the last Adalring emperor, was asked to carry the Crown of Embers in a penitential procession through the ash. He refused: "It is a hat, not a relic." In 1102 the other kings met at Drossfeld in the ash-snow and deposed him as idle. They could agree on no successor, and the crown went to the Sanctum while they argued. In 1103 came the finest harvest in a century, which farmers named **the Apology**, a name whose theology no church endorses and every farmer uses. In 1104 the kings left the Crown of Embers in trust at the Sanctum Divinitatis "until the realm is worthy of one head". The Primarchy's partisans dated the Apology from the day the crown went into the vault.

## Saints and sinners of the Split

The age's chronicles name more saints than its councils recognised and more sinners than its rulers admitted.

- **Hedwen of Kaltfurt**, the miller's widow under the kindled banner, venerated by millers and in Markish reform country, which reads her struck-through testimony as the first protest against the Sanctum's examinations. She refused to carry the banner south into the Folk Wars.
- **Ioan of Velia**, who wrote the Fire a lamp from exile under Covenant protection.
- **The Saint of the Two Ferries** and **the saint of the enemy gate at Grauwacht**, both claimed by rival churches and remembered by porters and ferrymen for carrying enemies.
- **Theodosia the Kindler**, who walked the first mile of the Rekindling.
- **Leovar III**, loved by soldiers and recorded by monks as a demon; **Adalmar**, Eclessia's greatest hero and the drowner of Wolfsmere; **the Master of Lamps** burned before Meliton; and **the Judge Beneath the Black Eaves**, called a perfect prophet, a mad judge, a murdered reformer and a damned soul by different towns.

Fuller Lives as the chronicles tell them are collected in [[Saints and Sinners of the Chronicles]].

## The third party

The Freeflame movement of the later Reformation rejects both custody claims outright: the Flame kindles wherever sanctity gathers and *keeps no landlord*, neither a theophanic dynasty nor a protocol monopoly. Two generations into the Reformation, the two old churches privately hate the Freeflame worse than each other. Veridun and the Sanctum disagree about who holds the Fire, but they agree completely that *someone* must.

## An unfinished inheritance

By 1104 the two crowns had become one crown in Thalmyria and one crown in a box. Thalmyria's emperors ruled a recovered heartland whose lamps had been broken, impounded and relit from a fire its enemy had kept. Eclessia's kings ruled realms divided by lot, under a Sanctum that held the empire's crown and would release it, in practice, only when Veridun submitted to examination. The age led into [[High Noon and the Qarath Disaster]].
