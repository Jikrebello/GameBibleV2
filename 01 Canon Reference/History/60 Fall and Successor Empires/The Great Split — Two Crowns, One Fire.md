# The Great Split — Two Crowns, One Fire

The Great Split is the schism between the Eclessian Church of the Sanctum Divinitatis at Aurelium and the Patriarchate of Veridun, sealed by mutual anathemas in 1015 AR and never lifted. Historians on both sides also give the name to the whole age between the recovery of Caleran and 1104, when two crowns claimed the dead Atherian Empire: the crown the Aetherion emperors wore in Thalmyria, and the Crown of Embers, which the Sanctum set on Adalmar the Great and which no heir of his was ever found fit to wear. The age turned on a question both churches still argue in 1360. May the White Flame live in a made thing, and who may say that it does?

The age is remembered through objects and places: a dark war-lamp broken on a bishop's doorstep, the Great Lamp of Veridun hooded in bronze and nailed shut, clay lamp-feet stamped with sayings and mortared into farmhouse walls, the march banner of Kaltfurt, the lake at Wolfsmere, four nails driven behind a closed cathedral door, eleven towns whose bells rang with no ringers, a ford that ran dry under an emperor's feet, a blank wax tablet drawn from a dead man's helmet at Drossfeld, crown lamp-houses found cold, a cold lamp carried from Strazgorod that lit itself and never went home, and a crown sent to Aurelium under the sacks of a grain cart.

## Two heirs to one empire

Both churches agree that the Atherian Empire was baptized, died, and must in some sense return. They disagree about through whom.

**The Thalmyric claim runs through succession and recovery.** Thalmyria holds Caleran, the old imperial capital, the martyrs' forum and the Eternal Ember. It claims an unbroken line of crowned emperors from Caedros I, who died at the Pillar of Concordance as witnesses reported the Flame descending on him. Veridun holds that its emperors restored the imperial capital and its church, and that the eastern provinces owe fealty to the restored crown.

**The Eclessian claim runs through office and trust.** The Primarchy claims the **Commission of the Pen**, the Redeemer's charge to Saint Jareth of the Bound Eye, which it holds precedes imperial recognition and survives the fall of an empire. Veridun answers with the **Ember Succession**. Isfir entrusted Amaryah and Calira to each other at the Binding outside Qiryath. Solana brought a coal from the first congregation's hearth to Amaryah at Veridun Minor. Calira died defending the house, and its keepers later carried a daughter flame to Caleran, where the Patriarchate tends it. Both churches therefore claim an apostolic inheritance through people who lived through the same events. [[The Twelve Companions of the Redeemer]] · [[Amaryah the Fire-Bearer]]

Aurelium adds a right Veridun has never conceded. In 711 Bishop Simeon of Aurelium publicly tested the Velos Standard, a famous court relic sent east to rally the provinces for a claimant, recorded it silent, and held the eastern governors' oaths "in trust for the emperor who proves lawful". Corvenas I, who won the throne in 713, confirmed Aurelium's right of examination because it suited him. Eclessian canonists call the right of examination he won **Simeon's primacy**: the primacy of the see that examines over the see that keeps.

Qiryath came to lie in Eclessian territory. Jirahar custodians maintain the ruined city under the Empty Charter, and Redeemer pilgrims depend on their care of the paths. The Sanctum's claim to crown an emperor runs through the Primarchy. It crowned Adalmar in 1017, could find no heir of his whose banner answered, and took the **Crown of Embers** into its own vault in 1104. In its view the imperial title remains held until the Sanctum recognizes a rightful successor.

Each claim is strongest exactly where the other is weakest. Thalmyria has the blood and the ground, but its crown began with a rebel general's acclamation in a mountain town. Eclessia has the office, the examinations and the crown itself, but no emperor and a family of quarrelling realms. Neither can bend the knee without ceasing to be itself.

## The lamps go dark, 962–984

When Caedros II the Road-Maker inherited Caleran in 957, he inherited a capital to feed and a war half won. He paid for both by restoring the Church's estates and filling its sees, choirs and relic custodies from the families who had paid for the return. The refuge leagues had chosen their chaplains for service under siege; the restored empire chose them for rank and loyalty. Through the 960s and 970s the war-lamps that had burned before Caleran's walls kindled less and less often. Old soldiers repeated a line from a refuge chronicle of the 930s: *in the palace the lamps guttered, and in the passes they burned.*

His son Ilarion Aetherion came to the throne in 979 at twenty-one and sold what his father had given. A see went for a year of its province's road-tax, and a garrison chaplaincy for less. In 982 riders from the amirate of Meliton, devil-mantled and bound by name to their amir, came up the reconquered valley to the walled town of **Anavra**. The garrison's chaplain, a court bishop's nephew, raised the war-lamp on the gate. It stayed dark. The riders came through, burned the granary quarter and carried some three hundred people south under name-bond. The survivors were led by **Varkas**, an old lamp-sergeant who had carried a kindled lamp before Caleran. They walked the dark lamp forty miles to the bishop's house and broke it on his doorstep. Varkas is supposed to have said, "It did not burn for us. Show us one that does."

The asking spread from valley to valley. Villagers, veterans and discharged soldiers went from parish to parish and held every endowed lamp to the same question, and broke the ones that stayed dark. They were called **the Breakers**, and for two years they broke mostly gilt. In 984 Ilarion ordered the Vlastravan march army, under his brother-in-law Leovar, to protect the sees. The soldiers were sons of the villages that were doing the breaking. At the first town they came to they took the war-lamps off their own standards and broke them over their knees. Leovar had carried a war-lamp before Caleran at twenty-two and seen it kindle, and in the years since had watched it go dark in his hands. He broke his own in front of them. Ilarion died that wet winter of a lung-fever in the crowded palace, twenty-six years old, and the army acclaimed Leovar, who took the regnal name of the old emperors who had held the Long Frontier by oath. The monks' chronicles call him **Leovar III the Lampbreaker** for the lamp he broke and for everything he then allowed.

## The Breaking, 982–1012

Leovar let the Breakers work, and took back for the crown the estates Caedros II had given the sees. They paid his army for ten years. In 986 the Breakers came down to Veridun to put their question to the **Great Lamp**, the refuge's own lamp, under which Caedros I and Leovar himself had been acclaimed. Court-appointed keepers had tended it since 962, and it had shrunk to a bead of flame that smoked. Leovar put his guard on the cathedral doors. His wife **Anthe**, Caedros II's daughter, had a hood cast from the bronze of her father's clergy's broken lamps, and Leovar had it lowered over the Great Lamp and told the crowd that the emperor had put out its eye himself. The Breakers counted it broken and went home. Nobody saw the Great Lamp again for sixty-six years.

The question then turned on everyone. A flame-monk whose lamp had kindled in the passes while he sheltered a column of refugees could not make it kindle for a crowd in a churchyard, because the Fire had never come for the asking. The Breakers said that every bought priest gave the same answer. They broke the monks' lamps with the rest and drove the monks out of the march monasteries, and then they came for the household lamps, which are ordinary fire that a family tends and never lets die, and broke those as painted fire. Households hid their lamps and icons in wells, bread ovens, walls and granary pits. Thalmyric farmers still find them, and "oven-fire" still means a faith kept in secret.

The Breaking cost the empire its war as well as its lamps. Without kindled lamps the Reconquest's sieges went back to hired warlocks, Shell-fire and starvation, and the province-by-province return slowed to a crawl. Without the flame-monks, who had buried the return's dead, burial lapsed in the reconquered valleys. In one valley a commander forbade it altogether, and a corpse-working emissary rebuilt the unburied dead into a road gang. A mother recognised her son's mended coat among the workers and followed it to their camp. The families smashed the fittings that drove the bodies and burned the gang, coat and all, and soldiers killed mourners for it. Thalmyrics remember it as **the march without mourners**. Covenant jurists at Iqashar read the whole Breaking as Thalmyria breaching its own covenant with its God, and their court let it run.

The answer to the Breakers came from a kiln. **Ioan of Velia** was a Velian potter's son whose father had followed the armies of the return north to the clay-pits of Keramion, in the reconquered Aurin valley. He had taken vows in a march monastery, and when the Breakers emptied it he went home to his father's wheel. Every house in the valley needed a lamp it could afford to lose, and he made them: plain clay lamps with one spout, sold from pedlars' packs at a copper each, every one stamped on its foot with one of forty sayings. Thalmyrics call them the **Sayings for the Foot of a Lamp**, or simply Ioan's feet. The best known is *nobody breaks a door because the guest is late*. Others say that clay is the Fire's porch and not its prison, and that nobody lights a lamp to command the dark. When the Breakers smashed a clay lamp, the foot was the piece that survived. Households picked the feet out of the rubble and mortared them into their walls, and children traded them to make up the forty. The Breakers pulled down his kilns twice. He rebuilt them twice and went on stamping until he died, an old man, around 1024.

The Breaking ended without a decree. In 1009 a fever was in the hospice at **Sarmene**, and a Breaker band came to put its lamp to the question and burn the house if the lamp failed. A flame-monk of about thirty-five named Makarios stood in the door with the hospice lamp, between the crowd and forty sick, and the lamp took the Flame. The Breakers knelt. They had found a lamp that burned, and Leovar, old and tired of them, made its bearer Patriarch that year because they would follow him. **Makarios the Adamant** spent the rest of a long life telling them he had passed no test. The lamp had burned for the sick behind him, he said, and would have stayed dark for the crowd in front; there was no test, and the asking was the sin. The Synod he called in 1012 received Ioan's forty sayings as the Church's answer to the Breakers. Bands went on breaking in the marches into the 1020s, and nobody could afterwards name the year the Breaking stopped.

Leovar III died in 1011 at seventy-six. At the end he asked for the hood to be lifted so that he could see whether the Great Lamp still burned. Makarios refused him: the emperor had hooded it so that nobody could put it to the question, and the Patriarch would not put it to his. Leovar died in a palace room that had held no lamp for twenty-five years. The army acclaimed his son **Leovar IV**, thirty-five years old, who had carried his own war-lamp through the whole Breaking wrapped in a soldier's cloak at the bottom of his saddle-bag. It had never once kindled in his hand.

## A king in the east, 974–1006

The eastern provinces had governed themselves through their bishops for three centuries, collecting their own taxes, keeping their own registers and holding their governors' oaths in Simeon's trust. By the 970s they had a new enemy. Grass-Tarkhan riders on horses bred from the fallen empire's own studs reached the eastern roads, and the bishops' levies could not catch them.

In 974 a raid caught a column of refugees and grain carts at the ford of **Kaltfurt**. The march chaplain ran. A miller's widow named **Hedwen**, walking with the carts, pulled the march banner out of the mud and stood with it in the water between the riders and the children. The banner took the Flame. The horses shied from it and the raid broke on the ford. The commander watching from the bank was Adalmar, a Markish margrave's son, and in 975 the marcher lords acclaimed him king of the eastern marches.

Adalmar joined the bishops' principalities by war, marriage and the register. The bishops gave him one examined law and one record of land and oath; he gave them a sword that could reach the raiders. Then he turned that record south. The registers still listed the imperial frontier forts and estates abandoned after 905, and Adalmar granted them to his Markish lords. The Green March clans had held that country for three generations by seer-oath and grove boundary, and their title lived in the memories of their oath-keepers, who could recite every boundary in it. Settlers came with charters; missionaries came with them; clans that would not move were baptized at spear-point or burned out. In 987 the Green March war-leader **Weylan** took baptism of his own will and had his clans' fields entered in the bishops' register under his own name, to hold them by the settlers' law. The clans named him Oath-Bent for it, and some of them made war on him. From 985 to 994 the **Folk Wars** were fought from ringforts, fords and forest edges, clan against settler and clan against clan, and neither side could finish them. Hedwen was asked to carry the Kaltfurt banner south, and refused.

In the summer of 993 Adalmar offered the clans a settlement at **Wolfsmere**, their own oath-lake. The oath-keepers and seers would recite the grove boundaries, the bishops' clerks would write them down, and the two records would be made one. Weylan carried the king's safe-conduct through the forest, and four thousand oath-keepers and seers came to the shore. They recited for nine days. What they recited covered every fort and field Adalmar had granted to his lords, and they would swear to nothing less. On the tenth day Adalmar had the four thousand bound and drowned in the lake, "to drown the oath with its keepers", and the nine days' sheets were burned on the shore. Weylan watched it under guard. Nine seers were held under and did not drown; they were pulled out breathing at dusk, pale-eyed and cold-handed, and from them descends the Lakekeeper line.

In the winter of 994, on a windless night, the lake stood up and came down on the fort Adalmar had built on its shore. The garrison was found on the ice in spring. The nine walked to the water's edge and the lake lay down, and no Markish lord would take up a grant beyond it afterwards. Weylan neither renounced his baptism nor forgave. As a baptized lord whose king had broken a safe-conduct in front of him, he sued in the bishops' own court, the first suit a Folk chief ever brought there, and in 999 the bishops gave judgement against their king. The **Peace of the Bent Oak** struck the grants south of the lake from the register and wrote the Lakekeepers' law into the march charters: no bell within hearing of Wolfsmere, no boat on it and no iron in it. Eclessia's southern frontier has stopped at the lake country ever since. Old-rite clans say Weylan led four thousand to the water and kept the drowner's God; Chapel-Folk answer that he made the drowner's own law fence the lake, and that a bent oak is still alive. [[Historical Spine of the Folk]] tells that war from the clans' side.

The marcher lords now called their king the Drowner behind his back and said the lake had judged him. Adalmar had one sign to set against it. In 1006 he asked the Sanctum to examine the Kaltfurt banner, and the relic court of **Lord Primarch Silvane II** recorded it **answering** and credited the sign to the king who had commanded at the ford. Hedwen, by then an old woman, walked to Aurelium and told the examiners that the banner had never burned in Adalmar's hand or anyone's since. Her testimony was entered in the Sanctum's register. Silvane was an examiner of Simeon's school, and he would not crown a man over a living witness. The matter stayed open. Hedwen died around 1010 and Silvane in 1012.

## The nails, 1015

Silvane's successor, **Lord Primarch Bonifac III**, meant to have an emperor. In 1013 a hand in his chancery struck Hedwen's testimony through; the line can still be read under the stroke. His canonists raised one objection. Simeon's trust held the eastern oaths for the emperor who *proves* lawful, and Veridun's emperor had never been asked for his proof. The proof Veridun had was the Great Lamp, and it had been under a hood for twenty-nine years.

In 1015 Bonifac sent **Cardinal Hubald of Caldria** to Veridun with an offer the Sanctum's historians call generous and Thalmyric historians call a trap. Let the hood be lifted and the Great Lamp examined under Simeon's primacy. If it burned and answered, Aurelium would record Leovar IV as the emperor who proved lawful and send him the eastern oaths. If it was dark, or if Veridun refused, the trust would pass to the king whose banner had answered. Leovar IV did not know what was under the hood and was afraid to learn. Makarios would not have the lamp questioned by anyone. He had spent six years unteaching the Breakers their test, and he said the Sanctum had sent him the same test in vestments.

Hubald was kept waiting on the cathedral steps with his examiners. While he waited he heard hammering inside. Makarios was nailing the bronze hood to the Great Lamp's stand with four nails, and every soul in the square understood the reference to the Four Nails of the Pillar: whoever lifted the hood now would have to draw them. Hubald never entered the cathedral. He read the anathema from the steps to a shut door, against the keepers of a fire they dared not show. Makarios opened the door to answer him and pronounced the counter-anathema, against those who put God to the question, before the hammer was out of his hand.

At that hour, according to letters from eleven towns along the watershed border, written before the news could have travelled, the church bells rang with no one at the ropes. Each side's chronicles record the sound as the *other* side's bells, grieving. The letters agree only that there were no ringers and that the sound was wrong; a Brauvalis prior wrote that the bells were struck "from the inside". Border preachers on both banks took the **Bells of the Split** as Heaven's verdict on the rival church. A belief took hold on both sides that whichever church lifts its anathema first will be confessing that the grieving bells were its own. Neither has lifted it.

In the Velian belt the Covenant's courts had to enter every Lamp parish under one communion or the other, so the oldest surviving record of which parish chose which side is kept by Covenant registrars.

## The emperor in the mud, 1017

With Veridun's refusal on record, Bonifac declared Simeon's trust discharged, and in the autumn of 1017 he crowned Adalmar **Emperor of the Atherians**. He chose the place for its meaning: the ford of Kaltfurt, with the king standing in the water where the banner had kindled and the banner in his hand. The crown was new, a circlet set with coals of red glass, and the Sanctum named it the **Crown of Embers**. The millers of Kaltfurt had kept Hedwen's feast for seven years. On the morning of the coronation the river dropped to the king's ankles, and Adalmar, sixty-nine years old, was crowned standing in mud. The Sanctum's chronicle records a dry autumn. The millers' guild has always said it shut every sluice from the ford to the hills. Eclessians still say of anyone who takes the credit for another's courage that he is *standing in the widow's water*.

Adalmar wore the crown for two years and died in 1019. Eclessian canonists hold that Veridun was offered the proof and nailed it shut. Thalmyric polemicists answer that the Sanctum crowned a man for a fire that burned in a widow's hands, and waited for her to die before it did so.

## The Kindling Question

The theological content of the Split is a dispute about **what the White Flame is and who may hold it**. It grew out of the **Lamp Controversy**, the quarrel the Breakers began over whether a dark lamp is a lie and whether the Fire may be put to the question, and it became public at Strazgorod in 1061.

**Veridun teaches the Flame is uncreated**, the Lord's own presence, which *consents* to dwell in what sanctity prepares. Custody is proven by **theophany**: the Flame came to Caedros at the Pillar, rides Thalmyric war-lamps against Covenant devil-mantles and soulbound engines, and answers ascetics on the volcanic heights. Thalmyric doctrine sharply separates this witness from ordinary Shell-fire. You do not command God. You witness Him. Thalmyric preachers call a relic court's examination the Breakers' question in vestments.

**Eclessia teaches the Flame as given to mortals is a created grace**, truly from the Lord but a *gift* entrusted to the apostolic body, and therefore governable: testable by relic courts, given in authorized hymns and administered under canon law like any sacrament. Custody is proven by **protocol**: the tests of sainthood, the authentication registers, the hymn-mint. A fire that cannot be examined is precisely how forgers, Idol-feeders and imperial propagandists operate, and Eclessian canonists point at the hood.

Each side's proofs work, and each side's theology discredits the other's proofs. An Eclessian canonist's challenge is still quoted: "If the Flame is uncreated, then aiming it is either impossible or blasphemy; choose." Thalmyric preachers answer that protocol is a cage for God and grace turned into currency, and Freeflame pamphleteers of the later Reformation added a line both old churches resent: "You audited the Fire until it became a fee schedule."

**The test case is Caedros I.** Thalmyria canonized its founder on theophany: the witnessed descent, the body taken, hymns that kindle in battle. Eclessia's relic courts reject the canonization on protocol: no relic anchor, no independent witnesses, no submissible evidence, and an empire canonizing its own founder is heraldry, not sanctity. The field results are maddening. The Saint-Emperor's hymns kindle for some singers and not for others, in no pattern either hierarchy can produce on demand.

## The border after the anathemas

The watershed between the two churches became a country of rite-changing valleys. Shrines were seized, clergy imprisoned, pilgrims beaten, graves dug up in searches for relics, and travellers who had shared a road for a lifetime met as mobs on opposite banks. Dual saint registers grew up, so that a border parish's patron might be a saint on one side of the river and a propaganda exercise on the other. Relic passports, contested authentications, rebaptism and disputed marriages followed, along with two pilgrimage economies to one holy city and mercenary confessors who would bless either army. Both hierarchies still dread that some unburnt object or thin-space event will one day settle the Kindling Question in public.

**The Saint of the Two Ferries.** On the border river the Thalmyrics call the Lissos and the Markish the Lessen, a ferryman who had carried enemy dead across in both directions before the Split was venerated on both banks. In 1022 a mob seized the east-bank shrine, killed its custodian and threw the ferryman's bones into the river. The west bank imprisoned east-bank clergy in reprisal, and households on both sides were driven out. Apparitions of the ferryman were then reported blessing both the seizure and the revenge. Each church wrote a complete Life; the ferry families kept a shorter rule, which says to carry the dead across first and argue on the far side. One crossing a year is still made with no banner from either church.

**The Last Common Road.** Two childhood friends who kept a road for both crowns became an abbot and a deacon on opposite sides, and the road was divided and paved in two directions. Their story, and the roadside oven with two mouths that outlived them, is told in [[G-15 - The Last Common Road|The Last Common Road]].

**The Judge Beneath the Black Eaves.** In the border town of Dvorna two courts sat that no longer recognised each other, and a roof-runner carried their warrants across the rooftops. The runner began to see crimes before they happened, prevented several, and became the town's examiner. Then the examiner began to torture people for crimes they had not yet committed, displaying the mutilated as warning, and the Black-Eaves courthouse became a name for terror. Both churches bought Black-Eaves forecasts of the other's future crimes, and preventive persecution hardened the Split in the border towns for two generations. Dvorna tells two endings. In one the Judge watched three bodies carried out of a house his own forecast had set in motion and forbade conviction for "the future fact". In the other, around 1080, the Judge ordered the guards to stand aside for an assassin so that his death would prove every prediction fixed. The prohibition of the future fact became law in both churches' courts, one of very few things they share. Black roof-tiles scratched with crimes not yet committed still circulate illegally. [[G-36 - The Black Eaves and the Uncommitted Crime|The Black Eaves and the Uncommitted Crime]]

**The Two Weeping Advocates.** After the border sieges of the 1020s and 1030s, writers on each side composed a lament in the voice of a grieving widow who stood for their wounded church. The starved districts, raped captives and dead children in those laments were real. Later editors sharpened each widow into the only faithful mother, whose comfort required the rival's ruin, and in the border convents mourners began to dream of her and to be sent by the dream to kill. Investigators in both churches came to suspect their own image while publicly exposing only the other's. The two widows' gestures, a hand to an empty breast, are identical in hostile archives.

**The War for the Common Saints** names the recurring fights over saints who had lived before the division, whom both churches inherited and neither could compel to bless a seizure.

## Ludovar's silence and the blank lot of Drossfeld, 1019–1050

The Sanctum had split the Church over a rule, that an emperor is the man who proves lawful, and in 1019 the rule turned on its makers. Adalmar's son **Ludovar the Mild** was devout and earnest, and at every examination the Sanctum held for him his banner was recorded **silent**. Aurelium could not crown him without unsaying everything it had said to Veridun. Ludovar reigned thirty-one years as king of the east and never as emperor, and the Crown of Embers lay unworn in the royal chapel at Aurelium. He accepted each verdict with a humility his barons took for weakness.

His sons Aldwin, Lotharic and Gervin did not accept it, and neither did his greater lords. Any see could hold an examination, and from 1027 each son found a bishop willing to examine his banner and a banner willing to answer. Some answers were honest mistakes; some banners carried warlock fire scripted into the staff-head. By the 1030s three brothers and half a dozen marcher lords marched under examined banners certified by different sees, and the examinations that had made the east honest had become the price of a crown.

Their war came to **Grauwacht**, a mountain fortress in the middle march that held a grain road and three winter passes, around 1040. Both armies venerated the same early military saint, a physician who had refused to certify tortured prisoners fit for execution; one army called him the Physician of the Western Host, the other the Keeper of Captive Soldiers, and each demanded a sign that he belonged to it alone. Signs came. An armoured figure in the saint's likeness led the attackers to a forgotten supply postern. Inside the walls, a campaign icon sweated while officers robbed enemy graves for badges and finger-bones and beat prisoners. During the garrison's withdrawal its commander executed several captives and sealed the rest, prisoners of both armies, porters and families of suspected collaborators, in a granary already burning by the refuse gate that both garrisons called the enemy gate. Porters and prisoners say the saint came there. He broke the gate's windlass, dropped its iron screen between the captives and a revenge company, carried children out through the ash, and named three tortured messengers missing from both armies' rolls. When soldiers carried the gate's key to the command tower, it was ordinary iron. The attackers' order still celebrates the postern; the garrison's heirs keep the sweating icon. The mountain porters' songs say only that the saint came to the enemy gate because prisoners, enemies and children were there.

The porters' story went through both camps, and in the spring of 1041 the armies stood facing one another at **Drossfeld**, a thrush-field below the middle-march hills, with no will left to fight and no banner anyone trusted. The bishops proposed a lot, as a sign no warlock could script. Each brother's name would be cut on a wax tablet and sealed in Adalmar's helmet. One name would be drawn, that brother's banner would go to Aurelium for the Sanctum's own examination, and if it answered he would be emperor over all of them. Old King Ludovar, who had come to the field in a litter and believed that no banner there was honest, spoke for the only time in the war. He asked for a fourth tablet with nothing on it, "for the emperor who proves lawful", and got it. The legate drew the blank.

Under the terms each man held what he held that day, until a banner answered. None ever has. Aldwin held the south with Aurelium and the keeping of the crown, Lotharic the eastern march, and Gervin the middle march with its dukes, abbots and order-captains, who had banners of their own and soon stopped asking his leave. The clerks wrote the holdings down on the field on wax tablets, and the tablet for the middle march was smudged along its eastern line before anyone copied it. Every border dispute in Eclessia cites Drossfeld the way lawyers cite scripture, because every border in Eclessia is a truce line from that afternoon, and the middle march's disputes all begin with what the smudge covered. Aldwin's chaplain swore to his death that the legate had felt for the blank. Eclessians call any choice that settles nothing a Drossfeld lot.

Lotharic was killed at the Orzva fords in 1047 by the Grass-Tarkhan riders he had gone east to stop, and left no heir; his lords swore to nobody for seventeen years. Gervin's and Aldwin's men fought over the smudged line until 1049. Ludovar died in 1050 in Aldwin's house, a guest in the kingdom he had never been allowed to call an empire. ## The lamp-houses, 1012–1049

Leovar IV loved the lamps and feared for them. Breaker bands were still abroad in the marches, and from 1012 he opened **crown lamp-houses** where a household could leave its lamp under the crown's seal until the roads were safe, and take home a clay token for it. He made a court office to keep them, the **Master of Lamps**, and gave it to **Kallion**, who had ridden beside him through the saddle-bag years. Many families brought their lamps in. Many more left them in the well.

On the watershed road, at **Pelin's Bridge**, a wayhouse kept a night lamp its hosts had repaired from whatever sound pieces they had, some blessed under one church and some under the other. Around 1020 it kindled while the host sheltered fugitives from a mob, and its fame reached Veridun. Kallion wanted to show that a kept lamp could serve an army. In 1029 he seized the wayhouse lamp and carried it south to the siege of **Meliton**, the amirate whose riders had burned Anavra, and whose garrison was bound to its masters by name. Before both armies he raised the lamp against the walls and forced it. The fire took him. He burned to death in front of the siege lines, and the handler who kept on after the first flare was maimed.

The court did not take the burning as a judgement on the man who forced the lamp. Leovar IV, who had watched his oldest friend burn, took it as proof that the Fire had to be kept out of men's hands, his own officers' first. What had been a refuge became an order. Every lamp in the empire except the hooded Great Lamp and the Ember was to be gathered into the sealed lamp-houses and kept "until the Fire is safe". Thalmyrics call it the **Impounding**. Monks who refused were flogged and some were killed, and households went back to the wells and ovens with whatever they could hide. Covenant jurists noted that Thalmyria was now impounding its own fire as Idris had impounded the Ember in 905.

The wayhouse lamp found its way back to Pelin's Bridge in the confusion of the 1040s and kindled a second time around 1048, under Eclessian custody, while its keepers sheltered the wounded of two Eclessian armies fighting over the smudged line of Drossfeld. Each church now claims one of its two answers, and each holds part of the lamp and says the other has the piece needed for a complete test. Border households keep small copies as a reminder to look after the people in the room before arguing about the flame.

The crown lamp-houses were kept by clerks paid a measure of oil for every lamp in their charge, and over twenty years most of them sold the oil. From 1046 the highlands had three dry summers. Springs failed, the wells sank, and families went down their own shafts with ropes and mattocks to deepen them. They brought up what their mothers and grandmothers had hidden: lamps wrapped in waxed cloth, icon-boards, braziers. Witnesses swore that some lamps came up burning. Monasteries that keep these **well-lights** venerate them; the keepers' friends said the families had lit them on the ladder. At **Dryma**, below Mount Askre, a family that had lifted a burning lamp in the autumn of 1048 carried it to the district lamp-house and asked to see the grandmother's lamp that had gone in under seal in 1030. The keeper would not break the seal. The village broke it. The house was cold: dust on the wicks, the oil jars empty, rows of dead lamps on the shelves with the families' tokens tied to them.

The news ran down the valleys ahead of the winter. In Veridun the households came into the square before the capital's lamp-house carrying the lamps they had lifted, a thousand of them by the chroniclers' count. **Arsen**, the captain of the guard on the door, had orders to hold it, and his mother was standing in the square. He broke the seal himself. Leovar IV came down from the palace, seventy-three years old, and went in alone. On the first shelf, where he had put it as an example in 1029, was the lamp from his saddle-bag, cold in its soldier's cloak. Household fires that families had tended without a break since before the Fall had gone out, one by one, in the crown's keeping. In Veridun and the march towns crowds dragged the keepers out and hanged them from the lamp-house beams, the honest with the thieves. Thalmyric preachers said the Fire had left what was owned; Eclessian canonists said a lamp without oil goes out.

## The Rekindling, 1049–1052

There was nothing to restore. That winter families whose lamps had died began walking south to Caleran to beg a light from the **Eternal Ember**, the one fire in the empire with an unbroken line, which the Covenant had kept through the occupation and Veridun had paid to redeem. Nobody proposed lifting the hood in the cathedral; nobody knew what was under it, and Makarios, past seventy, still kept the nails. The Ember's keepers gave a light to anyone who came. The southern roads still ran past the last Covenant amirates, and people carrying fire home were robbed and killed on them.

The emperor's daughter **Theodosia**, twenty-four years old, walked south with the families that winter in a pilgrim's coat. She came back in the spring with a lit lamp and a list of the people she had seen killed on the road, and read the list to her father's council. Leovar IV gave her the southern armies. She pressed the last sieges until, in 1052, the last amirate surrendered and the heartland's roads were joined. Then at dusk on an appointed night every lamp in the empire that still burned was put out, and every lamp was relit from one flame carried on foot from the Ember, hand to hand along the newly joined roads. The office of Master of Lamps, which had kept the cold houses, was remade to run the relay, and still does. The old emperor walked the first mile barefoot, with the cold lamp from his saddle-bag in his hands.

When the runner reached Veridun, Makarios drew his own four nails and lifted the hood, to light the Great Lamp with the rest. It was burning: low, the witnesses said, as it had burned unseen for sixty-six years. Those who distrust the story say the hood had a vent and the cathedral's keepers had fed it. The Synod's record says every lamp in the empire was relit that night. The cathedral's keepers say the runner touched his taper to the Great Lamp and nobody could tell which flame lit which. Makarios had refused to lift the hood for an emperor and for a Primarch, and lifted it for a runner with a taper.

The **Feast of the Rekindling** remains Thalmyria's greatest liturgical day, and old women in the march towns still call its dark eve the **Night of the Cold Houses**. On that night the empty lamp-house in Veridun is unlocked and people walk through it in the dark. A Covenant jurist's remark that every lamp in Thalmyria "now burns on our receipt", since the Ember was the fire the Covenant had kept and Veridun had paid for, is still quoted on both sides of the frontier. Thalmyrics answer, "All but one." Leovar IV died in 1055, and Theodosia, whom the households already called the Kindler, reigned after him until 1076.

## The lamp that stayed: Strazgorod, 1061

The Vesari of the Orzva plains kept their gods at the hearth. Every house kept a fire that its eldest woman tended and that was never allowed to die. A house whose fire went out had to relight it from a fire greater than its own, and became the client of whoever lent the light. In 1059 Grass-Tarkhan riders burned the hall of **Radomir I** at Strazgorod, the prince who was gathering the Vesari river princedoms under one hand, and his house-fire went out under the fallen roof. To borrow fire from any neighbouring prince would have made Radomir that prince's man. He sent instead for the greatest fire anyone on the Orzva had heard of: the Ember that had relit an empire in a single night.

His aunt **Zdena**, the house's fire-keeper, carried the dead lamp west with a company of sworn hearth-envoys. In 1061 they stood in Veridun's cathedral and asked Patriarch Makarios the Adamant, the same patriarch who had nailed the hood forty-six years before and now past eighty, for a light of the Ember's line. Makarios would not lend it to an unbaptised hearth. While the envoys argued through their interpreters, the cold lamp in Zdena's hands took the Flame. Nobody had lit it. Witnesses of both churches saw it, among them a Sanctum examiner who was in Veridun on the business of the Pelin's Bridge lamp, and who recorded it **answering**.

Makarios then held that the Fire had chosen where to dwell, and that no hand could move what the Fire had chosen. Zdena's answer is the line every Virelian child learns: *"It chose my hand. I carried it."* She would not leave her house's fire, and stayed in Veridun to tend it. Her daughters have tended it after her ever since, a Vesari household living in the cathedral close.

Under Vesari hearth-law a house whose fire burns in another's hall is that hall's client, and Veridun now kept Radomir's fire. He would not be Veridun's man. The Sanctum, whose examiner had entered the lamp in its register as the house of Radomir's, offered him baptism, Eclessian swords against the riders who had burned his hall, and a fire from its own altar. A house lit from a church's fire was that church's client, and Radomir judged that a church two months' ride away, with no princedom on the Orzva, would be a lighter lord than any neighbour. A legate carried the fire east in an iron pot, and Radomir's new hall was lit from it. In 1064 the Markish and Latinate lords of the eastern march, kingless since Lotharic's death, met the Vesari princes in a moot of sabres and took Radomir for their king, since his was the only hall between the middle march and the grass with swords behind it. The two countries have been one kingdom since, **Virelia**, and its kings keep a hearth lit from the Sanctum's altar while their own first fire burns in Veridun.

The **Virelian Lamp** still burns in Veridun's cathedral. Veridun tends it as an honoured hostage, proof in its telling that the Fire came to the east's lamp under Veridun's roof. Virelia's hierarchy went to the Sanctum and its village piety stayed close to Veridun: icon-corners in Vesari farmhouses, standing vigils the parish priests pretend are local custom, and a suspicion in the villages that the fire-keeper was right and the prince was clever. The kindling turned the Lamp Controversy into the public **Kindling Question**. The Fire had come unbidden to an unbaptised house's lamp, lit by no priest and in no rite. Veridun taught that the Fire consents to dwell where sanctity prepares it, and could not say who had prepared a heathen hearth-lamp. The Sanctum taught that grace given to mortals is entrusted to the apostolic body, and had recorded it answering in hands that belonged to no church. Each held half of an answer it could not use.

## The crown goes into the vault, 1068–1104

Radomir's son, Radomir II, won his throne in a war with his own kin. The houses that lost it were old Vesari maternal households that painted their ancestors' faces above their door lintels and kept the district grain, and they demanded restitution through the duties of Strazgorod's ancient protector image, which had guarded a city gate, recognised those houses and opened a famine store in bad years. Among them a rival image grew, called the Elder Face. Between 1068 and 1102 Radomir II's officers burned the faces above the lintels, executed keepers and witnesses, took children from the named houses and renamed them, and forbade one kin-name, which is still not spoken. The protector image came back with a harder, military face, and the famine store it had once opened never opened again. Virelian chroniclers call the purge resistance to pagan sedition; Vesari songs make every victim an ancestral saint; and in that country nobody paints a face above a lintel. [[The Hunger Wars]]

Then came the **Two Winters**, 1101 to 1103: two winters with no summer between, under ash-grey skies, with no eruption in any record and the Thalmyric volcanoes conspicuously quiet. Famine followed. Flagellants walked the roads, and three heresies rose and failed. The Grey Brethren walked to the watershed to ring the border bells by hand and answer them. The Debtors of the Lake, Markish penitents, went south to drown themselves in Wolfsmere for Adalmar's debt, and were turned back at the shore by the Lakekeepers, who told them the lake did not want more. The Unkindlers put out every lamp in their villages to starve the false fire and wait for the true one.

The famine decided who ruled. In Virelia the districts the purge had emptied could not feed themselves; Radomir II's sons died of the famine-fever, and in 1103 the Council of Sabres, which had chosen the house, chose again and elected a king. The moot-men argued that a house whose own fire burned in another's hall could not pass its seat by blood alone, and the Radomirs' first fire was in Veridun. Veridun offered to send the Virelian Lamp home if the "eastern candidate", the choice of the old Vesari river country, won. He lost by three sabres, and the offer has been made at every Virelian election since. Vesari lawyers point out that a fire handed back from another's hall is a lent fire, so only a king willing to be Veridun's client could take it.

In Aurelia the kings of Aldwin's line had kept the Crown of Embers unworn for eighty-three years, each of them examined at his accession and each recorded silent. In the ash-snow of 1102 the lords of the east met at Drossfeld to find one head for the famine, and asked King **Ermbold** to carry the crown barefoot to the Sanctum and stand his examination. Ermbold refused. "It is a hat, not a relic," he said, and he sent it to the Primarch in a grain cart, under the sacks, with word that the sacks were worth more. Aurelia remembers him as Ermbold the Idle, who would not walk a mile for an empire, and he died in his bed as its king. In 1103 came the finest harvest in a century, which farmers named **the Apology**, a name whose theology no church endorses and every farmer uses. In 1104 the kings and dukes swore to leave the Crown of Embers where Ermbold had sent it, in trust at the Sanctum Divinitatis "until the realm is worthy of one head", and the claim passed from Adalmar's blood to whoever the Sanctum should judge worthy. The Primarchy's partisans dated the Apology from the day the crown went into the vault.

## Saints and sinners of the Split

The age's chronicles name more saints than its councils recognised and more sinners than its rulers admitted.

- **Hedwen of Kaltfurt**, the miller's widow under the kindled banner, venerated by millers and in Markish reform country, which reads her struck-through testimony as the first protest against the Sanctum's examinations. She refused to carry the banner south into the Folk Wars.
- **Ioan of Velia**, the potter-monk who answered the Breakers with forty sayings stamped on the feet of clay lamps, and rebuilt his kilns each time they pulled them down.
- **Varkas of Anavra**, the lamp-sergeant who broke the first dark lamp on a bishop's doorstep. Preachers still argue whether he asked an honest question or taught a country to put God to the test.
- **Arsen of the gate guard**, who broke the seal on the Veridun lamp-house with his mother watching; the palace guard still swears its captains at that door.
- **Zdena of Strazgorod**, the heathen fire-keeper in whose hands a cold lamp lit itself, whom Virelian villages count a saint and both churches leave off their calendars.
- **The Saint of the Two Ferries** and **the saint of the enemy gate at Grauwacht**, both claimed by rival churches and remembered by porters and ferrymen for carrying enemies.
- **Theodosia the Kindler**, who walked to the Ember with the families and came back with a list of their dead.
- **Leovar III**, who broke his own lamp, hooded the one he could not bear to see fail, and died asking to look under the hood; **Leovar IV**, who took the empire's lamps into his keeping to protect them and found his own cold on the first shelf; **Kallion, the Master of Lamps**, burned before Meliton; **Makarios the Adamant**, who nailed a lamp shut against two churches and an emperor; **Bonifac III**, who struck out a dead widow's word to make an emperor; **Adalmar**, Eclessia's greatest hero and the drowner of Wolfsmere; **Weylan Oath-Bent**, who carried the safe-conduct to the lake; and **the Judge Beneath the Black Eaves**, called a perfect prophet, a mad judge, a murdered reformer and a damned soul by different towns.

Fuller Lives as the chronicles tell them are collected in [[Saints and Sinners of the Chronicles]].

## The third party

The Freeflame movement of the later Reformation rejects both custody claims outright: the Flame kindles wherever sanctity gathers and *keeps no landlord*, neither a theophanic dynasty nor a protocol monopoly. Two generations into the Reformation, the two old churches privately hate the Freeflame worse than each other. Veridun and the Sanctum disagree about who holds the Fire, but they agree completely that *someone* must.

## An unfinished inheritance

By 1104 the two crowns had become one crown in Thalmyria and one crown in a box. Thalmyria's emperors ruled a recovered heartland whose lamps had been broken by its own villages, locked away until they died in the crown's keeping, and relit from a fire its enemy had kept, all but the one in the cathedral that nobody had been allowed to see. Eclessia's kings ruled realms frozen along the truce lines of one afternoon, under a Sanctum that held the empire's crown and would release it, in practice, only when Veridun submitted to examination. The age led into [[High Noon and the Qarath Disaster]].
