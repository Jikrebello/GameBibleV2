# The Reclamation of Caleran

The Caleran campaign of 950-962 AR recovered the old Atherian capital and opened the central lowlands. It was the sacred midpoint of the Long Reconquest. The city was taken by siege, by a revolt that began at a funeral, and in the end by a fight over a lamp: its Covenant amir, Durayd ibn Sahl, tried to carry the Eternal Ember off to buy his way home, and the Ember's own Covenant keepers held it against him. The dockers burned his ships, the keepers made the terms on which the city opened, and Durayd ended his life as Thalmyria's prisoner, signing away the garrisons that were sworn to him.

## The city the army came for

By 950 Caleran had been occupied for forty-five years. Its garrison families had been born there, its estates along the Aurin were worked by contract-bound survivors, and its port fed a regime that owed Iqashar little more than a name in the Friday prayers. When Sultan-Caliph Idris died in 931, every oath sworn to him had lapsed, and Durayd had sworn the southern cities and garrisons to himself as Amir of the Southern Threshold. Iqashar refused him engines unless he gave those oaths back to its throne. He would not, and so he held the old capital with garrison troops, walls and whatever he could buy.

The city's fear gathered round its funerals. Bound people's names sat in the occupation's name vaults, and the custodians seldom made the release entry Covenant law required at a death, because a released debt-name was a debt written off. Caleran's parish clergy taught that a held name might hold a soul. In 948 Durayd offered to sell the Eternal Ember back to Veridun for the price of its account; Caedros I's court refused to acknowledge that he had anything to sell.

## The Descent

Highland armies moved along repaired Atherian roads while coastal and local resistance disrupted occupation supply. Holy Flame in war-lamps first kindled reliably in this campaign, carried by hymns, sanctified lamps and bearers, and no two companies could promise the same results.

The timing was the Thalmyric army's great fortune. In 951 Grass-Tarkhan riders broke through the Covenant's northern wells in the raid season Tarkhan memory calls the Long Reach, and Iqashar's field army spent the whole campaign on the grass, a season's march from Caleran. Thalmyric chroniclers later claimed Caedros had arranged it. Tarkhan genealogists deny that any envoy drank at their fires.

## The speaking engine, 956

Iqashar sent Durayd one thing. In 955 its last Slave-Soul engine came south by sea, crewed by men sworn to the Covenant throne and not to the amir. It reached Caleran's outer works in 956 and met the war-lamps. The lamps opened its name-cage. People of the occupied parishes, watching from the walls, said the frame cried out once in a Caleran voice, then knelt where it stood and did not rise. Thalmyric clergy said a prisoner had gone on to judgement, and Caleran families believed he was one of the Second Crusade's captives whom the old court had never ransomed, condemned to the foundry before the Fall; the Thalmyric name "martyr irons" comes from that belief. The Covenant crew said only that the cage had failed. The parishes that saw it remembered that the Flame had come for one of their own.

Why the Fire answered a refugee army when it had failed the old palace was argued at once. Thalmyric preachers said it had left the palace for the refuge with the hermits and hospital keepers who never stopped kindling; Covenant jurists, who had kept a file of the old court's counterfeit kindlings for two centuries, had believed it gone altogether.

The outer works themselves cost the besiegers dearly. Among the commanders who threw companies against them was the Warden of the Last Redoubt, who had once sealed a gate on fleeing families to save a royal banner, and whose men now died in assaults that seemed meant to prove he was no coward. His later forts, measured from their smallest doors, were his penance. [[G-35 - The Child's Cord at the Redoubt|The Child's Cord at the Redoubt]] tells how.

## Caleran, 957 AR

The city fell after a combined siege and a revolt within the occupied districts. The revolt began at a funeral. The year before, the occupied parishes had heard a Caleran voice cry out of an iron frame; in the spring of 957 a custodian refused the release entry for a bound dockworker, and the mourners carried the coffin into the custodian's court. The quarter rose behind them, and the next quarter rose behind it.

With no army coming from the north and the parishes in the streets, Durayd decided to go home, and he could not go home with nothing after twenty-six years of defying Iqashar. In the second week of the revolt he sent his household guard to lift the Ember from the basilica of the Celestial Forum and carry it to his ships. The keepers barred the doors against him. They were the third generation of Covenant keepers, born in the occupation, and their own names stood pledged that the lamp would be neither divided, removed nor put out. The guard broke the doors and fought the keepers at the lamp, and eleven keepers died on the basilica floor before the funeral crowds and the deacons came up from the quarters and drove the guard out. The lamp was never moved. The rising had begun at a docker's funeral, and that night the bound dockers of the harbour quarter burned the amir's galleys at the mole. Durayd shut himself in the harbour fort with his household and his treasury and shut the garrison families out.

At dawn the parishes opened the river gate. The Thalmyric army came in through streets the revolt already held, and found the forum precinct closed and the surviving keepers on its steps. They would not open it until the army's heralds heard their terms. Horn players on the walls sounded the three broken notes, and the keepers' terms named the protected before anything else: the lives of the garrison families their amir had shut out, the safety of the surrendered quarters, and the discharge of the Ember's account on payment. They left one man outside the protection: their amir, as the thief of a deposit. The harbour fort held for nineteen days, until its cistern fouled and Durayd's own guard opened the gate. Iqashari chroniclers say he tried to steal the one thing in Caleran that was not his. The occupation-born families who later went north said Iqashar had abandoned him first.

Thalmyric troops found the Eternal Ember still burning under Covenant guard, accompanied by fifty-two years of fuel and custody records and the blood of its keepers on the floor around it.

Veridun paid the invoice in full. The payment acknowledged neither ownership nor gratitude; it acknowledged that the occupation had preserved what it could not extinguish. Covenant jurists add that it released the keepers' pledged names, and they cite it as proof that even the People of the Lamp honour a deposit.

Caedros I entered the martyrs' precinct in the former imperial forum and died at the Pillar of Concordance. Witnesses reported that the White Flame descended. No body remained. Thalmyria glorified him within a decade. Eclessian relic courts reject the evidence for his canonization.

## Completion, 957-962 AR

Caedros II secured the city, coast road, and immediate river districts. He rebuilt walls, protected surrendered quarters, and made Caleran the Patriarchal and pilgrimage centre. The returners cut the words of Sarion the Pale, the march monk stoned in the Forum during the last emperor's Jubilee, onto a memorial wall: *"The inked ones are coming. They walk with chains and offers of peace. The crown will burn in silence."* The imperial court remained at Veridun because the return war was not over and the refuge institutions could not simply be uprooted.

Caedros II neither ransomed Durayd nor tried him. The amirs of the other occupied districts had sworn their garrisons through him, and he was kept in a walled house at Veridun for the fourteen years he had left, signing releases. An amir who came to terms received his garrison's release in Durayd's own hand and could open his gates without breaking faith. An amir who would not had to swear his garrison to himself, in defiance of both empires. By 962 the heartland beyond the coast was a patchwork of released towns and separately sworn amirates. The next ninety years of the Reconquest were fought over the ones that held out, mostly at the canal heads in the hills that watered their fields. Durayd died at Veridun about 971. Iqashari registers strike him from the roll of the south's amirs and enter him among its spoils.
