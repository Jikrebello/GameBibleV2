# The House of Veloran

The House of Veloran is the name later historians gave to the century of the unpainted emperors, 245 to 151 BR. Samor Veloran had covered the speaking portrait at Caleran and refused to let the painters put his face on it, and for four reigns no emperor's features went onto the board. An emperor of this century was agreed by the divided commands of the river army and then made in the open, standing under an arch while loaded wagons crossed above him. Three of the four were Samor and his descendants. The fourth, who reigned between them, was the last child of the old painted house, whom Samor had raised as a hostage. Their roads, eastern campaigns and municipal settlements extended Atherian institutions while increasing the labour and revenue demanded from provincial communities.

## Samor I, 245–229 BR

Samor entered Caleran in 245 over a span his own wardens had thrown across the water gate, and stood beneath it while the first grain wagons crossed. He paid soldiers, confirmed officers and destroyed the remaining military organisation of his rivals. Daran Thalion's surviving supporters were screened household by household: some entered Samor's service, while others were executed or dispossessed.

His first law concerned the accession portrait in the audience hall, which had spoken in the Emperor's voice during the vacancy and which he had covered with his cloak. The Face, as Caleran called it, was to stay veiled except at accessions and on the founding anniversary, and questioning it became a capital offence. Custody passed to the First Celebrant of the Common Sky, whose college had refused the rites Avaren Mott performed before it, and away from the priests of the Red Star. The Celebrants of the Veil became a lasting rival to the Red Star's priests at court, while the copies of the imperial face in governors' courts and army standards stayed in the Red Star's keeping.

He would not be painted. The painters who came to put his face over Olarion's were sent away, and no heir's board went out to the companies in his reign. With the board covered, something else had to make an emperor, and Samor, a warden by training, used the rite he trusted. A work was loaded before the Red Star and its weakness found before anyone relied on it, and the man who opened it stood under it. He had stood under the water-gate span in the sight of the river quarter. He ordered that every emperor after him should be agreed first and then do the same, under an arch of his own opening, before the Face was unveiled to him. Soldiers called it being made under the arch.

Samor reorganised the central river army into commands supplied from different districts. No single commander would control every approach to Caleran, and no successor could be agreed without all of them. The arrangement made another rapid seizure more difficult and required greater coordination from the palace.

His only son had died fighting Halven's fleet at the river ports, leaving a child, Orent, born after his father's death. Samor's daughter Tessa managed correspondence and supply for her father's household and never married. The old house had left one child, Aren Soreth, ten years old in 245 and a great-grandson of Cassara. Samor took him into the river camp as a ward and as a hostage against the western districts that had risen for Daran. Aren grew up among the wardens, loading spans and standing under them.

## Aren I, 229–203 BR

Samor died in 229. His grandson Orent was seventeen and had never held a command. The river commanders met at the Aurin camp, as Samor's law required, and could agree on none of their own number. They agreed on the hostage. Aren Soreth was twenty-six. He had served in every one of their districts and belonged to none of them, and he carried the blood the western veterans still honoured. He stood under the new span of the camp bridge, the Face was unveiled last, and the house Samor had displaced came back to the audience hall in the person of a man Samor had brought up. Tessa Veloran kept the court's correspondence under him as she had under her father, and the two worked together for twenty years.

Aren's government built bridges and cisterns, supported survey schools and negotiated agreements setting the obligations of provincial towns. Tessa brought reports of shortages from municipal households into court correspondence. Aren heard governors and town representatives together before confirming assessments.

The works brought clean water and shortened journeys. They also required stone, timber and compulsory labour. Aren punished theft by contractors while defending the assessments that supplied them. Provincial songs remember both the reopening of routes and the people who died building them.

In 219 BR, the general Cavar Oren seized the [[The Crown of Olveth|Crown of Olveth]] from a mountain shelter and tried to wear it during a victory procession. The object killed him and burned several attendants. Aren returned it after army specialists confirmed the limited conditions under which it responded.

Aren was the first emperor of the old blood whose face was never painted, and he kept Samor's rule. He left no child.

## Orent I, 203–174 BR

At Aren's death the commands agreed quickly on Orent Veloran, Samor's grandson and Tessa's nephew, now forty-three, who had governed eastern districts and cultivated ties among harbour councils. He stood under the first arch of the eastern trunk road.

Early in his reign, around 200 BR, came the Sleepwalkers' Summer. For one season the people of three provinces rose from their beds at night and walked, harmlessly and all, the wardens' records insist, in the same direction. Families tied children to bedposts and woke on the road beside them. Orent's surveyors followed the trampled line, and the empire built a road on it, the Via Somnia, which is still in use and still has oddly good footing. The walking stopped everywhere on the same morning. The road ends at an unremarkable hillside that has since been dug four times by three civilisations and found, each time, to contain dirt. The augurs said that a summons had been withdrawn. Later the Church claimed the summer as a prefiguration of its pilgrim roads, and Vorthara keeps a standing prize for anyone who can explain it, which nobody has collected.

His eastern campaigns secured the Arcelon corridor and several mountain approaches. Vashar's Dawn Road let its relay riders and reserves cross the eastern country faster than any Atherian column, and Atherian soldiers still sang the stations at which that road had outrun Darion Velsar. Orent wanted a road of his own. Commanders destroyed settlements that supported resistance and moved captives to road works. The capital's account celebrated a connected route and the end of raiding. [[The Two Roads of Arcelon]] preserves the provincial knowledge and deaths removed from that history, including the embankment that passed its test in the dry season and slid onto the labourers in the rains.

Orent enjoyed hunting and maintained preserves near provincial residences. Villages lost access to woodland they had used for fuel and grazing. He also employed local trackers and sponsored studies of animals and plants whose copies circulated through imperial schools.

## Orent II, 174–151 BR

Orent II, the son of Orent I, was agreed without dispute. He inherited an experienced administration and fewer active wars. His reign encouraged municipal building, travel and scholarship. Tax relief after two regional crop failures helped towns preserve seed and repair water systems.

The Arcelon road changed the eastern map in his reign. Raveth's river towns had paid canal duty to the Vasharan road command at Avesh, but their trade now ran west along the new road, and late in Orent's reign their councils negotiated entry into the empire. Atheria took over the river dues, stationed troops at the landings and fixed the province's first assessment in a good year.

His elder sister Mariel supported public disputations in which advocates from several philosophical schools addressed the same practical question. Their contests attracted visitors and comic imitation. Provincial teachers gained patronage, though writers who attacked the imperial household could still lose posts or face exile.

Orent had no surviving child and died in the fever summer of 151 before the commands had agreed on anyone to follow him. The principal commanders declined to fight. Samor's divided river commands meant none of them held every approach to Caleran, the fever had filled their camps with sick men, and the veiled Face could not be uncovered for anyone until the others agreed. The empire waited through the summer with nobody to stand under an arch. The answer came from Sarqen, where the spared towns laid their boards out of season; [[The Last Dynasties of the Age of Glory]] tells how.

## The empty seat at Sarqen

None of the four crossed to Sarqen for the spring renewal more than once or twice. The governors swore, as they always had, and the towns, who would leave no seat empty, filled the emperor's place each year with whoever they chose: a ferryman's widow, a salt-boiler, once a boy who had pulled a carter's child out of the harbour. Caleran thought it a provincial joke. The towns kept a list.

## Inheritance

Later histories called the Veloran period the Peace of the Open Roads. Merchants, students and officials travelled those roads in its calmer decades; frontier households, conscript labourers and families moved off campaign ground had built them, and their songs remember the building. A warden's grandson and a hostage had sat in the audience hall, and many more provincial families began to imagine their own there.
