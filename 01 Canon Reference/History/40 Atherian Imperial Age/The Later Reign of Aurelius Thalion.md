# The Later Reign of Aurelius Thalion

Aurelius Thalion ruled the Atherian Empire from its settlement around 350 BR until his death in 318 BR. He had to disband armies that had not dared go home for twenty years, govern conquered Sarqen under the oath he had sworn at its landing, and persuade cities that one permanent command would cost them less than a dozen competing ones. The institutions later associated with his name took shape during these decades.

## The great discharge

For twenty years the Red Field had stood open and no commander had dared disband an army. Thalion disbanded nearly all of them in four. Istran Melvo broke up the war fleets, sending useful hulls to public services, military stations and approved merchants. A smaller standing army stayed under continuing pay from the treasury, and its officers answered to the emperor's appointments instead of to the generals who had raised them.

Casvilar's veterans had held their Noon Roll plots for nearly thirty years as soldiers of an open Field, unable to sell them or leave them to a son. Those plots now became their property. The rest needed land. They received the estates of the men written into the register in 367, and farms whose cultivators had fled the fighting. When those families came home they found veterans in their houses. Petitions, resistance and killings followed. Thalion confirmed most grants, because undoing them would have threatened the army on which his rule depended. Several Aurin villages kept two sets of boundary stones for a century, one for the veterans and one for the families who said the land was theirs.

Regular assessments slowly replaced the requisitions of the war years. Municipal councils were told in advance what grain, money, animals and labour they owed. Some households could plan for the first time in a generation. Officials still punished failure, and an assessment often stayed unchanged through a bad local harvest.

## The store-house government

Thalion governed from the store-house above the muster ground, where the Four at the Stores had sat in 367, and his clerks worked among the grain bins and tally boards that had fed the war. The senate kept the city's markets, festivals, water and streets, which was to say the business of Caleran and not of the empire. Thalion never claimed to have restored the republic, and when an orator praised him for it he is said to have asked which part.

Governors received their commands from him and could be removed before a personal following grew around them. The treasury separated military revenue from municipal funds. Road stations carried orders and lodged official travellers. These arrangements varied by province and often rested on older local institutions.

## Sarqen and the landing

Sarqen was the only royal country in the empire placed under a governor. The governor kept the working harbour and appointed his officers beside the town councils. Every spring he came down to the landing, sat at the unranked table among carters and fishwives, ate what was put in front of him and swore the Table Oath, and only then would the towns fill his stores. Esharat and the other towns kept their irrigation, storage and festivals.

Thalion came to the renewal himself whenever he could, which was more often than his council liked. When he could not, the towns would not leave his seat empty and put whoever they chose in it; a governor's dispatch complained that one year it was a ferryman's widow who talked through the whole meal.

Samyra Nerethan lived at Esharat as a guest of the towns until her death in 333 BR. She sat at the renewal every spring, never at a head because the table had none, and for seventeen years she ate at the same boards as Tobal of Hazreth, the rower her officers had left on the basin quay in 351. Her children Talven and Lethia grew up in the town. Talven later accepted an appointment in Sarqen's harbour administration from his cousin Meren. Lethia endowed a house at Esharat for children whose fathers had been taken in the Fourth Collection. Amesar lived out his life in the sanctuary on Ilamar.

## The ruler's household

Thalion did not marry again after Merath's death in the blockade of Sarqen. His sister Evara ran the household, managed audiences and dealt with the petitioners, widows and relatives who crowded the store-house, and her favour could secure an appointment as easily as Ilvara's had. Their mother Lysara raised the children.

Caelor, born in 363 to Thalion's first wife Ilvara, served in provincial administration and was treated as the likely heir. Meren, born in 358 to Merath, had spent his first eight years in the queen's household at Sarqen and came to Caleran speaking the Gateway towns' speech, with an accent the capital's schoolmasters never quite removed. He went back to the landing for the renewal whenever his father did. Thalion's daughter Lysara married into a senatorial house, and her supporters expected her children to take precedence over a half-Gateway son whose mother had been a claimant to a foreign crown.

## The hall that was never tested

The building that fell on Caelor was a new treasury hall on the forum at Caleran, roofed with cedar beams long enough to need a dozen yoke of oxen each. Military engineers had used the Red Star's test since Casvilar's southern wars: they loaded a valuable frame before the priests until a hairline crack showed where it would fail, then built to the crack. Since the Red Star's priests had declared the Field closed for Thalion, they had added a gentler rite for public works, dedicating a finished building and asking their patron that it stand. The treasury hall received that blessing and nothing else. Its roof came down in 326 while Caelor walked the upper gallery with the clerks and the workmen who had built it. Workmen and enslaved labourers died with him, though imperial memorials remembered the prince.

The engineers said aloud what the priests would not: the hall had been asked for strength without a trial. Thalion could not disown the priesthood that had closed the Field for him, so he divided its work. Priests kept the blessings and the victory rites. The trial went to rune-trained engineers, the wardens, with a priest of the Red Star beside them as witness. No one was to enter a public work until the wardens had loaded it past its expected burden and listened for the stone to speak. Every bridge, hall and aqueduct carried a portion built to be broken in the test, which masons called the Star's share. When the wardens had repaired the crack, the official who opened the work stood beneath the arch while the first loaded wagons crossed above him.

Thalion, sixty-four years old and too heavy to walk far, had himself carried beneath the first arch tested under the order. Builders soon told stories of officials who paid a slave or a condemned man to stand under in their place, and of the works that fell on them afterwards. The wardens' rule outlived his dynasty and shaped quarry labour, road building and festival works for the rest of the age.

Caelor died of his injuries in 324. Meren became his father's heir, and the army and the governors recognised him over the next six years. Lysara's party never accepted it gladly.

## Public religion and the painted board

Thalion placed Astaroth's priesthood first in ceremonies of victory and imperial command, because its priests had declared the Red Field closed when the Common Sky's college would not. Seluc Varroth, its First Celebrant, had died on the Salt Field at Velsar's side, and the Celebrant the college chose after him walked second at every festival and kept Varroth's ruling: the Field of 370 had been emptied, never closed. The two priesthoods remained rivals at court for the rest of the age. A blunt practice sword hung on a peg in the store-house through all those years. Belisar Venn had sent it when Thalion asked him to close the Field, and visitors were told whatever story suited the day.

The board that had gone to war in Thalion's place hung at the end of the audience hall. Soldiers who had sworn to the painting for fifteen years were not easily persuaded to swear to the heavy, short-sighted man it represented, and Thalion let them swear to the painting. Coins set a star above the same face. Later emperors were confirmed before the board, each reign painting a new face over the old pose.

## Death and succession

Thalion went to Sarqen for the spring renewal of 318 BR, at seventy-two, against the advice of his physicians and his sister. He died at the table on the landing, between a salt-boiler and a widow from Belqara, before the meal was finished. Imperial accounts called it a feast of concord. The towns said he had died as he had sworn, served by no one's worth.

Meren was with him. He swore the Table Oath at the same table before he sailed home with his father's body, as his mother's family had sworn it for generations. The army and senate had already recognised him, and the first imperial succession passed without open war. Meren inherited a working government, unresolved land claims and a family that understood succession in several ways.
