# The House of Thalion

The House of Thalion was the first imperial house of Atheria. Its rulers were Aurelius Thalion, his son Meren I, Meren's daughter Cassara with her husband Varelan I, and their son Olarion. It governed for slightly more than a century. Through Meren's mother it carried the blood of the Nerethan kings of Sarqen. Its emperors were made in two places: before the painted board in Caleran's audience hall, to which the armies swore, and at the spared towns' unranked table on Sarqen's landing, where Thalion had sworn in 350. For one generation the two makings fell to two different people. The house ended when its last emperor allowed no heir to be painted, and the board answered for itself.

## The board and the table

Thalion's soldiers had sworn for twenty years to a painted board of his face, and he let them go on swearing to it after 350. An heir was made with paint. A second, smaller board with the heir's face went out to the standing companies, who swore to it in the emperor's lifetime, and at the accession the painters put the new face over the old pose on the great board in the audience hall. The camps called it being painted in.

The table was older and belonged to other people. Every spring the governor of Sarqen sat down among carters and fishwives at the landing and swore the Table Oath before the towns would fill his stores. Thalion went himself when he could, and he died there. When an emperor did not come, the towns would not leave his seat empty and put in it whoever they chose. A ruler whom the towns were willing to seat year after year had the grain of the Gateway country behind him.

## Meren I, 318–286 BR

Meren was born at Esharat in 358 BR to Aurelius Thalion and Merath Nerethan, elder sister of Queen Samyra of Sarqen. The queen's officers brought mother and child to her palace that summer, and he spent his first eight years in her household while his father's fleet closed its harbour. Each spring the elders of Esharat looked him over at the renewal of the oath, under the rule that forbade a child for the table. His mother died in the palace in 351. In 350 he sat on the bench at the towns' table between his aunt and a father he had never seen, and went home with his father. His grandmother Lysara raised him at Caleran beside his half-brother Caelor and half-sister Lysara, and the capital's schoolmasters never quite removed his accent.

Caelor's death in 324 made Meren his father's heir, and his face went out to the companies on the small board. At his accession in 318, at the table on Sarqen's landing where his father died, he swore the Table Oath before he sailed home. The towns had known him since he was in arms. He confirmed military pay, retained his aunt Evara's experienced staff and gave his cousin Talven, Samyra's son, who had shared his nursery, a supervised appointment in Sarqen's harbour administration. His half-sister's party, which had expected her children to inherit, never forgave him for being born.

Meren preferred meetings with a small number of prepared speakers. He enjoyed horse races and funded teams from provincial cities, creating a popular circuit whose rivalries sometimes ended in street violence. His government punished organisers of riots while continuing the contests that drew the crowds. The new stands at the Caleran course were among the first public works opened under Thalion's rule of the test: wardens loaded them with sandbags until a cross-beam split, the carpenters replaced it, and the city prefect sat alone in the front row through the first race before anyone else was allowed in.

He reduced several emergency levies and confirmed town charters. Southern campaigning combined treaties with a succession of short punitive expeditions. Enslaved captives and confiscated land remained sources of reward.

Meren had one child who lived, his daughter Cassara. She supervised the Aurin stores from her twenties. When his legs began to fail she crossed to Sarqen each spring in his place, and the towns seated her without being asked. In 292 Meren had her face painted on the heir's board. The pose on every board was the founder's, a heavy man in a red-bordered mantle, and the painters set a young woman's face on his shoulders. The river companies swore to it. The standard-bearers of two southern commands would not carry it, and their soldiers stood behind them. Meren had grown up as a hostage and disliked forcing anyone. He let the matter lie for three years and then gave way. In 289 the painters put the face of Cassara's husband on the small board instead. Varelan Soreth was a harbour-supply officer from a port family, competent and well liked, and nobody had thought of him as an emperor, himself included. Meren died in 286.

## Cassara and Varelan, 286–263 BR

The armies swore to Varelan's face and the painters put it on the great board, and he stands in the lists as Varelan I. At Sarqen that spring the towns laid the boards and seated Cassara, as they had for six years, and she swore the oath. The elders said they had promised in 350 never to seat a Nerethan as queen of the Gateway country, and that an empress of Atheria was another matter. For twenty years the empire had an emperor of the board and an empress of the table, and it was fortunate that they were married and liked each other.

Cassara kept the stores, the grain contracts and the spring crossing to Sarqen, which made her the court's source of information about shortages. Varelan, who had served in harbour supply and held military command, kept the companies and built. He enlarged water storage, harbour granaries and the paved approaches to several provincial capitals. Cassara travelled with the engineers and developed a reputation for asking workers to explain a problem before officials described the solution.

Construction drew labour and material from subject districts. Since Caelor's death every public work carried a Star's share, a portion built to be broken when the wardens loaded it before the Red Star, and Varelan's new aqueduct needed a breaking span in every run of arches. The quarries could not cut the extra stone in the working season. In 274 BR the managers extended compulsory service through the harvest, and the quarrymen struck rather than leave their fields unreaped to cut "stone for the Star to break". Troops killed strikers and their families were expelled from housing attached to the works. Cassara pressed for the removal of two managers whose conduct endangered the supply programme. Varelan dismissed them, kept the compulsory labour assessment and ordered the aqueduct completed. He also kept the test. The court said that a house which had buried an heir under an untested hall would not open an untested aqueduct; the quarry villages said that the house had found someone else to pay for its fear.

The imperial household supported musicians, astronomers and comic performers. Provincial artists acquired commissions at Caleran and brought imperial styles home. Cassara died in 266 and Varelan in 263. Their son Olarion had been painted in since boyhood and had eaten at the landing beside his mother.

## Olarion, 263–247 BR

Olarion was the last emperor to hold both makings, and he let one of them go. He swore at Sarqen at his accession and never crossed again, and the towns filled his seat each spring with whoever they pleased. He inherited a court rich in honours and suspicious of any commander who acquired independent popularity. He enjoyed mechanical birds, private performances and elaborate night processions through the palace. Craftspeople produced ingenious works for him while treasury officials struggled to meet the cost.

He made the great board the centre of his court. His guards knelt to it at every change of watch. Petitions were read aloud before it, the clerks of the Aurin stores read their tallies in its sight, and officials renewed their loyalty in its presence each year. His informers swore their denunciations before the same painted face. Families learned to fear being named in the hall more than being named in a court.

He would have no second face. No heir's board was painted in his reign, and a painter who sketched one for a cousin's household was among those denounced. Olarion ordered the deaths of two relatives after accusations that their household rites concealed a succession gathering. Confiscations rewarded informers and drove other families towards frontier armies willing to shelter them. Aster Halven, admiral of the eastern fleet, was summoned to renew his oath before the board. He sent it by letter and stayed with his ships. Olarion had no other fleet, and Halven was still at sea when the emperor died.

The founder's reputation did not trouble him. The history Hadram had finished on Ilamar, which called Thalion a man who had other men killed for him, was read in Caleran's schools throughout the reign, and Olarion was said to enjoy it. The face on the board was now his own, and the dead man under the paint was no concern of his.

Food shortages at Caleran in 252 brought demonstrations. The palace released grain while soldiers killed people attempting to enter a closed store. Olarion's subsequent games quieted the city without restoring the families of the dead.

In 247 the emperor was found dead on the floor of the audience hall, in front of the board. Accounts variously blamed illness, poison and a frightened attendant. No explanation became conclusive. No heir had been painted in. Commanders in three provinces raised their own standards before the funeral rites were complete, and on the second night of the mourning the portrait before which Olarion's informers had sworn began to answer questions in the Emperor's voice. [[The Painted Reign]] follows the war and the portrait.

## Dynastic memory

Later rulers retained Thalion's name within their titles and represented Meren and Varelan as peaceful continuators; they seldom mentioned Cassara's seat. Olarion became the wicked relative against whom the wisdom of the others could be displayed. His government nevertheless used officers, tax arrangements and ceremonies built by his predecessors. The house left three habits that outlived it: the wardens' test of every public work; the portrait cult that grew from the founder's painted war board and answered in 247; and the spring meal at Sarqen's landing, where every governor of the province sat at the unranked table and swore the towns' oath, and where the towns went on filling the emperor's seat with whoever they chose.
