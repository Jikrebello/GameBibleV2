# The Two Roads of Arcelon

The Two Roads of Arcelon are the imperial and provincial accounts of a route built after Orent I's eastern campaign, approximately 190–182 BR. The physical road was one undertaking. Two bodies of song preserved the different knowledge needed to maintain it and incompatible memories of the victory that made its construction possible.

## The campaign

Arcelon connected an eastern market basin to mountain passages and an inner-sea port. Local toll houses and armed escorts controlled movement before the imperial campaign. Some communities welcomed an agreement promising safer travel; others resisted the garrisons and assessments attached to it.

General Vara Deneth defeated the principal coalition after taking its winter stores. One settlement continued to shelter fighters and their families. Her troops burned it, killing inhabitants and forcing survivors into flight. Captives were assigned to the road works alongside conscripted provincial labourers.

The court history described an empty robber stronghold and a road opened for peaceful exchange. Its marching song gave distances, gradients and the seasons in which pack animals could climb particular sections. It omitted the civilian settlement and the bodies beneath a collapsed embankment.

## The second song

Workers composed another sequence while cutting drains, moving stone and carrying water. Its verses named springs, unstable slopes and the places where supervisors had refused to stop work. One verse described labourers crushed after rain loosened an embankment. Another named a child who carried messages between the workers and the displaced settlement.

Officials discouraged the song because it accused the victory army of murder and forced labour. Road crews continued singing it at night. Families along the corridor learnt the verses concerning water because they accurately predicted which culverts would fail.

## Repair

A generation later, a major section washed out. Engineers possessed the official distances and grades but lacked the workers' account of groundwater and unstable soil. Local crews refused to begin until both songs were performed.

The combined knowledge enabled repairs. Thereafter, maintenance gatherings alternated verses: the bright marching measure followed by the lower work song. The practice preserved useful information and forced officials to hear the costs excluded from the public victory.

## Surviving tradition

Modern travellers sometimes speak of two roads, although archaeology shows a single principal route repeatedly rebuilt. “Taking the second road” means learning what a successful account has omitted.

The songs disagree over names and the number of dead. Burned foundations, graves and the construction sequence establish the settlement's destruction and lethal labour. Many victims remain unnamed.
