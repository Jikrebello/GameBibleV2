# The Last Dynasties of the Age of Glory

The last century and a half before the Redeemer was governed by the Serrat emperors, whom the spared towns of Sarqen seated at their table before any army agreed to them, and by two shorter court houses. It was the world into which Isfir was born in 33 BR. Four long pressures shaped it. Citizenship spread through the eastern cities, each new citizen swearing before the emperor's image. A famine in Raveth turned free farmers into dependants of a vast crown estate. Copies of the emperor's face multiplied in town halls and governors' courts while the original hung veiled at Caleran. On the eastern frontier, a Vasharan civil war let Atheria take the Jirahar sanctuary of Qiryath under a charter whose clauses still governed the city when Isfir came to it.

The empire that resulted was old, rich and confident. Its roads carried grain and governors to the eastern towers, its courts knew how to hear the sanctuary's cases, and its provincial portraits had never been hungrier.

## The fever summer and the vacancy of 151

In the summer of 151 BR a fever came up the Aurin from the harbours. It filled the river camps with sick men, emptied streets in the lower city and killed Orent II, who had no surviving child and had named no heir. Crowds carried their sick to the doors of the audience hall and begged the Celebrants of the Common Sky to lift the cloth from the Face, the veiled accession portrait that had once spoken in the Emperor's voice. The Celebrants kept the doors shut.

For some months the empire had no way to make an emperor. Samor Veloran had divided the river army a century earlier so that every approach to Caleran answered to a different district command, each fed from different country; a commander who marched first would march without the others' grain, and this summer half of them were burying soldiers. Samor's law required the commands to agree before anyone stood under an arch, and the Face could not be unveiled until they had. Old people in the poor districts still told how the portrait had been carried ahead of the soldiers in the searches, and senators whose great-grandfathers had sat with Avaren Mott did not want to be seen near it. So the commanders talked, and the fever went on.

The answer came from a table. The governor of Sarqen that year was Elian Serrat, the son of an eastern council household, who had come down to the landing nine springs running and sworn the spared towns' oath among the carters. In the autumn of 151 he asked the towns for grain for the fever camps on the Aurin. The elders answered that they filled stores for a governor sworn that spring in an emperor's name, that the emperor was dead, and that he had better swear again. They laid the boards at the landing out of season, for the first time since 350. The emperor's seat stood empty, which their oath forbade. For a century they had filled it with ferrymen's widows and salt-boilers. This time they put the governor in it. Hostile writers said Elian had bought the seat with a year's harbour dues. The towns said he was the first Atherian since Meren who had eaten with them often enough to be known.

The eastern cities were richer than ever and untouched by the fever. Raveth's river towns had lately joined the empire after the Arcelon road pulled their trade west, and the eastern councils handled grain, tolls and garrison contracts on two frontiers. They took the seat at Sarqen for a signal and named their price for standing behind it: citizenship for the households that ran their councils, courts and roads. The river commanders, sick and short of bread, agreed to a man who could feed them. Elian stood under an arch of the Aurin camp bridge and kept the Veloran municipal advisers. The Face was unveiled last, before a ruler already agreed. The commanders had made one condition of their own.

## The grant and its oath

Elian I ruled from 151 to 126. He kept his promise early and shaped it to his needs and to the commanders' condition. Citizenship went to officers, teachers and municipal families able to meet public obligations: the people who would sit on councils, collect Raveth's new assessments, keep the Arcelon stations and pay for festivals the treasury could no longer fund. Each new citizen swore before the emperor's image in a governor's court or town hall, so that the grant bound its holders to the office and to no eastern council or provincial commander. An emperor made at an unranked table had every citizen he made swear before a picture.

Jirahar scribes and interpreters, a large part of the class Elian needed, would not swear before an image. The eastern negotiators won them an exception on the precedent of Samor's veil: they swore with the copy covered. The veiled oath, as it was called, made citizens of houses that would serve the eastern chancery for generations, among them the scribal house of Qoreth.

Townsmen who had walked in the same processions but could not meet the obligations watched their neighbours enter the courts and the contracts. Every town that wanted standing commissioned a better portrait for its hall, and painters' workshops in the eastern cities did well for fifty years.

## Raveth: the hunger and the loan before the Face

Raveth's river towns had paid canal duty to the Vasharan road command at Avesh before they joined the empire, and under that duty every household sent men each spring to clean the flood channels. Their first imperial assessment was fixed in a good year. The new road levy took the same men for the Arcelon stations, and the channels silted. Two harvests failed. The third was fair, and estate managers shipped it out under contract. Then blight took the fields. From 119 BR the province starved.

Elian II, who had succeeded his father in 126 after serving in the eastern administration, gave the relief to Neriah bar Sorek, a Jirahar store examiner who carried the measures of three provinces in his head. The emperor's sister Lethara, who managed family estates and food contracts, had brought the estate stewards' accounts to court. Neriah bought grain, requisitioned boats, opened military stores and had every release countersigned by local witnesses. The treasury would release nothing as a gift, so relief went out as debt, owed by each village together and written on tallies of which the village kept a copy. The boats reached the river towns first; hill villages died waiting for the pack trains, and soldiers killed a crowd trying to force one store.

Neriah meant the debt to be worked off in the flood channels, a season's digging for a season's grain, which would have mended what had caused the hunger. Lethara's stewards wanted the land. When the worst had passed they persuaded Elian that only a single estate could repair the channels, and Lethara's officers took over the relief stores. They set aside the villages' countersigned tallies and had each householder swear, alone, before the emperor's image in the store-house yard, to a debt entered against his own fields. Neriah would not administer that oath. He resigned, and before he left he had copies of every release account carried to the villages that had countersigned them. Raveth noticed the difference within a season. Where villages held the Keeper's tallies, households argued, delayed, went to law and kept part of their land. Where debts had been sworn before the image, families walked their animals to the estate office without being asked and stood waiting at the gate. Villagers called it "the loan before the Face". Relief was wound down by 113, and the crown estate it left covered the eastern basins. [[The Keeper of Borrowed Grain]] follows Neriah and the arguments over his responsibility.

## Cassen's cities and the estate economy

Elian II died in 101. Cassen I, Lethara's son and Elian's nephew and heir, inherited the crown estates and the officials managing them. His reign, 101–76, filled the central cities with new baths and theatres paid for by Raveth's revenue. The estate's dependants answered with unworked labour days, burned seed stores and families who vanished from the rolls overnight.

Raveth's grain reached the garrisons through estate contracts, so towns like Lacaris paid in prices what Raveth had paid in land. When soldiers tried to clear the Lacaris market during a dispute over arrears and bread around 96 BR, the riot that followed set fire to the magistrate's house. Six servants dragged him out through a refuse passage. Their rise from that night is told in [[The Companions Who Entered by the Low Door - Imperial History]].

## The War of the Two Dawns

Late in Cassen's reign the Vasharan crown passed in dispute. A new Vasharan king opened his reign at a dawn audience to which the keepers of Daryam carried the royal flame, an ordinary household lamp tended across generations. That year two claimants held dawn audiences in the same week. The keepers carried the flame to the one backed by the Ruzhan horse houses and left the one who held Kavresh to rise in lamplight; his enemies said his mirrors showed him nations kneeling. Atherian writers called the struggle that followed the War of the Two Dawns. It lasted years. The Vasharan road command at Avesh chose the losing side. The victor purged it, pulled garrisons east to fight in Ruzhan and, needing silver, sold confirmations of offices across the western sanctuaries. Vasharan tribute from the Qahuri crowns lapsed in the same years.

## Qiryath under two empires

In those years the Keeper of Qiryath's sanctuary fell on the cistern stair and died. The city council named an interim Keeper from the tower houses, whose pilgrims now came mostly from Atherian provinces, many of them citizens under Elian's grant. A river-quarter house long in Vasharan road service bought the confirmation of the office from Daryam.

Before the purchaser arrived, the interim Keeper made the pilgrimage offering. The court was full. His offering burned without smoke, and the altar stones went cool under the priests' hands: the sign of acceptance, given as it had been given since the return from exile. The sanctuary's witnesses wrote it down. A month later the purchaser came up the Dawn Road with a Vasharan escort and took the altar.

The tower houses wrote to the Atherian governor at Arcelon, in letters a Qoreth scribe drafted in the chancery's own forms. The governor came to the next pilgrimage with an augur of the Red Star, and the augur proposed what Atherians did with a disputed claim: test it. Let each claimant offer, he said, and let the sign decide. The purchaser refused to make the Lord answer a foreigner's question. The interim Keeper made his offering before the crowd, the governor and the augur, and the court waited. The fire smoked like any fire, and the stones stayed hot. People who were there remembered the silence more than anything said afterwards. Atherian writers called the day the Trial before the Stars; in the towers it was the Governor's Question.

The governor decided on the sanctuary's written record of the earlier sign and on the purchaser's refusal, and the Qoreth scribe drafted the charter that followed. Sacrifice, the communal courts and the burial grounds were protected at Qiryath and in the province's tower towns. No image of any kind would pass the sanctuary gate. The council would choose the Keeper and the governor confirm him, and every day the priests would make an offering on the emperor's behalf. The governor would come up for the pilgrimage, with an augur for the public rites, to hear whatever cases the elders sent him, and only he could put a man to death for an offence against imperial rule. The sanctuary's dues stood surety for the tribute. One copy of the charter went into the sanctuary archive and one to the chancery. The Qoreth house kept the sanctuary copy for three generations and trained its sons in both archives.

The purchaser's house, and the river-quarter families who would not serve under the charter, loaded their scrolls, bedding and cooking pots onto mules and went east by the Dawn Road into Vasharan country. Tower communities there took them in, and they told the story of the Trial before the Stars in their own way: the sign had refused to answer a governor. At Qiryath the sign went on answering the appointed offerings, under the confirmed Keeper and under priests of the defeated houses who stayed. Each side cited it.

Early in Velen I's reign the victor of the War of the Two Dawns, still fighting in Ruzhan, accepted the charter by treaty in return for the Dawn Portion, a share of pilgrim dues paid each year to Avesh. Vasharan scribes wrote "lease"; Atherian scribes wrote "cession".

## Velen and the Face

Cassen's son Velen I succeeded in 76. He trusted a narrow palace circle and removed commanders whose popularity alarmed him. Every emperor since Samor had been made under an arch. Velen stood under his at his accession and never under another, and he let it be known that the Emperor did not stand under. Public games and extravagant river spectacles made him popular in parts of Caleran, and he had the Face unveiled at them so that crowds acclaimed image and emperor together. The temporary banks built for the displays were exempted from the wardens' tests as festival works. When the wardens objected, the palace answered that the works were the Emperor's and the Face was present.

The palace scarcely noticed the treaty with Vashar. At the founding-anniversary spectacle of 61 the Face stood unveiled on the stone terrace above the river with the guard drawn up before it, and Velen sat below among the crowd on the highest of the timber banks. The banks gave way. Workers, spectators and the emperor went into the Aurin together, and Velen's body was taken from the water two days later, a mile downstream. Survivors remembered the guard. Every man of it had sworn before the image, like every guard since Olarion's day, and through the screaming they stood in their ranks on the terrace with their faces to the portrait. Not one of them went down to the water. Velen's relatives divided the available commands and argued.

## Doran and the low door

Doran, governor of the central military district, moved first because the road stores between his district and Caleran and the capital's armour workshops declared for him. Two of the Lacaris servants held them: Miro Fen managed the stores and Brana Keth directed the workshops. Sena Varris, by then a frontier commander, held a crossing against a relative's column. Doran reached Caleran first and was recognised as Doran I.

He ruled from 61 to 42 BR. He had the Face carried back to the audience hall and re-veiled, abolished the festival exemption and confirmed much of the existing administration. He did not punish the terrace guard. Men who had stood there told him they had heard the banks go and had been unable to turn their heads, and he believed them, or found it useful to. He ended the oath before the image instead. A new officer crossed into his command through a low entrance, stripped of insignia and carrying a plain bowl, as the six companions of Lacaris had entered their dining room since the riot. Officers believed the low door kept the image's pull off them; the Celebrants called that superstition. Doran did not stand under an arch himself, and no emperor after him did: the excuse Velen had invented outlived the banks that killed him. Doran's land settlements rewarded the units that supported him. Southern towns supplied farms for some of these grants, displacing households already living there and reopening claims that older surveys had seemed to settle.

## The Othrans

Doran had no son. His officers, who had all come in by the low door, agreed among themselves on Othran, a commander from an eastern provincial house that knew the charters and the husband of Doran's daughter Mira, and the river commands accepted their choice in 42. He was the first emperor who had entered his own command stooping, with a bowl in his hands. Othran I preferred provincial residence, spent long periods inspecting roads and garrisons, and kept away from the audience hall. Caleran officials retained considerable influence during his absences.

Isfir was born in 33 BR during Othran I's reign, in a Jirahar tower town of the eastern provinces whose communal court sat under the charter's protection. The governor's hall in the nearest city held a copy of the emperor's face, and much of the farmland around the town was crown estate. Imperial roads ran past the tower. Jirahar citizens swore with the image veiled. The sign was given each year at Qiryath, and Vashar collected the Dawn Portion and called it rent.

Othran II succeeded his father in 24 BR. He inherited good roads, a professional army and temples sure of their right to speak for the sky. He also inherited crown estates worked by debtors, slave gangs in the quarries and tower towns that kept their courts under a charter and swore their oaths under a veil. His estates fed the cities, his eastern citizens staffed the courts, and lawyers in every province knew that a test answers only the question asked.

## The world of 0 AR

At Qiryath, Ezrahel bar Qoreth, Lord-Scribe and keeper of the sanctuary copy, knew the charter better than any governor. When a teacher from the towers drew crowds at the pilgrimage, everything Ezrahel needed was already in place: a governor sitting for the pilgrimage with an augur of the Red Star beside him, a clause for sending him cases that touched imperial rule, a right of complaint to Caleran, and a burial right under which an elder could ask for a body. The governor's court at Second Qiryath turned a dispute over the sanctuary and Isfir's claims into an imperial capital case. Othran II received the account from his provincial officials after the sentence.

## The threshold of another age

The Redeemer's death and resurrection mark the boundary of the calendar at 0 AR, and Othran II's reign runs across it. In the provinces people went on building houses, marrying, walking to festivals and buying their sons a first post. The empire looked old, adaptable and well able to survive another quarrel in a sanctuary town. The governor's report of the sentence went into the judgement archive at Caleran with the nails in its chest, and nobody there expected to open it again.
