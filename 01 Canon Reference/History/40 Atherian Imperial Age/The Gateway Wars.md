# The Gateway Wars

The Gateway Wars were a succession of maritime and land conflicts between the Caleran republic, its changing allies and the [[The Gateway Republic|Gateway Republic]], approximately 650–520 BR. An initial contest over ports grew into a struggle involving naval finance, dependent communities and the survival of Caleran's alliance system. The final war destroyed the gateway capital and its child-sacrificing establishment while leaving lesser towns to carry the country's subsequent history.

The wars helped make overseas commitments a permanent concern of Caleran's government. They enlarged provincial revenue and the influence of military patrons, imposed repeated service on coastal and river households, and brought enslaved people into Atherian estates and markets. The republic inherited these consequences for generations before the rise of Aurelius Thalion.

## The sea and the alliances

Caleran stood at the mouth of the Aurin on the outer coast. Its forces reached the inner Thalassian through cooperating ports and landward supply routes. Arkenan and Saronikan communities contributed ships, pilots and repair facilities. Some were close allies; others negotiated particular services or accepted obligations after defeat. Shared Atherian culture did not give every port the same political relationship with Caleran.

The gateway capital commanded a cultivated country at the meeting of desert approaches and inner-sea exchange. Its government relied on ships and fortified ports, together with food and labour from dependants inland. Smaller states had interests of their own. A town seeking protection from a neighbour could invite one republic into a dispute that its inhabitants had expected to remain local.

## The first war, c. 650–625 BR

War began after an inner-sea port changed allegiance amid a quarrel between its garrison and resident households. Gateway and Atherian parties claimed to be protecting their partners. Within Caleran, advocates expected commercial access and military distinction; opponents feared the cost of maintaining a distant commitment. Intervention nevertheless expanded as forces occupied neighbouring anchorages.

The Atherians obtained vessels and pilots from cooperating maritime cities. Grain, rope, timber and replacement equipment travelled from inland suppliers to those ports and then to the forces abroad. A garrison could survive defeat ashore if its harbour remained open. Conversely, a victory brought little lasting advantage when its commanders lacked provisions to remain through another season.

Gateway commanders defended fortified positions, raided vulnerable supply and offered concessions intended to separate Caleran from its partners. Both governments imposed further collections as ships and crews were lost. Families expected home after a campaign remained absent, and inland communities supplied grain beyond what their own harvests could comfortably spare.

The war ended after a gateway relief movement failed and the capital could no longer support its distant positions. Caleran obtained access and payments, with some allied ports sharing the advantages. Others continued to bear considerable service with little influence over the settlement. Deaths at sea, damaged communities and the sale of captives were lasting consequences of a victory celebrated through new public honours.

## Revolt over pay and collection, c. 625–619 BR

Defeat left the gateway council unable or unwilling to meet the demands of returning forces. It sought reductions or delays in payment while rural communities refused another round of collection. Foreign soldiers feared discharge without wages; local levies wanted to return home; cultivators sought relief from officials and garrisons. These grievances brought together groups with different ideas of what a settlement should provide.

A negotiator was seized after rival commanders undermined confidence in the offered terms. Rebel leaders tortured and killed prisoners to make reconciliation more difficult. Government forces answered with executions and the killing or enslavement of captured dependants. Each round of violence gave those directing it further reason to fear surrender.

The commander who restored the capital's position depended upon a neighbouring ruler. Assistance carried a price in territory and marriage, establishing relationships that outlived the revolt. Caleran initially benefited from the survival of a government with which it could deal, then exploited its exhaustion by taking an exposed anchorage. Later gateway advocates of war pointed to that seizure as an injury added after peace.

The revolt damaged trust in civic promises and increased the standing of the military household that had ended it. Families also carried more intimate inheritances: unpaid service, captives who never returned and relatives killed by people with whom survivors would again have to trade.

## An overseas military household, c. 619–605 BR

A senior gateway commander received authority to obtain revenue and military access beyond the weakened core country. His following established itself among existing coastal settlements and productive districts of the inner sea. Conquest brought captives and payments, while agreements with local rulers supplied protection, provisions and allies against their own enemies.

Soldiers married into resident communities, and officers developed relationships with suppliers and leading families. A successor from the commander's household retained experienced followers and clients. The capital gained resources while becoming increasingly dependent on a family whose influence had acquired a territorial base abroad.

This recovery created the means for another war. It also complicated relations between the civic government and its commanders. Ending a particular appointment could threaten revenues and alliances which the council had little direct ability to replace.

## The alliance-breaking invasion, c. 600–580 BR

The household's next commander sought to defeat Caleran by breaking its relationships with allied communities. He offered selected towns lighter obligations and relief from recent levies. His army reached a landing on the inner-sea side and moved through coastal and upland approaches into allied country. Friendly ports, pack transport, captured stores and successive harvests sustained the advance.

Caleran's first armies suffered severe defeats while trying to defend allies and force a decisive battle. Some towns changed sides. Others feared the invader's local partners more than they resented Caleran, or doubted that promises made during an advance would survive victory. A fortified community that remained loyal could obstruct supply even after the invader had won a major battle nearby.

A delaying Atherian commander preserved his forces and contested forage. The policy required households beyond immediate protection to endure the destruction of their property. Political opponents demanded a decisive engagement, while allies measured the value of Atherian protection against the damage they could see in their fields.

The war widened. One Atherian command attacked the gateway's overseas base, limiting replacements and revenue for the invading army. Naval forces kept enough allied ports open to support an expedition against the gateway homeland. There, a border ruler joined the Atherians to defeat a competing dynasty and gain land. Local rivalries shaped the invasion's outcome as materially as commands issued in Caleran.

The gateway army eventually withdrew from Atherian allied country. Its commander had inflicted great losses and retained a loyal following, but the invasion had failed to build a coalition able to replace Caleran's. The settlement restricted the gateway republic's fleet and freedom to wage war. The successful Atherian commander returned with prestige that alarmed other senate households.

## Recovery and renewed danger, c. 580–525 BR

Over the following generations, gateway communities repaired cultivated land and revived trade. Public payments continued alongside the demands of former soldiers, returning captives and households with very unequal losses. The neighbouring ruler rewarded by Atheria, and later heirs pursuing their own interests, encroached on disputed districts. Appeals to Caleran received inconsistent support.

Senators differed over the rival's future. Some preferred a prosperous subordinate state capable of meeting its obligations. Others regarded renewed wealth as a resource for another war. Suppliers, commanders and provincial clients assessed peace according to the security and opportunities it offered them.

Within the capital, the sacrificial establishment increased its demands for children as promises of divine protection failed to end these pressures. Leading families sometimes offered their own children; others purchased substitutes or drew upon captive households. Dependent towns bore demands backed by civic authority.

Families resisted by hiding children, moving them among relatives or seeking refuge beyond the republic's control. Escaped temple workers described the killings. Former captives identified the places where children awaited sacrifice. Their testimony gave Atherian opponents of the rite practical knowledge and a constituency among the threatened communities.

## The decision to destroy the capital

An influential advocate in Caleran made ending the sacrifices a central argument for intervention. Several supporters sincerely sought the release of children and protection for the families hiding them. Others wanted secure shipping, land or the permanent removal of a recovered rival. Together they obtained support that neither interest could simply have assumed on its own.

Negotiations demanded the release of captives and the dismantling of the sacrificial institution. The capital's ruling faction protected its priesthood and demanded the return of fugitives. Some lesser towns offered the Atherians provisions and passage in exchange for protection. Their defection weakened the capital and supplied places where threatened families could gather under allied cover.

Caleran's adopted policy distinguished the capital from the lesser towns accepting the settlement. Those communities were to retain their inhabitants and civic life. The capital had to surrender weapons, release captives and accept the destruction of the sacrificial establishment. After an initial surrender, the demands expanded to abandonment of the city itself. Families who had opposed the sacrifices now faced removal with the officials responsible, and resistance drew together people with sharply different motives.

## Siege, rescue and salting, c. 525–520 BR

The final siege involved blockade, shortages, siege works and fighting through inhabited districts. Atherian detachments released children from confinement and destroyed the apparatus of sacrifice. Other soldiers murdered inhabitants, raped captives and seized people for sale. Commanders permitted these acts while pursuing the destruction of the city.

Priests who had ordered sacrifices and officials who had supplied children were captured, executed or driven into flight. Families recovered some living relatives and learned of others' deaths. The bronze-faced provider lost its sanctuary, its organised offerings and the protection of the government that had supplied them. Soldiers broke its image and dismantled the serving chambers.

After the sack, the capital was burned and razed. Salt arrived through the captured harbour from coastal stores and was spread heavily over the temple enclosure, selected residential ground and gardens. Cisterns were spoiled and broken. The labour and transport involved made the salting a deliberate undertaking, remembered as the most conspicuous image of the city's exclusion from the country around it.

Demolished waterworks, displacement and restrictions on rebuilding prolonged the desolation. The salt affected the ground upon which it had been spread; cultivated districts elsewhere continued to produce food. Lesser towns retained wells, workshops and landing places. They had endured requisition, losses and in some cases fighting, but the final policy spared them the capital's planned erasure.

Parents who welcomed the destruction of the sacrificial establishment could also oppose the sale of neighbouring children by the conquerors. Survivors carried both experiences into the lesser towns. The later dynasty sought authority among communities whose memories included rescue, betrayal and refusal to supply victims to the former capital.

## Consequences for the two countries

The spared towns became the material basis of [[The Spared Towns and the Queen's Country|royal recovery]]. Their farms, smaller ports and local relationships sustained the people who remained and those able to return. The destroyed capital's former country acquired a new political centre over several generations.

Caleran gained revenue, military access and wider opportunities for its patrons. Commanders rewarded followers, suppliers enlarged their businesses and captives entered estates and urban households. Repeated naval obligations also strengthened the ability of organised transport owners to negotiate. Nerava's supply dispute arose within these broader pressures while retaining its own quarrel over exemptions and unequal burdens.

These wars left a long interval before the imperial founding. Their importance lay in the resources, expectations and inequalities they helped establish. Later commanders inherited a republic accustomed to distant service, provincial wealth and political competition supported by military followings.
