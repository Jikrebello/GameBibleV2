# The Gateway Wars

The **Gateway Wars** were three wars between the Caleran republic, its allies and the [[The Gateway Republic|Gateway Republic]] of Qarzeth, fought between about 674 and 520 BR. The first began over bread sold at a festival table. The second was begun by a Gateway captain whose own city disowned him, and whose gifts of grain emptied the storehouses of the towns that accepted them; Caleran answered him with a sacrifice on its own festival ground, and that sacrifice brought the whole Gateway into the war. The third began when Qarzeth's assembly surrendered and its god would not. It ended with the Gateway capital starved, broken, burned and salted, its bronze-faced god defeated and its lesser towns sworn to an oath that outlived both republics.

The wars made overseas commitments a permanent concern of Caleran's government. They enlarged provincial revenue and the followings of military patrons, imposed repeated service on coastal and river households, and filled Atherian estates and markets with enslaved people. They also left Caleran with an office it could not close and the Gateway country with two lists of children's names. Both mattered for more than two centuries.

## The sea and the alliances

Caleran stood at the mouth of the Aurin on the outer coast. To fight in the inner Thalassian it needed other people's harbours: Arkenan and Saronikan ports that supplied ships, pilots, rope and repair yards, and landward routes along which grain moved from the Aurin towns to the sea. Some ports were close allies. Others served for a price or because they had lost a previous war.

Qarzeth commanded a cultivated country where desert roads met inner-sea shipping. Its harbour was crowded with ships, but its strength lay in its granaries, which visitors described as the fullest on any coast. Gateway merchants said their god fed the city. Caleran's grain buyers came to believe them.

Smaller states had interests of their own. A town afraid of its neighbour could invite one republic into a quarrel its people had expected to stay local, and then find both republics' armies in its fields.

## The Tammur Tables, c. 674–660 BR

Three springs in succession, around 676–674 BR, the rains failed over the Aurin. The river ran low past Nerava, seed shrivelled in the furrows, and the Compact cities that bought grain abroad found the usual markets already stripped. Caleran was in those years settling its own unpaid sailors on the burned fields of Marenthe, in ships bought cheaply from the unpaid commanders of [[Eryndor III and the Ortheian Eastern Conquest|Eryndor III of Ortheia]]. It had hulls to spare and no grain to put in them. The nearest surplus lay at **Tammur**, an inner-sea market town held by the Gateway Republic.

Qarzeth's factors at Tammur sold grain on one condition: the buyer first ate at the Provider's festival. Long tables were laid in the market square beneath a bronze face, with bread, oil, salt fish and wine for every merchant who sat down. Buyers who ate went home with full holds. Pyrassan grain houses, whose city had lived on imported grain since Doreva's day, ate and bought, and several brought small bronze faces home to hang above their store doors.

Caleran, as host of the [[The Celestial Compact and the Caleran Republic|Celestial Compact]], forbade the trade. Pyrassa bought anyway. Around 666 Caleran's ships closed the approaches to Tammur, and when Verion's ruler withheld his levy, Caleran struck his city's festival and contract days out of the common year until Verion's own crews refused to sail. The Quay Elder **Qedran Suthar** treated the blockade as an attack on the whole Gateway Republic. The Atherian commander **Aster Vey** promised his senate a short expedition and stayed four years.

Both governments raised further levies as ships were lost. Inland farms sent grain they could not spare to ports they would never see. Families expected home after one season waited through three. Aster Vey took Tammur in 663 after a Gateway relief convoy was caught on a lee shore and broken on the rocks within sight of the town.

The peace of 660 gave Caleran Tammur. The Gateway Council Houses also surrendered children as hostages, to be renewed every seven years, and the children were lodged with Atherian households near Caleran's festival ground. Within two seasons Qarzeth had a new fleet on the water. Caleran's shipwrights could not account for the timber or the bread that fed the carpenters. Gateway sailors said the Provider fed its own, and Caleran's senate began to fear a city that healed faster than it could be hurt.

## Tashqar's Bread, c. 612–604 BR

**Tashqar Ordan** belonged to the hereditary captains of **Oresh**, the Gateway's old overseas harbour on the inner sea. The Ordans kept ships, crews and granaries of their own, and the Council Houses at Qarzeth found it easier to confirm an Ordan in his command than to remove one. Around 612 BR two blighted harvests, coming after two generations of Caleran's levies, left the inner-sea side of Caleran's allied country short of bread. Tashqar landed near the shore market of **Erenost** with Oreshi crews, hired companies and a train of grain ships. He came on his own account.

The Council Houses had reason to fear what he was doing. Their children were in Caleran, and the next exchange of hostages fell due in 611. Envoys from Qarzeth told Caleran's senate that the city had sent no army and wanted no war. When the seventh year came the hostage ships sailed as they had sailed since 660. They brought home the young men and women of the last term and left in their place boys and girls of about six to fourteen, as proof that Qarzeth meant to keep the peace. Oreshi tradition says Tashqar was warned what might happen to those children and sailed anyway. Some tellers add that he wanted the Council Houses forced into his war.

He marched on no city. He went from town to town offering relief from Caleran's levies, set long tables in the market squares and gave Gateway grain by the sack to any council that opened its gates. Several towns accepted, and their people ate well through a hard season. At the next count of the public stores, jars sealed full at harvest were found half empty, the seals unbroken and small bite marks in the wax. Caleran's priests said the Gateway's god was eating from the jars. Tashqar's officers blamed Atherian sabotage. The towns that had taken the grain went hungry while still owing the giver for his gift.

Caleran sent armies to drive him out, and he would not fight them. He withdrew from one fed town to the next with his carts, and the columns that followed him marched through a country whose granaries had been bitten empty. The army of 611 never formed a line of battle. It shrank through the autumn as men walked home or sat down at the Gateway's tables, and its commander brought back a third of it. The army of 609 was better supplied from the Nerava boats and went hungry all the same. Its soldiers ended by breaking open the granaries of towns that had stayed loyal, and three of those towns opened their gates to Tashqar within the month. A hill town that kept its gates shut could still sit across his supply for a season, and he spent much of those years camped below small places with his grain carts drawn up where the walls could see them.

### The Lustration of the Field, 609 BR

Caleran had lost two armies without fighting a battle. The priests of the Red Star taught that the Star gave victory only where a contest was joined, and the republic faced an enemy who refused one. On the festival ground that [[Ilaron and Tarsenna]] had built, the magistrate **Dema Varin** presided over the killing of Gateway soldiers taken from Tashqar's foraging parties and of the hostage children delivered two years before, boys and girls by then of about eight to sixteen. They were offered to Astaroth, the Red Star, "to give the Star a contestant". Atherian annals call the rite **the Lustration of the Field** and name no victims. The Gateway families kept the children's names, the first of their two lists.

Qarzeth had disowned Tashqar's war. After the Lustration its Council Houses voted it their own. In 608 they gave Tashqar the city's fleet and the name of its general, more of Caleran's allies changed sides, and the war reached the islands and the inner gulf. The augurs drew a lesson from the failure, and it was not a moral one. The victims had been the wrong kind; the Red Star wanted a man who would stand against a named enemy. From 607 the field of battle could be accepted only by a living commander in person, the **Warden of the Red Field**, who might not quit the field while the enemy he had named still held it. No one in Caleran said the killing had been wrong.

### The city of the turned gate, c. 608 BR

In the inner gulf, an allied city kept a fortified refuge gate, a granary for strangers and a house of names where guests were registered. As Tashqar's allies gathered outside and the saying "do not eat the Gateway's bread" passed along the coast, the city's magistrates read the register of guests as a list of enemy agents. Guards dragged registered foreigners from their beds, separated mixed households, executed several families in the square and seized their grain. Others were driven out through the gate into the camps of the besiegers. Clerks altered the gate's covenant so that the dead appeared to have broken guest-law first.

Survivors said that the lamps of the refuge gate then went out one by one without smoke, and that the register in the gatehouse turned to plain parchment. The city fell before the season ended. Church tellers later called the darkness a heavenly withdrawal, Star priests called it a failed machine from the Giant age, and the city's name is not agreed. Its survivors carried roof tiles out of the houses that had taken part, and that procession is still repeated where a disgraced sanctuary is put to another use. [[G-38 - The Houses Unhoused before the Gate Turns|The Houses Unhoused before the Gate Turns]] preserves one telling.

### The stand below Thesra, 606 BR

In 607 **Celor Marath** became the first Warden of the Red Field and named Tashqar Ordan as his enemy. A Warden could not delay, and Marath did not. Tashqar now had a city's army to feed as well as his own, and his grain came inland by the road below **Thesra**, the Serathi hill town whose teachers had shaped Caleran's calendar. In 606 Marath offered battle on those approaches, and for the first time in the war Tashqar had to accept. Marath held the road. Atherian annals call the stand a victory. Thesran house-books count the dead, the burned olive terraces and the ancestor tombs used as breastworks, and call it a draw that Thesra paid for. Dema Varin, whose rite had made the office, lived to see it consume the armies he voted for.

### The store doors of Erenost, 604 BR

The war was decided in the storehouses. Towns that had taken Tashqar's grain refused the next shipment, and "do not eat the Gateway's bread" became the allies' proverb. The alliance held out of fear of the gift more than loyalty to Caleran. Then the jars in Tashqar's own camp began to come up short. His quartermasters found bitten wax on stores that had been sealed at Oresh, his hired companies left when the bread ration was cut, and by 605 Gateway soldiers were going hungry on Gateway grain.

In 604 he fell back on Erenost, where he had first landed. It was the seventh year since the last hostage ships had sailed, the year the next children would have gone to Caleran, and no ship left Qarzeth for Caleran or for Erenost. The resident houses of Erenost had kept a joint store under all their seals since an old captain's seizure of the harbour's grain, and after eight years of Tashqar's bread that store stood nearly empty. When he asked the houses to open it for his army that autumn, a crowd of yard workers, fishwives and store porters pulled him from his horse at the store doors and killed him. Erenost says he was buried under the store's threshold, so that every sack carried in or out would pass over him. Oreshi tradition says the Council Houses, which had lost their children to his war, sent no ships to bring him off.

His captains bought their passage home with the fleet. The settlement restricted the Gateway's warships, and Tammur stayed Atherian. The Council Houses would send no more children to Caleran, and Caleran's envoys, who had heard the names of 609 read out at every parley, did not ask for any. Marath's named enemy had died at other people's hands. The augurs argued for a year over whether a bread crowd could win a Warden's field for him, and he laid down the office with one drawn battle to his name. No commander asked for it again for more than a century.

## The fast recovery and the bill, c. 600–540 BR

Qarzeth recovered faster than anyone could explain. Its granaries filled while those of its dependent towns emptied. At Esharat and Belqara, jars sealed at harvest were found short in spring, again with the seals intact. Around 551 the custodians of [[Ilamar before 350 BR|Ilamar]], the sanctuary island whose officers certified grain measures for half the sea, recorded the Esharati and Belqaran findings and refused to certify the Provider's grain. Orsen Valeor and Mera Castren later cited the same record for opposite purposes.

The Provider's sanctuary demanded more with every year of plenty. By the 550s its doors opened only when a living child's name had been entered on the collectors' household lists. The Council Houses, which would never again send a child of their own to Caleran, put their seals to those lists. The [[The Three Collections and the Fall of Qarzeth|Years of Three Collections]], 548–540 BR, were the bill coming due. Families hid children in orchards, cargo and relatives' houses. **Ilya of Qarzeth** copied the names of imprisoned children from the sanctuary stores and escaped in a grain cart. **Sapha of Esharat**, a midwife, organised carriers and farming households into a rescue route; collectors killed her husband and took her daughter **Neral**, who was sacrificed in Qarzeth. The border kingdom of **Hadrath**, under King Harzun, held with the capital.

## The Rope Camp stoppage, c. 536–531 BR

The towns answered with their carts. Carriers and boat crews from **Belqara**, **Esharat**, **Hazreth** and **Sarqen** camped on the rope-makers' ground outside Qarzeth and stopped carrying grain and children into the capital. The camp became known as **Rope Camp**. For the first time in living memory the capital's markets ran short.

**Damen**, a transport officer, carried the council's first terms into the camp. Collectors came in behind his escort to take the carriers' own children from the tents. The camp's harder men killed the collectors they caught and tortured two of them for the names on their lists. **Yasra of Belqara**, speaking for districts already stripped by the collections, stayed with the camp without accepting its murders. It was Yasra who stood between Damen and the rope-makers' knives and sent him back to the city alive with the camp's answer.

The carts stood on the rope ground for five years. The capital ate its reserves, and then the sanctuary's doors began to open less often, because fewer names were coming in from the towns. In 531 the council gave way. Damen brought its oath out to the camp: no collector would enter the four towns again, and the carriers' arrears would be paid. The camp struck its tents and the carts rolled. The towns believed they had won.

The peace lasted until the next festival. With no names from the four towns the sanctuary's doors stayed shut on the feast day, and bread rioters filled the squares of the capital. The council broke its oath within the month. **Teshun Ordan**, of Oresh's captains, took the collectors back into the towns behind his crews, with riders lent by **King Harzun of Hadrath**. Harzun's daughter **Sapha of Hadrath**, no kin to the midwife of Esharat, had been married to Teshun and lodged in the capital, and Hadrath's riders went where her husband sent them. The men who had led Rope Camp were executed at their own doors, and their dependants were killed or carried off for sale. The council had Damen killed as well, for swearing away the god's bread. The reprisals drove the towns to Caleran. Families also kept more intimate inheritances: unpaid carriage, relatives who never came home, and neighbours killed by people with whom they would have to trade again.

## The surrender and the last war, c. 529–520 BR

Fugitive families carried Ilya's list to Atherian ports. In Caleran the senator **Orsen Valeor** stood with survivors beside him and demanded that the child-taking city fall. He made the demand in a city that had killed Gateway children within his grandfather's lifetime, and the exiles answered him with the names of the hostages of 609. **Mera Castren** drew the opposite lesson from the same festival ground: Caleran had stopped, so Qarzeth could stop too, under inspectors. She had meant to ask for hostages as well, and dropped the clause after the names were read. Valeor answered that the rooms would reopen the day the inspectors sailed. His coalition gathered people who wanted rescued children, safer shipping, land, profit and glory. The senate chose the cheaper course, and Castren carried the vote.

She went to Qarzeth herself with a fleet behind her. Caleran demanded the children then held in the sanctuary and the city's arms and warships, and Qarzeth gave them up. Then it demanded the collectors' books, the household lists on which a child's name had to be entered before the sanctuary's doors would open. The assembly argued for nine days. Citizens who hated the collections voted beside merchants who feared the fleet, and the books went down to the quay in eleven chests.

The doors stayed shut. The city had eaten from the Provider's granaries for two generations, and within a month the lower markets had no bread. The priests and the sanctuary's patrons called the people up to the sanctuary district. The two Quay Elders who had sealed the surrender were killed on the sanctuary stair, and collectors began new books in the capital's own poorest streets. The doors opened. Qarzeth's assembly had surrendered, and its god had not. Sanctuary store-workers who had known Ilya got Castren and the eleven chests out of the harbour by night. In Caleran Valeor said that the Provider had answered her for him, and the senate voted for war without sending a fourth demand. The lesser towns offered the Atherians food and passage in return for their survival.

The blockade lasted five campaigning seasons. With the towns' carts stopped, the famous granaries emptied for the first time anyone could remember; the priests killed more children, now from the capital's own households, and the granaries stayed empty. Hunger took children, the old and enslaved labourers first. Citizens who tried to pull down the priesthood were killed in the streets below the sanctuary, and when the wall was attacked opponents of sacrifice defended their houses beside the cult's patrons. Belqara and Esharat fed the besiegers, and Sarqen took in refugees and repaired allied ships. When the wall was breached in 520, Ilya led a detachment to the holding rooms, where soldiers found living children beside cremated remains and her list put names to the dead. Then the bronze face broke. Atherian annals credit a soldier with an axe; Esharat credits its own midwife Sapha and her carriers; Ilya's surviving fragment says only that "it broke when the last name was spoken".

Through the rest of the city Atherian soldiers murdered residents, raped captives and herded survivors for sale, and their commanders let them. Rescued children were torn from their families again when the prisoners were divided, and some were sold. Captains from the wandering harbour-city of [[Marelith]] bought them cheap and resold many to Esharat's ransom funds at a profit, and Ilya cursed those captains by name. Valeor, by then an old man, had come out with the army to see the children brought home. He spent that summer on Sarqen's landing buying them back from the captains out of his own estate, a few at a time. A few days after the breach, at the same landing, the people the war had spared shared one meal at which no one was served by rank and no seat was kept empty. Its rules became the spared towns' **Table Oath**: *no child for the table; no seat kept empty; no guest served by worth.* [[The Three Collections and the Fall of Qarzeth]] follows the siege and the rescue in full, and [[The Spared Towns and the Queen's Country]] the meal and the towns' oath.

## Burning and salting

Caleran's senate had decided that Qarzeth would never again rule the Gateway country. Engineers burned and razed the capital, spoiled its cisterns, broke its channels and carted off its stone. The sanctuary's store compounds were another matter. Grain contractors who had followed the army had the doors and bins sealed under guard, and word went through the towns that Caleran meant to keep the granaries that filled themselves. The towns did not wait to learn whether it was true. Carriers from Hazreth and Sarqen brought salt by boat from the Gateway's own pans and laid it by night on the sanctuary, the gardens and the store compounds, where the plenty had grown. The soldiers on guard had seen the holding rooms, and they stood aside. Their commander reported the salting to Caleran as his own order. Atherian annals record a punishment. Gateway tradition calls it closing the table, and says that "the salt of the table and the salt of the ruin were one salt".

The salted ground died. The fields beyond it went on bearing, and the lesser towns kept their wells, workshops and landings. Parents who had welcomed the end of the sacrifices also cursed the soldiers who had sold their neighbours' children, and the spared towns kept both memories.

The records of the sack do not agree. Caleran's rolls name no officer for the sale of rescued children; Esharat's ransom registers name officers whom no Caleran roll places at the siege. Atherian archivists blamed Gateway forgers and Esharati elders blamed Caleran's clerks. Later historians point to the Compact's archive, which lay in a highland valley where the rolls of the war were being copied when the valley was lost, and whose name the copies themselves disagree about.

## Consequences for the two countries

The spared towns became the centre of [[The Spared Towns and the Queen's Country|the country that followed]]. Their farms, small ports and kinship sustained the people who remained and those who returned, and a later crown at Sarqen ruled them only after swearing their oath. The ruined capital stayed a salted field at the edge of their country.

Caleran gained revenue, access to the inner sea and wider opportunities for its patrons. Commanders rewarded followers, suppliers enlarged their businesses, and captives entered estates and town households. Boatmen called out season after season for the fleets learned to bargain, and at Nerava they refused to load. Caleran kept the Warden's office among its rites, unused since Marath had laid it down, and the Gateway towns kept the hostages' names. Both reappear in [[The Last Generations of the Caleran Republic]].
