# Star Religion and the Imperial Cult

Imperial star religion joined the older Common Sky, city cults and regional powers to ceremonies centred upon the emperor. Temples organised festivals, education, food, employment and public honour. Their priests interpreted the Menhir through Atherian astronomy and treated imperial order as the proper earthly reflection of the heavens. Two events changed the cult during the Age of Glory: the fall of an untested hall in 326 BR, which gave the Red Star's priests a part in every public work, and the speaking of the accession portrait in 247 BR, which led Samor Veloran to veil it and sent the cult's appetite out into the provinces.

This article includes authorial knowledge about the dependent Pride Mask called the Emperor after the Emperor. People within the setting know its manifestations through conflicting religious and historical explanations; the public telling is in [[The Atherian Empire – Age of Glory#The Emperor after the Emperor]].

## The imperial settlement

Thalion elevated Astaroth's priesthood in rites of victory and command. The First Celebrant of the Common Sky retained senior ceremonial duties, while imperial occasions gave the Warrior a new precedence. The arrangement presented the emperor as defender of a public religious order inherited from the cities.

The Red Star's priests also took up a gentler rite, dedicating finished public works and asking their patron that they stand. Military engineers had long used his true test, loading a valuable frame until a crack showed where force would carry. In 326 BR a new treasury hall at Caleran received the dedication and no test, and it fell during the heir Caelor's inspection. Thalion would not disown the priesthood on which his victory rites rested, so he divided its work. Priests kept blessings and victory rites. Rune-trained wardens performed the test before a priest of the Red Star, broke the portion of each work built to break (the Star's share), and the official who opened the work stood beneath it while the first loaded wagons crossed. The wardens' rite remained standing practice through the age and beyond it.

Other Star Children remained important. Lyraxis governed performance and inspired deception; Orionis hunting and wilderness; Hecatara crossroads and consequential choice; Typhonius catastrophe and monstrous birth; Arachnara infestation and concealed networks. Local traditions described the same powers through different stories, and the Astral Keepers' Circle tables quarrelled with temple hymns over whether the powers were a family at all.

Thanatos occupied a different category behind the public theology. Atherian priests gathered signs associated with the Boatman's work into a star cult of death, theft and passage. Their rites comforted mourners, while the passage of human souls remained wholly outside priestly control.

## Temples and communities

A temple could own land, employ craftspeople, teach pupils and provide meals on feast days. Treasuries held gifts and funds used for public works. Priesthoods offered families honourable careers and rulers a means by which ceremonies could be repeated across provinces.

Provincial cults entered the imperial hierarchy through translated names, paired festivals and accepted local titles. Some communities welcomed recognition and patronage. Others lost land or saw a dangerous local power described as a friendly attendant of the stars.

An imperial sacrifice expressed political membership as well as religious belief. Refusal could therefore be treated as disloyalty. Enforcement varied by ruler and province before the Redeemer era made the issue central.

## Images of the ruler

Coins, statues and painted portraits allowed people who had never seen the emperor to recognise him. Workshops used established poses and titles so that a new ruler appeared within a familiar office. Accession ceremonies joined the living person to victories, abundance and celestial favour attributed to predecessors.

The representations were never perfect copies. Provincial artists gave rulers familiar local features; old statues received new names; jokes compared an official portrait with the emperor's actual face. Even so, repeated acclaim produced a stable cultural image greater than any one biography.

At Caleran every emperor from Meren onwards was confirmed before one accession portrait in the palace audience hall, later called the Face. Olarion made it the centre of court life: guards knelt to it at each change of watch, officials renewed their loyalty before it, and his informers swore their denunciations in its sight.

## The Emperor after the Emperor

During the House of Thalion, this accumulated attention formed a dependent Mask of Pride. Its first well-supported autonomous response occurred during the empty succession of 247 BR. On the second night of Olarion's mourning, the Face answered a chamberlain's question in the voice Caleran expected of "the Emperor", and went on answering.

Avaren Mott, who held the senate and part of the guard inside Caleran, governed through it while the three other claimants held the western districts, the fleet and the river army. Decrees were read before it, the guard swore in its presence, and it addressed Mott as the magistrate governing in the Emperor's name. Within its sight obedience became fervent. Beyond the palace walls it counted for nothing, and Mott learned the difference when Veloran and Halven stopped the grain. Asked where the grain was, the Face answered as an emperor must: no granary of the Emperor was empty, and those who said otherwise were hiding it. Mott ordered the searches. The portrait was carried ahead of the search parties so that households knelt before the soldiers entered. A river-quarter household whose son had been denounced before the same image under Olarion refused to kneel, and the soldiers killed the father on his threshold. Demonstrators outside an inner market died in the portrait's sight.

Samor Veloran's river army had never stood before it. In the audience hall he had the Face covered with a soldier's cloak before he spoke to the guard and refused to be addressed by it. Mott, running for the hall as his sanctuary, was killed at its threshold. Whether the cloak had fallen before the blow remains disputed in Caleran's accounts.

**The veil.** Samor's first law kept the Face veiled except at accessions and on the founding anniversary, and made questioning it a capital offence. Custody passed to the First Celebrant of the Common Sky, whose college had refused Mott's rites, and away from Astaroth's ceremonial priests, who had performed them. The Celebrants of the Veil became a check on the Red Star's precedence at court and kept the Face through 0 AR. In the fever summer of 151 they refused crowds who brought their sick to the audience hall doors and begged them to lift the cloth.

**Acclamation order.** From 246 the Face was unveiled last at an accession, before a ruler whom the armies and senate had already agreed. The vacancy of 151 was the first full test.

**The appetite moves outward.** The copies in governors' courts, town halls and army standards stayed in the Red Star priests' keeping, and with the central image covered the Mask fed through them. Elian I's citizenship oath after 151 put thousands of new citizens before provincial copies; Jirahar scribes swore with the copy veiled. In Raveth from 119, Lethara's estate officers made relief debtors swear before the image in place of the neighbours' countersignature, and households sworn before it surrendered land and animals with an obedience their countersigned neighbours never showed. Towns commissioned costlier portraits for their halls.

**Velen and Doran.** Velen I had the Face unveiled at his river spectacles so that crowds acclaimed image and emperor together, and exempted the festival banks from the wardens' test because the works were the Emperor's and the Face was present. The banks failed and drowned workers and spectators. His guards, sworn before the image, killed him in 61. Doran I re-veiled the Face, abolished the exemption and had his officers enter their commands by the Low Door Companions' low threshold, carrying a bowl, instead of swearing before the image. Officers believed the rite weakened the image's pull; the Celebrants called that superstition.

**At 0 AR.** The Face in Caleran was quiet; the provincial copies were hungrier than it had been since 247. Governors heard cases beneath them, so that a claim to kingship offended the office they represented. The governor at Second Qiryath travelled with a copy and with Severan, High Augur of the Red Star. The Jirahar, with their veiled oath and an imageless sanctuary under the Qiryath charter, were the empire's most visible refusers of the face.

Priests, courtiers and rulers sometimes exploited its responses. The Mask also encouraged emperors to resemble its increasingly inhuman pattern. A ruler who accepted every ceremonial expectation could become more legible to the cult while losing contact with the circumstances of living subjects.

> The Mask was a route through the Idol Throne of Pride. Olarion's ghost, Thalion's soul and the human imperial office remained separate from it, and Olarion, Thalion, Mott and every claimant retained complete human souls; the Mask served whoever stood beside the image. Coins and portraits supplied imagery; praise, obedience, denunciation and desire for a ruler beyond ordinary judgement supplied nourishment. Punishing households that remembered a dead ruler's cruelty fed it (`atlas-b3-04`).
>
> It acts where its image is seen and addressed, intensifying obedience to the represented office. It could reproduce familiar gestures, recall praise formulae and make a claimant appear continuous with an ideal predecessor. It knows nothing of stores, armies or private facts: the grain answer of 247 was the expected imperial sentence, and the searches were Mott's choice. Covering one image interrupts its pull in that room without starving it. It created no debt in Raveth and chose no policy there; Neriah advised the estate, Elian II authorised it, and Lethara's officers chose the oath. Whether Doran's low door weakens its pull stays unresolved even authorially.

## Rival interpretations

Temple accounts treated answering portraits as proof that the stars preserved the imperial office. Sceptics exposed hollow statues, concealed speakers and staged responses in several cities, and some Caleran writers said Mott's chamberlains had spoken from behind the panel in 247. Those frauds existed alongside the genuine dependent Mask.

Some provincial traditions recognised the hunger behind the uniform face and avoided naming a deceased ruler before his image. Others addressed the portrait as the ruler himself. Raveth's villages said the image "took the argument out of them". Later Redeemer writers gave the hunger its name.

The Mask survived dynastic changes because workshops and ceremonies continued. Its later history through the Redeemer, Conversion and destruction of star temples belongs to the following chronological packages. The possibility of an autonomous Archmask arising from later cult remnants remains unresolved.

## Limits

No imperial rite creates a god, saint or immortal human continuity. Menhir responses establish their own natural-law relationships and confer no moral legitimacy: the wardens' test reveals the weakness of whatever is genuinely submitted to force, and Astaroth remains blind to who pays for the answer. The Emperor after the Emperor remains a dependent Mask of an existing Idol Throne; it introduces no new Idol succession.
