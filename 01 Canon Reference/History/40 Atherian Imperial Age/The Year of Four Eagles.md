# The Year of Four Eagles

The Year of Four Eagles was the succession war of 247–246 BR which ended the House of Thalion and brought Samor Veloran to power. Four claimants held different parts of the empire: the capital, the western veterans, the eastern fleet and the river army that fed Caleran. Caleran remembers the year for something stranger. In the first days after Olarion's death, the accession portrait in the palace audience hall answered questions in the voice the city expected of "the Emperor". Avaren Mott governed through it, and soldiers carried it ahead of the grain searches into the poor districts. Samor's most famous act was a refusal: he had the portrait covered with a soldier's cloak and would not let it speak to him.

From that year the portrait, called the Face, was kept veiled. It was uncovered only at an accession and on the founding anniversary, and always last, after the armies and the senate had already agreed.

## The Face in the audience hall

Every emperor since Meren had been confirmed before the same painted panel at the end of the audience hall: a ruler in the red-bordered mantle, the founder's features softened by a century of retouching, the eyes painted so that they seemed to follow a petitioner from the door to the dais. Each new reign added a new face over the old pose. Olarion made the panel the centre of court life. His guards knelt to it at every change of watch. His informers swore their denunciations before it, and men who had lost relatives to those denunciations learned to keep their eyes on the floor when they passed.

Olarion died suddenly in 247. Accounts variously blamed illness, poison and a frightened attendant, and no explanation became conclusive. On the second night of the mourning, a chamberlain followed custom and addressed a question to the portrait about the funeral order. Witnesses in the hall said it answered in a dry, carrying voice, using the formulas of an emperor in audience. It went on answering.

## The four claims

Avaren Mott, senior magistrate at Caleran, received recognition from the senate and part of the palace guard. He promised to restore appointments made before Olarion's purges. His authority depended on the capital and the impression that government continued there, and the speaking portrait gave him that impression on a scale no senate vote could match. Decrees were read before it. The guard swore in its presence. It addressed Mott as the magistrate governing in the Emperor's name, and within its sight obedience became fervent. Officials who had hesitated over his orders in the morning carried them out at a run in the afternoon.

Daran Thalion, a relative of Cassara, held estates and veteran support in the western military districts. He claimed family right and accused Mott of concealing Olarion's murder. Households that had lost property under the last emperor joined him in expectation of restitution.

Aster Halven commanded ships and eastern garrisons. Provincial ports supported him because he could keep their routes open and promised to reduce Caleran's arrears. His strength came from long provincial service and command of the fleet.

Samor Veloran commanded the central river army and its grain corridor. His father had entered imperial service through a provincial municipal household. His soldiers had never stood before the Face. Samor promised them pay, the confirmation of local officers and an end to the denunciations Olarion's informers had sworn before the portrait.

## War along the supplies

Beyond the palace walls the portrait counted for nothing, and Mott learned the difference when the grain stopped. Convoys for Caleran passed through forces answering to Veloran or Halven, and both commanders held them back.

Daran marched towards the Aurin and seized food from towns unwilling to declare for him. One town opened its stores after his men took the families of councillors as hostages. Veloran defeated him before he reached Caleran. Daran surrendered under a promise of safe custody and was executed several days later on Samor's order.

Halven moved against the river ports. His fleet won an initial engagement and burned military stores, together with a neighbouring working quarter. Melvo's earlier naval system had divided command among ports capable of continuing after one loss; local officers now used those arrangements against Halven. He died during a later battle, and his surviving captains negotiated separately.

## The searches

Bread prices at Caleran doubled and then doubled again. Mott asked the portrait where the grain was. It answered as an emperor must: no granary of the Emperor was empty, and those who said otherwise were hiding it. Mott ordered the searches.

Soldiers went into the poor districts with the portrait carried before them on a litter, so that a household knelt before the door was forced. Within its sight people gave up what they had and named neighbours who had more. The searches found little grain and broke a great many doors. In a river-quarter street, a household whose son had been denounced before the same portrait under Olarion would not kneel when it was carried over their threshold. The soldiers killed the father in the doorway and took the family's stored barley. The street kept the story; later temple accounts dropped it.

Demonstrators gathered outside an inner market to demand the grain the portrait had promised. The litter was brought up, and the soldiers who killed them did so in its sight. That killing ended Mott's claim to civic legitimacy in the districts, though the guard in the palace stayed loyal for some days more.

## The cloak

Veloran reached the capital after promising the guard its arrears. In the audience hall he had the Face covered with a soldier's cloak before he spoke to the guardsmen, and when the voice began beneath the cloth he refused to be addressed by it. His men called him "the soldier who would not answer".

Mott ran for the audience hall as his sanctuary and was killed at its threshold by guardsmen. Caleran's accounts disagree over whether the cloak had slipped from the panel before the blow fell. Partisans of the Celebrants, who later kept the veil, said it had stayed in place and the guard had simply chosen its pay. Old soldiers' tellings said a corner had fallen and the portrait had been looking at Mott when he died. Samor punished some of the killers and rewarded their unit, actions serving different needs within the same week.

## The settlement

In 246, the senate and principal commands recognised Samor I. He gave his three dead rivals unequal funeral honours. He married his daughter Tessa to Aren Cassar, a surviving member of the old imperial household. Towns that had supplied him received charters and tax relief; those associated with his rivals supplied settlements for soldiers.

His first law concerned the portrait. The Face was to stay veiled except at accessions and on the anniversary of the founding, and anyone who questioned it would die. Its custody passed to the First Celebrant of the Common Sky, whose college had refused to perform Mott's rites, and away from the priests of the Red Star, who had performed them. The Celebrants of the Veil, as the custodians came to be called, kept the cloth in place and the keys of the audience hall for the rest of the age. At every later accession the Face was unveiled last, before a ruler whom the armies and senate had already agreed. The order was first tested in 151 BR, when Orent II died without an heir.

The veil covered one portrait. Governors' courts, town halls and army standards had their own copies of the imperial face, kept by priests of the Red Star, and in the following generations those copies multiplied.

## Interpretations

Temple writers held that the stars had preserved the imperial office through the portrait while no emperor lived. Sceptics in the capital said Mott's chamberlains had spoken from behind the panel; the guardsmen who had heard it denied that any man stood there. Provincial households that had suffered under Olarion said that the portrait had grown hungry on his denunciations and went on eating after he died. The Celebrants never gave an account of what they had covered.

## Later memory

The four eagles remained a common image for ambition feeding from one body. Plays made the claimants into recognisable types: the talkative magistrate, injured prince, proud admiral and patient soldier. Audiences knew the talkative magistrate was the one who had talked with the portrait. Provincial accounts retained the names of people whose grain and relatives had become part of the contest, and in the river quarter a closed door painted red was for generations a sign that a household would not kneel.
