# The Atherian Empire - Age of Glory

The Atherian Age of Glory was the long period, from Aurelius Thalion's settlement around 350 BR to the Redeemer's death in 0 AR, in which imperial law, armies, archives, roads, ports and the Star Pantheon held most of the known world's central belt within one political system. Later Thalmyric writers describe it as ordered abundance. Subject peoples remembered the taxes, land seizures, forced settlements and religious hierarchy that paid for the marble.

Three and a half centuries changed the empire from end to end. The age began with a hall falling on an emperor's heir and a law that made every bridge prove itself before anyone crossed it. It passed through a year in which a painted portrait governed Caleran, a summer in which three provinces walked in their sleep, a famine that turned free farmers into estate dependants, and a war in Vashar that handed Atheria the keys of the Jirahar sanctuary. By the Redeemer's lifetime, hungry copies of the emperor's face hung in every governor's court.

[[Historical Atlas of the Atherian Age of Glory]] provides the complete sequence from Thalion's settlement to 0 AR. Over those centuries an emperor of Atheria was made in three ways: painted on the board the armies swore to, seated at the spared towns' table at Sarqen, and stood under an arch. The sequence follows how each making failed in turn.

## Government

[[Aurelius Thalion]] established imperial supremacy around 350 BR after [[The Wars of the Atherian Succession]]. The settlement followed Casvilar's assassination, twenty years in which no commander dared disband an army while any of his seven killers lived, and the end of Samyra Nerethan's kingdom, which came at a table on Sarqen's landing rather than on a battlefield. Continuing army pay, central appointments and regular taxation gave the new government a more dependable basis than the competing commands it replaced. Thalion never pretended the republic had been restored; he governed from the store-house above the muster ground.

The emperor was presented as the stars' chosen mediator and guardian of terrestrial order. Provincial governors combined civil and military authority but depended on treasury officials, municipal councils and temple estates. The old senate retained control over portions of infrastructure, citizenship and commercial law while losing meaningful command of the army.

The supreme command needed more than descent. Thalion's soldiers had sworn to a painted board of his face, and for a century an heir was made by painting his face on it. The spared towns of Sarqen seated each emperor, or whoever they chose in his absence, at their unranked table every spring. After the board spoke for itself in 247, Samor Veloran covered it, and emperors were agreed by the divided river commands and made under an arch while loaded wagons crossed above them. Women of the ruling households administered estates, managed correspondence and directed patronage, and one of them, Cassara, held the seat at Sarqen while her husband's face hung on the board. Velen I declared that the Emperor did not stand under, and drowned beneath his own untested banks.

A professional bureaucracy maintained census rolls, property surveys, water rights, courts, road stations and grain distribution. Modern Thalmyric and Eclessian law still cites surviving Atherian registers, sometimes to resolve a dispute and sometimes to resurrect one.

The [[The House of Thalion|first imperial house]] ruled until 247 BR. [[The Painted Reign]] ended in 245 with Samor Veloran, a warden-general of the river army, standing under a grain bridge at Caleran's water gate, and the unpainted emperors of [[The House of Veloran|his century]] governed through 151. The Halreth rulers, first seated by the towns of Sarqen in a fever year, and the later Odrun household carried the empire to the Redeemer's lifetime. [[Provinces and Municipal Life in Imperial Atheria]] explains the cities and local officers through whom each house governed.

## The age in motion

**The fallen hall, 326 BR.** Thalion's priests of the Red Star blessed a new treasury hall on the forum at Caleran and asked their patron that it stand. No one loaded it first. It came down on the heir, Caelor, during an inspection, along with the workmen and enslaved labourers around him; Caelor died of his injuries in 324. The engineers said what the priests would not: strength had been asked for without a trial. From then on no one entered a public work until rune-trained wardens had loaded it before the Red Star, heard the stone crack where it would fail, and broken the portion built into every bridge and aqueduct for that purpose, which builders called the Star's share. The official who opened a work stood under the arch while the first loaded wagons crossed.

**The portrait that spoke, 247–245 BR.** Olarion died with no heir painted, and three commanders raised their standards. In the audience hall the accession portrait answered in the Emperor's voice, and the magistrate Avaren Mott governed through it for two winters. Store clerks who read their tallies in its sight read out full granaries while the bins emptied, and its promise that no imperial granary was empty sent soldiers behind the carried portrait into the poor districts. Samor Veloran threw his cloak over it and would not be painted in its place, and it was kept veiled for the rest of the age.

**The crown and the sleepers, 219 and c. 200 BR.** The [[The Crown of Olveth|Crown of Olveth]] burned to death a general who tried to wear a shelter's circlet as a trophy. Some twenty years later came the Sleepwalkers' Summer. For one season the people of three provinces rose from their beds at night and walked, harmlessly and all, the wardens' records insist, in the same direction. The empire surveyed the trampled line and built a road on it, the Via Somnia, which is still in use and still has oddly good footing. The walking stopped everywhere on the same morning. The road ends at an ordinary hillside that has been dug four times by three civilisations and found to contain dirt. Augurs said a summons had been withdrawn; the later Church calls it a prefiguration of the pilgrim roads. No account has satisfied the others.

**Roads east, c. 190–151 BR.** Orent I drove the Arcelon road through the eastern mountains to match Vashar's Dawn Road. Its embankment passed its test in the dry season and slid onto the labourers in the rains, which gave the road crews their phrase for a promise made in fair weather: "tested in the dry". The road pulled Raveth's river towns away from Vasharan tribute, and they joined the empire by negotiation late in Orent II's reign.

**Fever, oath and famine, 151–113 BR.** A summer fever killed Orent II in 151 without an heir and filled the river camps with sick men. The commanders talked instead of marching. The spared towns of Sarqen laid their boards out of season and put their governor, Elian Halreth, in the emperor's empty seat; the eastern cities stood behind him for citizenship, and the commanders required each new citizen to swear before the emperor's image. From 119 Raveth starved. Relief came as debt, the court turned the debt into a crown estate over its own administrator's resignation, and where debts were sworn before the image, families walked their animals to the estate offices without being asked.

**Qiryath and the last emperors, c. 85–0 BR.** A Vasharan civil war, the War of the Two Dawns, let Atheria take the Jirahar sanctuary of Qiryath under a charter that kept every image out of its courts. Velen I unveiled the Face at his river spectacles, exempted their banks from the test and in 61 drowned with his audience while his guard stood facing the portrait. Doran, supplied by former servants who had risen from a riot at Lacaris, re-veiled the Face and had his officers enter their commands by a low door instead of swearing before the image. Isfir was born in a Jirahar tower town under Odrun I in 33 BR.

## The Emperor after the Emperor

Atherian chronicles, provincial songs and later Church writers all record a hunger that grew inside the imperial cult. It began with an absence. Thalion never went to war in person, and through the succession wars his companies swore to a painted board of his face carried on a pole; the board that hung at the end of the Caleran audience hall after 350, before which every later emperor was confirmed, had been one of them. Workshops painted every emperor in the same pose, with the same mantle and the same mild eyes, so that a new ruler appeared within a familiar office. Coins, statues and town-hall portraits carried that face into provinces whose people would never see a living emperor. Praise was offered to it, oaths were sworn before it, and denunciations were made in its sight.

The chronicles say that the face began to answer. The portrait at Caleran spoke in 247 BR with no emperor alive to speak for. In the provinces, households who remembered a dead ruler's cruelty refused to honour his new, benign image and were punished for it, and the punishments themselves were said to make the image stronger. Temple accounts called the answering portraits proof that the stars preserved the imperial office. Sceptics exposed hollow statues, concealed speakers and staged responses in several cities. Some provincial traditions recognised the hunger behind the uniform face and avoided naming a deceased ruler before his image. Others addressed the portrait as the ruler himself.

Samor's veil kept the Face at Caleran quiet for most of the age. It could not cover the copies. By 0 AR every governor heard cases beneath one, citizens and debtors had sworn before them in their thousands, and Raveth remembered its lost farms through the image as well as through the officials who took them. Redeemer writers later called the hungry face the Emperor after the Emperor, and argued with priests of the old cult over whether it had ever been anything but paint and priests' voices. [[Overview of the Astratheon Religion]] describes the wider star religion.

## Religion

The Star Pantheon organised older cults beneath an imperial celestial hierarchy. Astaroth represented conquest and public order; Lyraxis art and civic performance; the figure called Thanatos death and passage; Arachnara illness, vermin and hidden passages; Typhonius calamity and monstrous birth. Local gods could survive as regional titles, attendants or disputed manifestations so long as their temples acknowledged imperial primacy. Temple hymns called the principal powers Star Children, born of the light of Sun and Moon. The astronomers of the Astral Keepers sorted the same powers into Circles by what they did rather than whom they were born to, and the two schools quarrelled throughout the age. Later imperial recensions added Thanatos, although older temple lists disagreed about his parentage, and his cult's tales of passage after death told of the Boatman.

Thalion's government raised the Red Star, Astaroth, to precedence in rites of victory and command, because its priests had declared the Red Field closed for him in 350 when the Common Sky's college, whose First Celebrant had died on the Salt Field beside Thalion's rival, would not. After 326 the Red Star's priests also stood witness at the load tests of every public work. The Common Sky's Celebrants, who held the veiled Face after 245, became the Red Star priesthood's rivals at court.

Temple complexes were schools, archives, treasuries, employers and political centres. Their observatories and ritual substructures form some of Thalmyria's most dangerous surviving ruins. Many were later converted into churches; others were sealed, dismantled or built over after the Redeemer's conversion movement.

## Cities and Infrastructure

Caleran stood at the centre of a road and water network extending across the empire. Milestones measured distance from its forum. Aqueducts supported dense urban populations and irrigated estates; ports moved grain, salt, wine, metals and enslaved people; fortified stations protected official couriers and tax shipments.

Every new arch carried its Star's share, a span or member built to be broken under test. Quarry towns cut extra stone for it, and in 274 BR quarrymen struck rather than cut "stone for the Star to break" through the harvest; troops killed them. A crowd at a bridge opening watched the magistrate stand beneath the keystone while the wagons rolled overhead. Builders told stories of officials who sent a slave to stand under in their place, and of the works that fell on them afterwards.

The system was never uniform. Core cities received monumental works while distant villages supplied labour and tax. Regional capitals adapted imperial plans to local materials and customs. The resulting variation is why modern ruins cannot be understood from Caleran's architecture alone.

## Learning and Art

Schools taught law, rhetoric, mathematics, medicine, astronomy, engineering and script magic. Public mosaics and reliefs joined local histories to imperial myth. Theatre, satire, athletic games, philosophical schools and astropoetry flourished under elite patronage. Archive culture preserved immense knowledge while allowing the state to decide whose testimony counted.

Warlock rune magic developed from older practical scripts into regulated military and engineering disciplines. Colleges standardised safe portions of the craft for siege, signalling, construction, firefighting and controlled elemental work. Their sealed laboratories are among the rare Atherian sites that may still function.

## Uneven Prosperity

Long-distance trade, durable roads and long stretches of peace were paid for with frontier war, forced labour, unequal citizenship and provincial grain shipped to the core, and the provinces rebelled over the bill more often than over the law. The road crews of Arcelon sang one song for the victory and another for the dead under the embankment, and Raveth's villages counted farms surrendered for bread.

## The Redeemer's World

By the Redeemer's lifetime, Atheria was ancient enough to mistake endurance for permanence. A governor sat for the pilgrimage at Second Qiryath with a copy of the emperor's face behind him and an augur of the Red Star at his side, and the charter that had brought Atheria into the sanctuary told him which cases the elders could send him. Its institutions could carry a new faith across continents once converted, but first they supplied the courts, roads, archives and execution machinery used against it.

## Material Legacy

In the Thalmyric heartland, imperial stone remains part of ordinary life. Houses reuse cut blocks; roads follow buried grades; farmers recover coins and rusted arms; churches occupy converted civic structures. Broken test-spans lie beside working bridges, and village children still dare one another to stand under them. Complete tombs, sealed archives, relic chambers and operational devices are rare because centuries of reuse, occupation, looting and war have already opened most accessible sites.
