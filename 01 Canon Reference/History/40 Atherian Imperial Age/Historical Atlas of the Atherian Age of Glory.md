# Historical Atlas of the Atherian Age of Glory

The Atherian Age of Glory extended from Aurelius Thalion's settlement around 350 BR to the world into which Isfir the Redeemer was born in 33 BR. Across those centuries, a government created from civil war became an empire of provinces, municipal cities, military roads and public star worship. Its inhabitants experienced long periods of peace within the central routes, repeated wars at their edges and striking inequalities between places joined under the same ruler.

Atherian writers called this period the Glory because its aqueducts, law, literature and armies seemed to prove that ordered civilisation could encompass the world. Provincial families could gain citizenship, education and office through the institutions of the empire. The same system transferred land to military settlers, moved enslaved people across seas and made distant households answer for the ambitions of Caleran.

## The settlement generation, 350–318 BR

[[The Later Reign of Aurelius Thalion]] began with disarmament, payment and the reassignment of commands. Thalion retained many municipal institutions while bringing provincial governors and the professional army under his household. Veterans received land, including farms taken during the succession wars. Sarqen remained a working port under imperial supervision.

Peace required further campaigns. Commanders secured roads between the Aurin and eastern ports, enforced settlements among former royal allies and negotiated with southern Folk leaders whose communities remained beyond direct occupation. Regular assessments gradually replaced indiscriminate military seizure as the usual means of supplying garrisons, though collection still involved force.

Thalion's son Caelor died before him, crushed in 326 BR when a new treasury hall that the Red Star's priests had blessed, but no one had loaded, came down during his inspection. Thalion answered with a rule that outlasted his house. No one was to enter a public work until rune-trained wardens had loaded it before the Red Star and broken the portion built into it to break, the Star's share, and the official who opened it stood under the arch while the first loaded wagons crossed.

The emperor chose his nephew Meren, son of Evara Thalion and Darion Velsar, as successor. That decision joined descendants of the two principal founding rivals within one household. It also angered relatives who expected the succession to remain among Thalion's children.

## The House of Thalion, 318–247 BR

[[The House of Thalion]] governed through Meren I, Valeran I and Olarion. Meren continued demobilisation and confirmed the position of towns that had supplied the founding coalition. His daughter Cassara supervised the Aurin stores. Her husband Valeran, whom Meren adopted and presented to the army, enlarged waterworks, harbour stores and road stations during his reign. His aqueduct needed a breaking span in every run of arches, and in 274 BR quarrymen ordered to cut "stone for the Star to break" through the harvest struck and were killed by troops.

The dynasty's government gradually acquired a ceremonial shape. An accession required army recognition, public confirmation at Caleran and the principal rites of the Common Sky and Astaroth. The separate ceremonies sometimes supported different candidates and gave powerful households several ways to contest a ruler.

Olarion used accusations, confiscation and execution against relatives and officers he feared, and his informers swore their denunciations before the accession portrait in the audience hall. His death in 247 BR left no candidate able to command the capital, fleets and frontier armies together. [[The Year of Four Eagles]] carried fighting into the Aurin, eastern ports and western military districts before Samor Veloran prevailed. In the capital the portrait answered in the Emperor's voice. The magistrate Avaren Mott governed through it, and its assurance that no imperial granary was empty sent soldiers behind the carried image into the poor districts. Samor covered it with a soldier's cloak and would not be addressed by it.

## The Veloran settlement, 246–151 BR

[[The House of Veloran]] arose from a river-army household outside the old Caleran aristocracy. Samor I rewarded soldiers and punished cities that had supported his rivals. His first law kept the portrait, now called the Face, veiled in the custody of the Common Sky's Celebrants, uncovered only at accessions and the founding anniversary and always last. He divided the river army so that no single commander held every approach to Caleran. His adopted successor Aren I made those emergency arrangements more durable through roads, town charters and a clearer provincial treasury. Aren's marriage to Tessa Veloran joined the new ruling family to the old through his descent from the House of Thalion.

Under Aren I, the [[The Crown of Olveth|Crown of Olveth]] killed a general who tried to make a recognised shelter object into a victory trophy. Its return became one of the age's best-known supernatural episodes.

Early in Orent I's reign, around 200 BR, came the Sleepwalkers' Summer. For one season the people of three provinces walked in their sleep, all, the wardens' records insist, in the same direction, until the walking stopped everywhere on the same morning. The empire built a road, the Via Somnia, on the trampled line. It ends at an ordinary hillside that has been dug four times by three civilisations and found to contain dirt.

Orent I pursued eastern campaigns and reorganised the roads behind them, building the Arcelon road to answer Vashar's Dawn Road. [[The Two Roads of Arcelon]] preserves the victory account beside the work songs of people whose settlement was burned and whose labourers died building the route; its embankment, tested in the dry season, slid onto them in the rains. His son Orent II inherited the resulting revenues and presided over several comparatively peaceful decades of municipal building, travel and scholarship. Late in his reign Raveth's river towns, whose trade now ran west along the new road, left Vasharan tribute and joined the empire by negotiation. A fever summer killed Orent II in 151 without an heir.

## Later dynasties, 151 BR–0 AR

[[The Last Dynasties of the Age of Glory]] began with Elian Serrat, a provincial commander accepted after negotiation among the army, senate and eastern cities while the river camps were still sick. The Serrat rulers depended heavily on municipal elites and professional administrators. Their reigns expanded citizenship among useful provincial households, each new citizen swearing before the emperor's image (Jirahar scribes swore with the copy veiled), while concentrating great estates around the roads and river basins.

During the Raveth hunger that began in 119 BR, the administrator Neriah bar Sorek organised grain imports and distribution that saved towns from starvation. His policy also transferred defaulting farms to the imperial domain and bound former owners to work them. When estate officers replaced the neighbours' countersignature with an oath before the emperor's image, Neriah resigned, and the households that swore gave up their land with a meekness their neighbours never showed. [[The Keeper of Borrowed Grain]] follows the preservation, confiscation and later arguments over his responsibility.

Under the late Serrats a Vasharan succession war, the War of the Two Dawns, emptied the western Vasharan commands and set offices in the sanctuaries up for sale. At Qiryath the sign of acceptance answered an interim Keeper chosen by the city while a Vasharan purchaser held the confirmation. The Atherian governor at Arcelon came to the next pilgrimage with an augur of the Red Star, who asked the sign to decide between the claimants; the purchaser refused, the interim Keeper offered, and no sign was given. The governor ruled on the sanctuary's written record, and a scribe of the house of Qoreth drafted the charter under which Atheria held Qiryath until 65 AR. Vashar took a yearly Dawn Portion of pilgrim dues and called it rent.

The [[The Companions Who Entered by the Low Door - Imperial History|Low Door Companions]] rose during the last Serrat generations from service work into military and provincial office. Their abilities allowed people of modest origin to enter imperial government. Hostage-taking, an unlawful raid and the betrayal of a refuge accompanied their ascent.

A succession of later court houses preserved the empire through alliances with armies and provinces. Velen Serrat unveiled the Face at his river spectacles and exempted their banks from the wardens' test; the banks failed and drowned workers and spectators. Doran I restored authority after Velen's murder in 61 BR, supplied first by stores and workshops in the hands of two Low Door Companions. He re-veiled the Face, ended the exemption and had his officers enter their commands through a low door carrying a bowl instead of swearing before the image. Othran I succeeded through marriage and military support in 42 BR, and Isfir was born in a Jirahar tower town under the Qiryath charter's protection in 33 BR. His son Othran II became emperor in 24 BR and was still reigning when the provincial governor ordered the Redeemer's execution at Second Qiryath. The case reached the capital through provincial reports.

## Provinces and frontiers

[[Provinces and Municipal Life in Imperial Atheria]] explains the layered administration beneath the rulers. Caleran governed most effectively through cities which collected taxes, maintained water and supplied transport. Local councils preserved languages, cults and influential families while extending imperial decisions.

The empire's eastern provinces included much of the country later inherited by Eclessia. Sestaran harbours occupied a changing range from direct provincial rule to alliance and commercial privilege. Sarqen connected the cultivated gateway country to inner-sea routes. Southern forts and treaty districts met independent Folk societies. Northern roads reached well kingdoms and mobile Tarkhan communities, while most of the dry belt remained outside imperial rule. Jirahar tower towns retained their own institutions inside and beyond eastern imperial territory.

Vashar remained a sovereign peer beyond the eastern provinces, holding its highland core and most of the Ubar and taking a yearly share of Qiryath's pilgrim dues for a city its scribes called leased. The Qahuri basin kept its three crowns. Atherian grain and harbour contracts bound Qemaret's lower river, where an imperial station stood at the harbour, and Tenaret and Sakhret ruled as crowned clients after Vasharan tribute lapsed during the War of the Two Dawns.

[[The Imperial Army and the Frontiers]] follows recruitment, military families, auxiliary service and the work of maintaining forts. Recruits entered from subject and allied communities as well as the Atherian citizen body. Veterans carried language and institutions into new settlements, often upon land whose earlier inhabitants contested their title.

## Work, movement and prosperity

[[Food, Trade and Enslavement in Imperial Atheria]] follows the grain fleets, farms, mines, workshops and households sustaining the cities. More dependable routes encouraged specialisation and allowed people to travel farther for work or learning. Inns, baths, theatres and festivals made urban life attractive to visitors and migrants.

Enslavement remained part of the economy and the household. War supplied captives; markets separated relatives; mines and great estates consumed lives. Manumission gave some people and their children routes into citizenship. Those sold elsewhere and the families severed by the market received no remedy.

[[Schools, Arts and Imperial Magic]] describes the pleasures and knowledge of the period: theatre, satire, mosaics, medicine, navigation, philosophy, engineering and regulated rune craft. The empire preserved and exchanged immense bodies of knowledge. Patronage and censorship influenced which texts acquired multiple copies and which survived only in provincial quotations.

## Religion and imperial memory

Imperial [[Overview of the Astratheon Religion|star religion]] developed the Common Sky into an imperial ceremonial system. Astaroth received precedence in rites of command and, after 326 BR, stood witness at the test of every public work; other Star Children governed festivals, occupations and local interpretations of the natural world. Temple hymns and the astronomers' Circle tables disagreed throughout the age about what the Star Children were.

Portraits, coins and praise formulae made successive rulers resemble one eternal imperial person. Temple accounts treated portraits that answered questions as proof that the stars preserved the imperial office, while sceptics exposed staged responses in several cities. After 247 the Face at Caleran stayed veiled, and the copies in governors' courts, town halls and army standards took its place in provincial life. [[The Atherian Empire – Age of Glory#The Emperor after the Emperor|The Emperor after the Emperor]] describes these answering images and the hunger later writers saw in them.

By 0 AR, imperial religion seemed inseparable from public life. Its temples employed workers, educated children and distributed food while also enforcing the celestial hierarchy of the state. The governor who sat for the pilgrimage at Second Qiryath did so beneath a copy of the emperor's face, with an augur of the Red Star beside him and the charter's clauses in his chancery. The trial and execution of the Redeemer therefore occurred within institutions possessing centuries of confidence and habit.
