# The Painted Reign

The Painted Reign is Caleran's name for the vacancy that followed Olarion's death in 247 BR. It ended the House of Thalion, and it lasted two winters. Three commanders raised their standards that first year, in the western military districts, on the eastern fleet and in the river army that fed Caleran. The capital followed none of them. In the palace audience hall, under the bronze eagle over the door, the accession portrait answered questions in the voice the city expected of "the Emperor". A painting governed the capital through the magistrate Avaren Mott while its clerks counted grain that was not there, and soldiers carried it ahead of the grain searches into the poor districts. Samor Veloran of the river army ended it in 245 by throwing his cloak over the panel. His most famous acts were two refusals: he would not let the portrait speak to him, and he would not let the painters put his own face on it.

From that year the portrait, called the Face, was kept veiled. It was uncovered only at an accession and on the founding anniversary, and always last, after the armies and the senate had already agreed.

## The Face in the audience hall

Every emperor since Meren had been confirmed before the same painted panel at the end of the audience hall: a ruler in the red-bordered mantle, the founder's features softened by a century of retouching, the eyes painted so that they seemed to follow a petitioner from the door to the dais. Each new reign added a new face over the old pose. The panel had begun as one of the painted boards Thalion's companies carried to war in his place, and the standing companies still carried copies on their standard-poles under a bronze eagle. Olarion made the panel the centre of court life. His guards knelt to it at every change of watch. The clerks of the Aurin stores read their tallies before it. His informers swore their denunciations before it, and men who had lost relatives to those denunciations learned to keep their eyes on the floor when they passed.

Olarion was found dead on the floor of the hall in 247. Accounts variously blamed illness, poison and a frightened attendant, and no explanation became conclusive. He had allowed no heir's face to be painted. On the second night of the mourning, a chamberlain followed custom and addressed a question to the portrait about the funeral order. Witnesses in the hall said it answered in a dry, carrying voice, using the formulas of an emperor in audience. It went on answering.

## Three standards and a painting

Avaren Mott, senior magistrate at Caleran, never claimed the throne. He claimed that it was not empty. The senate and the palace guard recognised him as the magistrate governing in the Emperor's name, and the speaking portrait gave that claim a force no senate vote could match. Decrees were read before it. The guard swore in its presence. Within its sight obedience became fervent, and officials who had hesitated over his orders in the morning carried them out at a run in the afternoon.

Daran Thalion, a relative of Cassara, held estates and veteran support in the western military districts. He claimed family right and accused Mott of concealing Olarion's murder. Households that had lost property under the last emperor joined him in expectation of restitution.

Aster Halven commanded ships and eastern garrisons. He had declined Olarion's summons to swear before the portrait and had stayed alive by staying at sea. Provincial ports supported him because he could keep their routes open and promised to reduce Caleran's arrears.

Samor Veloran commanded the central river army and its grain corridor. He had come up through the wardens, the rune-trained engineers who loaded bridges before the Red Star and listened for the stone to speak, and he had stood under most of the arches between the upper Aurin and the sea. His soldiers had never stood before the Face. He promised them pay, the confirmation of local officers and an end to the denunciations Olarion's informers had sworn before the portrait.

## The full granaries

Nobody starved Caleran on purpose. The capital's bread came down the Aurin on contracts that the store clerks let each season, against tallies they read aloud in the audience hall. In the first autumn of the vacancy the chief clerk came to the hall to report the bins a third short and to ask for buying orders. Standing before the Face, he read out full measures. He said afterwards that he had seen the true figures on the tablet in his hand and heard his own voice give the others. The books of the Emperor's granaries showed plenty through two winters. No buying orders went up the river, and the bins emptied behind the ledgers.

Bread prices at Caleran doubled and then doubled again. Samor sent barges down from the river army's own district stores, whose tallies had never been read before anything but a quartermaster. The palace turned them back at the water gate. To take relief grain was to say that the Emperor's granaries were short, and no official who had stood in the hall would sign for it.

## The prince and the admiral

Daran marched towards the Aurin and seized food from towns unwilling to declare for him. One town opened its stores after his men took the families of councillors as hostages. He reached Caleran before any rival and went into the audience hall to be confirmed as heir of the house, expecting the painters to be sent for. The Face told him that the Emperor lived and had no need of an heir. Daran knelt with everyone else in the hall. He gave up his command that day under the portrait's promise of safe custody as the Emperor's kinsman, and was executed several days later on Mott's order, given in the Emperor's name. His veterans outside the walls went home or went over to the river army.

Halven moved against the river ports to force the grain corridor. His fleet won an initial engagement and burned military stores, together with a neighbouring working quarter. Melvo's old naval system had divided command among ports capable of continuing after one loss, and local officers now used those arrangements against Halven. He died during a later battle, in which Samor's only son also fell, and his surviving captains negotiated separately.

## The searches

In the second winter Mott asked the portrait where the grain was. It answered as an emperor must: no granary of the Emperor was empty, and those who said otherwise were hiding it. Mott ordered the searches.

Soldiers went into the poor districts with the portrait carried before them on a litter, so that a household knelt before the door was forced. Within its sight people gave up what they had and named neighbours who had more. The searches found little grain and broke a great many doors. In a river-quarter street, a household whose son had been denounced before the same portrait under Olarion would not kneel when it was carried over their threshold. The soldiers killed the father in the doorway and took the family's stored barley. The street kept the story; later temple accounts dropped it.

Demonstrators gathered outside an inner market to demand the grain the portrait had promised. The litter was brought up, and the soldiers who killed them did so in its sight. That killing ended Mott's claim to civic legitimacy in the districts, though the guard in the palace stayed loyal for some weeks more.

## The arch and the cloak

Samor came down the river in the spring of 245 with barges of grain, having promised the palace guard its arrears. The water gate was still shut to relief. His wardens threw a timber span over it in a night, loaded it at first light before a priest of the Red Star until a brace cracked, and replaced the brace. Samor stood beneath the span while the first grain wagons rolled over his head into the city, as any official stood under a work he opened. The river quarter watched him do it.

In the audience hall he threw his own cloak over the Face before he spoke to the guardsmen, and when the voice began beneath the cloth he refused to be addressed by it. His men called him "the soldier who would not answer". A warden, they said, knew better than to put a question to a thing whose answer he did not want. The guard took its arrears.

Mott was not harmed that day. He had made no decision out of the portrait's sight in two years, and he sat down on the dais steps and had to be led away.

## The settlement

The senate and the principal commands recognised Samor as emperor that summer. He gave his dead rivals unequal funeral honours. Towns that had supplied him received charters and tax relief; those associated with his rivals supplied settlements for soldiers. He took Aren Soreth, a boy of ten and the last child of Cassara's line, into the river camp as his ward.

The painters came to the palace to put the new face over the old pose, as they had at every accession since Meren's. Samor sent them away. No face was painted for him then or afterwards, and under its cloth the Face kept Olarion's.

His first law concerned the portrait. The Face was to stay veiled except at accessions and on the anniversary of the founding, and anyone who questioned it would die. Its custody passed to the First Celebrant of the Common Sky, whose college had refused to perform Mott's rites, and away from the priests of the Red Star, who had performed them. The Celebrants of the Veil, as the custodians came to be called, kept the cloth in place and the keys of the audience hall for the rest of the age.

The first man to die under the law was Avaren Mott. A month after the settlement the Celebrants found him in the hall at night, on his knees before the cloth, asking it where the grain was. Caleran's accounts disagree over what he found there. Partisans of the Celebrants said the cloak had stayed in place and Mott had been talking to a blanket. Old soldiers' tellings said a corner had slipped, and that the portrait was looking at him and answering when the Celebrants came in. Samor had him executed in the forum, and the river quarter turned out to watch.

At every later accession the Face was unveiled last, before a ruler whom the armies and senate had already agreed. The order was first tested in 151 BR, when Orent II died without an heir. The veil covered one portrait. Governors' courts, town halls and army standards had their own copies of the imperial face, kept by priests of the Red Star, and in the following generations those copies multiplied.

## Interpretations

Temple writers held that the stars had preserved the imperial office through the portrait while no emperor lived. Sceptics in the capital said Mott's chamberlains had spoken from behind the panel; the guardsmen who had heard it denied that any man stood there. Provincial households that had suffered under Olarion said that the portrait had grown hungry on his denunciations and went on eating after he died. The store clerks kept a rule of their own: after 245 no tally of the Aurin stores was read aloud in any room that held a picture. The Celebrants never gave an account of what they had covered.

## Later memory

The three standards and the painted face remained a common image for ambition feeding from one body. Plays made the claimants into recognisable types: the talkative magistrate, injured prince, proud admiral and patient soldier. Audiences knew the talkative magistrate was the one who had talked with the portrait. Provincial accounts retained the names of people whose grain and relatives had become part of the contest, and in the river quarter a closed door painted red was for generations a sign that a household would not kneel.
