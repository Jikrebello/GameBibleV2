# Schools, Arts and Imperial Magic

The schools and arts of imperial Atheria developed through temples, households, municipal patronage and specialised colleges. The empire connected teachers and craftspeople who had inherited different regional traditions. Caleran became the largest centre of learning, while provincial cities retained disciplines and styles unavailable in the capital.

## Education

Most children learnt through households and work. They acquired language, counting, songs, religious observance and practical skill from relatives, neighbours and experienced workers. Formal schooling required time and payment which many families could not provide.

Town teachers instructed pupils in reading, calculation and public speech. Advanced schools studied law, medicine, astronomy, philosophy, engineering and textual interpretation. Wealthy households employed tutors, including educated enslaved people whose knowledge did not give them control of their own lives.

Students travelled to distinguished teachers. They rented rooms, formed clubs, attended performances and caused the ordinary disputes of young visitors living away from home. Rival schools competed through public lectures and satirical attacks.

## Libraries and books

Books were copied by hand in temple workshops, private households and municipal libraries. A successful work survived because patrons paid for copies and readers carried them elsewhere. Marginal notes and translations allowed provincial knowledge to enter wider debate.

Archives preserved the records of government, while libraries collected literary and technical works. Some institutions did both. A tax assessment might survive in one building and a poem criticising it in another.

Imperial patronage supported copying and could suppress it. Teachers lost posts, authors went into exile and unwanted manuscripts disappeared from public collections. Private copies and quotations sometimes preserved what the court attempted to remove.

## Theatre, music and visual art

Theatres presented tragedy, comedy, music and civic ceremony. Performers could become celebrated across provinces. Comic characters included boastful officers, clever servants and tax collectors unable to count their own household.

Mosaics and painted walls brought landscapes, animals, voyages and myth into baths and homes. Public monuments represented conquest and imperial generosity. Provincial artists inserted local dress, plants and faces into standard compositions, giving official subjects a distinct regional life.

Athletic contests and racing circuits attracted passionate followings. The emperor Meren's support for provincial racing teams enlarged their prestige. Violence between supporters occasionally required military intervention, but the excitement, skill and loyalty of the contests made them enduring institutions.

## Medicine and natural knowledge

Physicians learnt through apprenticeship, written instruction and observation. Military service brought experience of trauma and disease; city practice dealt with childbirth, infection, diet and the illnesses of dense settlement. Midwives maintained bodies of knowledge partly outside formal schools.

Astronomers observed movement used for calendars, navigation and religious interpretation. Scholars who agreed on a measurement frequently disputed its sacred meaning. Agricultural writers collected provincial knowledge of soil, weather and plants, sometimes crediting an estate owner for practices supplied by workers.

## Rune craft

Warlock rune magic grew from older practical scripts into regulated colleges. Training combined geometry, material preparation, repeated tests and knowledge of the consequences of failure. Engineers used it for signalling, firefighting, lifting, structural work and limited elemental control.

Military colleges restricted destructive applications and served imperial commands. Civil specialists adapted approved methods to mines, harbours and waterworks. A written sign did nothing by prestige alone; material, placement and a trained operator determined whether it functioned.

Accidents killed workers and led to the sealing of several laboratories. Colleges recorded failures because repeating one could destroy a building or open an uncontrolled correspondence. Their surviving sites are dangerous where later looters removed safeguards without understanding the system.

## Inheritance

Thalmyric, Eclessian and Sestaran societies inherited different portions of this learning. Conversion changed the institutions preserving it, while wars and occupation destroyed collections. The extent of loss gives exceptional prestige to complete provincial copies.

The Age of Glory remains visible in celebrated texts and monuments. Its knowledge also survives through recipes, work songs, children's games and craft habits whose users no longer remember an imperial author.
