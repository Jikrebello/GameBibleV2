# The Wars of the Atherian Succession

The Atherian succession wars began with Teren Casvilar's murder on the muster ground below Caleran's river stores in 370 BR and ended twenty years later at a table on the landing of Sarqen. In between, no commander in Atheria dared disband an army. Casvilar had died as Warden of the Red Field, struck down by seven men who had entered their names as his challengers, and soldiers and augurs agreed that the Field stood fouled while any of the seven still lived. The wars became a hunt for seven men through every refuge of the inner sea, and every refuge that sheltered one of them paid for it.

[[Aurelius Thalion]] came out of the wars as founder of the empire without ever commanding in a battle. His companies carried his painted face into the fighting in his place. The war ended when he sat down to eat with carters and fishwives at a table whose oath he swore, three seats from the queen he had come to depose and beside a son he had never seen. He stood on a field only once, on the day it was empty.

## The seven names, 370 BR

As Warden of the Red Field, Casvilar was bound to receive in person any challenger whose name was entered in the arms register kept by Deceran Tolm, quartermaster of the muster ground. He had carried the Field for thirteen years, since his named enemy Drenoc walked into a river ford at Kellovar and was never produced, and in all that time he had been unable to lay down his command or discharge a soldier. In the spring of 370 seven names were entered. Mavren Seneth and Corial Draven led them, and the seventh was a young man of the river halls written down as Drenoc's son. Casvilar came down to the muster ground to meet them, because a Warden who beat his enemy's son could close the old Field at last. They stabbed him before the contest was opened.

Caleran had lived beside an empty Field before. When Vespen Harod died in his tent around 441, still Warden, the enemies he had named were alive at Avesh and had never come to meet him. His Field lay vacant for two generations, a challenge nobody had answered, and magistrates raised armies and paid them off as they always had. Casvilar's own Field against Drenoc died with him. What the seven left on the muster ground was another thing. A Warden had been cut down on his own ground by seven men at once, before any contest opened, and the camps round Caleran settled what that meant before any college could: the ground was fouled, and blood shed out of bounds would be made up in bounds, wherever armed men stood, until the men who owed it were dead. Men who had followed Casvilar for twenty years would not go home and leave his killers walking.

[[Darion Velsar]], his senior lieutenant, held Casvilar's papers and the loyalty of officers who had served under both men, and claimed the Wardenship as his by seniority. Aurelius Thalion, Casvilar's sister's son and designated heir, reached Caleran four days after the killing and was taken to see the sand where his uncle had fallen. The next morning he refused the Field in the forum. It had killed his uncle, he said, as surely as the knives had, and he would not stand on it. Veterans jeered him. The senate's speaker, Belisar Venn, backed him all the same, reckoning that an heir who refused the army's rite would need the senate more than Velsar ever would. Thalion's mother Lysara sold the family's town houses to pay men her brother had promised land.

## Darsil, 369 BR

The seven had friends in Caleran and armies in the provinces. Mavren Seneth and Corial Draven understood the Field as well as anyone. While one of them lived, the armies hunting them could not safely disband, and armies that could not disband would eat the country that fed them and turn it against their masters. The seven did not give battle. They scattered to the refuges of the inner sea and left their officers and provincial friends to fight.

Those friends held the approaches around Darsil, an Atherian supply town on the mainland routes behind the Thalassian ports. Velsar went after them at once and broke them there in the summer of 369, after securing a road that let his own provisions through and threatened the defenders' stores. It was the largest battle of the wars, and it came in their second year. Afterwards his soldiers searched the dead and the prisoners for the seven and found none of them. Some defeated officers took service with the victors; others fled to the sea.

Velsar's men had been told the Field would close when the republican army fell. He paid off two of Casvilar's oldest companies on the battlefield, the first soldiers anyone had discharged since Kellovar, and sent them home to the upper Aurin with their bounty. By midwinter their valley was at war. The seven's partisans burned a veterans' village, the veterans burned two in return, and then the discharged men fought each other over the dead men's farms. Both companies came back to the standards in the spring, the only place, they said, where a man knew who was trying to kill him. No commander paid off a company after that, and no company asked.

One of the seven was killed at his own door in Caleran in the winter of 369 by veterans who had waited a week in the street for him. He was the last of them to die in the city, because the others had already gone. Deceran Tolm, who had entered the names and fixed the day, was taken on the coast road in 368 and killed. His family said he had only kept the book.

## The quartered winters, 369–366 BR

An army that could not disband could not be settled either. There were no veteran grants while the Field lay fouled, so Velsar quartered his victorious companies on Darsil itself and went east to take the ports and the royal clients. The town fed his soldiers through one winter, went short through the next and starved through the third. In the spring of 366 the bake-house women barred the ovens, and men of the lower town killed soldiers in the lanes at night. Velsar's brother Tarsen, who commanded the garrison, took what grain was left into the citadel and hanged eleven organisers in the market. The town gave in when its children began to die.

Velsar's wife Miretha Ardel came east that summer with carts of grain bought with her own estates and fed the town until the harvest. She came back with more every autumn for ten years, selling a farm for each bad season, and she died at Darsil of the winter fever in 356 with nothing left to sell. Darsil remembered Tarsen's gallows and Miretha's carts for three hundred years, often in the same song. Velsar had sent neither the order nor the grain. He had sent no countermand either.

## The Four at the Stores, 367 BR

In 367 [[Seluc Varroth]], First Celebrant of the Common Sky, gave the ruling every commander had been waiting for. A Warden's acceptance, he declared, belonged to the man who had stood bareheaded before the Red Star and could no more be inherited than a wound, and a contest fouled before its opening could be closed by no one who had not fought in it. Velsar's claim failed, though he had won the only great battle anyone had fought for it. So did any hope of a quick end. The Field stood fouled and unclaimed, and Darsil was already showing the country what an army did when it could not go home.

Armies that could not go home still had to eat. Four men took charge of their bread: Velsar, who commanded the largest of them; Thalion, who had Casvilar's money and his veterans' promises; Varroth, who held the magazines and passes between the Aurin and the eastern roads; and Belisar Venn, who kept the river stores for the senate. They met in the store-house above the muster ground, among the grain bins and the tally clerks, and soldiers stood in the aisles of the assembly while it confirmed what the store-house had decided. Caleran called them the Four at the Stores.

They divided the country by what it could feed. Velsar's armies took the eastern ports and their client grain, Thalion's companies the Aurin and the outer-coast districts, and Varroth's garrisons the magazines along the passes. The river stores fed the capital and the camps around it. Every barge that came down the Aurin was counted twice, once by Venn's clerks and once by soldiers.

## The Writing-In, 367–364 BR

The partners needed money and they needed their enemies dead, and the soldiers balked. Men who had fought other Atherians in the field would not drag a senator out of his dining room. Velsar found a way round their scruple. Augurs had counted men as contestants before: nine senators in Harod's day, Casvilar's banker, the senate's signatories of 377. The seven names stood in Tolm's register, which now lay with Varroth's augurs, and above them stood the men Casvilar had pardoned in 371, each with a line through his name, still legible. A man named in that book was a challenger on the fouled Field, and killing him was war, not murder. Velsar proposed that the lines be read as never drawn, and that other names be written in below.

They did. Through the winter of 367 the augurs entered the names the partners brought them, and squads took the named men from their houses, their farms and the boats they hid in, and killed them on the muster ground so that the deaths would count as deaths on the Field. Each name struck, the soldiers were told, brought them nearer home. Boys were dragged down with their fathers. Families paid augurs to keep a name out of the book or to put a rival's in, and buyers bid for the estates of men not yet dead. Some enslaved attendants hid their owners in grain pits and drains; others led the squads to the door or used the disorder to escape. The Aurin below the stores ran red that season, and the river people would not draw water there for a generation. Varroth, for his part, consented to the writing-in of a cousin whose household had sheltered one of the seven.

## Venn's sword, 366 BR

In the second winter of the writing-in, Velsar asked for Belisar Venn's name. Venn had spoken against the writing-in in the store-house, and Velsar wanted the river stores out of the senate's hands. Thalion consented, and Varroth's clerk wrote the name.

Venn did not wait to be fetched. He was sixty-eight. He walked down to the muster ground in the morning with a blunt practice sword borrowed from his grandson, stood bareheaded where Casvilar had stood and waited. Nine soldiers came. The first to reach him took the blunt edge across the eyes and went down, and the other eight stopped. They were Casvilar's veterans. They had watched seven men cut down one on that sand, and they had eaten Venn's bread through three winters. Their file-leader said he would not make it nine. They picked up their comrade and walked off the ground, and Venn stood there until dusk, when his grandson came to take him home. Varroth's clerk, asked what to write against the name, wrote *stood*.

After that, every written-in man who could lift a blade asked for one, and squads that had cut down kneeling men found they were being asked to fight. Some refused. The names came more slowly. Venn went back to the river stores the next morning, and no soldier in Caleran would have carried his name out a second time. Thalion walked to his house that week and was kept standing in the street until dark. What the two men said is not recorded. Venn's clerks went on feeding Thalion's companies on the day promised. Velsar's waited for whatever barges could be spared, and Velsar looked east for his bread, to the ports he held and to the queen of Sarqen.

The writing-in was supposed to bring the Field's closing nearer. The war grew instead. Eastern cities that had paid both sides chose one; provincial commands that had sat still marched; two foraging columns sent into different valleys met on the same narrow bridge in the eastern hills and fought until neither could cross. In the winter of 364 Varroth's own pass garrisons, unpaid since the autumn, fought each other over the magazines at the head of the Aurin road and burned them, and Varroth lost the stores on which his place among the partners rested. Old soldiers said the Red Star had been offered helpless men instead of fighters and was finding its own contests, and that the one man who had stood was the one man it had let go. The Common Sky's augurs called it disorder and nothing more. Augurs and soldiers argued over it for centuries. No one wrote in another name after 364.

## The hunt through the refuges

The seven went where Atherian law could not follow. Refuge was older than the republic, and the Chain Peace of 461 had made it law at sea: every harbour must take in a ship in distress, and the custodians of Ilamar heard the complaints. The partners had to break a refuge or buy it before they could reach a man inside it, and each refuge answered differently.

The young man entered as Drenoc's son went south to Darneth, to the forest people whose men Casvilar had maimed after the siege of 380. They took in their enemy's killer gladly, whoever's son he was, and he died among them of a winter fever around 362. Another of the seven reached Vorthara, where the households of the Kept Doors had held their doors against Lethren Ostar's agents in 410. They held them again against Velsar's, and when the man died of age behind them in 356, they sent his body down the pass on a mule so that his name could be struck. Iren Sallas sat for eight years in the sanctuary precinct on Ilamar, where Marovan Veyr had died, while Thalion's men waited on the quay; the custodians' court wrote the certificates that fed Thalion's armies, and he would not storm it. In 359, having heard what Darsil had suffered, Sallas walked out of the precinct and down to the quay to them, so that he would not be the reason the war went on. Corial Draven went to sea, to Ardel Veyr's fleet and the moving city of Marelith. Another went east to Avesh, the Vasharan road-city that had sheltered Caleran's fugitives since Vespen Harod's day.

Mavren Seneth went to Sarqen. He came to the landing in 362 on a carter's wagon and sat down at the spared towns' unranked table, from which no stranger who reached it could be sold. The towns fed him. Queen [[Samyra Nerethan]] kept him, because the oath on which her crown stood forbade her to do otherwise, and because while he lived the Atherians could not disband the armies that bought her grain. When the partners demanded him in 361, she answered them with the oath.

## The painted board

Thalion went on no campaign. Through twenty years of war he stayed at the river stores, where he paid, wrote, remembered the names of men he had met once, and sent other men to command. The companies he paid marched behind a painted board of his face on a pole, so that soldiers who had never seen him could swear to him. In the eastern camps such a board was simply called a thalion. Soldiers mocked an heir who sent his picture to war, and kept swearing to it, because the man behind it paid on the day he promised.

## Merath and the child the towns counted, 361–358 BR

Thalion's first wife, Ilvara Deneth, died in 361 giving birth to their daughter. Within the year he married again, and his choice made the war personal. Merath Nerethan was Samyra's elder sister, passed over when the towns restored Samyra in 373 and since then a claimant in search of a patron. She found one at Caleran. Velsar's bread came from one daughter of King Olymes; Thalion had now married the other.

In 359 Merath went east to Esharat, the inland town of her ancestor Hadrima, to bear her child where the heirs of the house were born and to be seen there by the towns that had passed her over. Her son was born in the spring of 358, and she named him Meren. Before midsummer the queen's officers came up from Sarqen with a litter and two hundred spearmen to bring mother and child to the palace; for their safety, the queen's letter said. The women of Esharat would not hand the boy over at the door. The elders made the officers sit down at the town's table and eat, and then told them what the oath said: *no child for the table*. The queen might keep her sister's son. She would show him at the landing every spring, where the towns could see that he was whole.

Samyra kept the bargain. Each spring Merath's son sat at the renewal of the oath, first on a nurse's knee and later on the bench among the carters' children, and the elders of Esharat looked him over as they looked over their sealed jars. Merath lived in a wing of the palace with a guard on the stair. Thalion's wife and his younger son were in the keeping of his partner's ally, and for five years that held the store-house together better than any oath between the partners had. He sent Velsar's share of the Aurin grain without argument, wrote to the queen each year in his own hand to ask after the boy, and waited. Whether Samyra meant the child for a hostage, an heir or a nephew is still argued. The towns believed the first, and counted him carefully.

## Marelith and the guests of Pyrassa, 355–353 BR

Ardel Veyr, Marovan Veyr's son, held the sea between the partners and their grain. He sheltered people who had fled the writing-in, Corial Draven among them, and seized merchant ships to feed his fleet. He hired [[Marelith]], the harbour-city carried on a living reefback, for ships and stores; its captains kept their own government. Sailors had called it "the harbour without a chain" since the gale of 466, when it took in the crews shut out of the League's harbours, and families fleeing the writing-in had gone there for the same reason. In 355 the city followed Ardel east of its ordinary circuit into the central Thalassian anchorages, where the water was shallow.

[[Istran Melvo]], Thalion's admiral, had grown up in the shipyards of Pyrassa, and he brought Pyrassa's great harbour chain with him. Marelith's founding covenant forbade it a permanent anchorage. Melvo's crews laid the chain across the shallows where the bearer fed and drew it tight between two sunken hulks. Accounts describe an apparent prize ship admitted near the city, carrying hidden attackers and the sea-fire whose making was a guarded Atherian secret. Held fast, the bearer fought the chain. The city's attachments tore, its lamps and cookfires spilled into the living works, and the fire ran along the shell. People died in the fire and smoke, were lost when structures tore away, or drowned when the vessels separated. The burned mass grounded as the [[Black Reef]]. Draven died aboard. Melvo's fleet broke Ardel's main force nearby in the same operation.

Ardel got clear of the anchorages with eleven hulls. He took aboard what the sea had left of the city's people and ran before a rising wind for the nearest great harbour that kept a refuge fire. It was Pyrassa. The harbour whose chain had held Marelith had no chain that night, since it lay across a shallow in the central sea, fouled with burnt shell. Pyrassa's wardens remembered the gale of 466, when their grandfathers had closed the harbour on Caleran's order and found it shut against their own grain ships until they cut the chain. At the Chain Peace they had sworn never to raise it against a vessel in distress, and they had already lent it once that year for a purpose they did not like to name. They lit the fire and let the burned hulls in.

So the people of the harbour without a chain were lodged in the boat sheds of the city whose chain had killed it, and Ardel Veyr sat on the mole of Melvo's home port, a guest under the old law of refuge. Thalion wrote ordering Melvo to take him. Melvo wrote back that he had carried his city's chain to sea and left it under a reef, and he would not carry off its guests as well; if Thalion wanted Ardel, he could come to Pyrassa and take him from the quay himself. It was the admiral's first quarrel with Thalion. Thalion had no other admiral, and he let the letter lie.

Ardel stayed. In 353 Calvena Casvilar, Thalion's cousin and Ardel's stepmother, the one person both men would receive, crossed to Pyrassa and made terms. Ardel gave up his remaining hulls and swore never to pass the mole towers. His captains could sail under Melvo or go home. The people of Marelith who had reached the refuge fire kept their freedom and the sheds they slept in, which became Pyrassa's Burnt Quarter. Those whom Melvo's crews had taken from the water at the anchorages had already been sold, and no terms brought them back. Ardel lived out his life on the mole. Atherian accounts celebrated the end of a pirate nest. The survivors and the Elmarai pilots called the burning a second Kelarion, and sailors said that the harbour without a chain had been killed with one.

## The names read at Caleran, 354 BR

Five of the seven were dead. One lived at Avesh under Vasharan protection. The other sat at a table in Sarqen, and Velsar's own ally kept him there.

In 354 the treasury advocate Corven Ilar rose in the assembly at Caleran and asked it to declare the queen of Sarqen an enemy. Most of the city assumed he spoke for Thalion. Samyra, he said, was queen of the child-burning city, heir of the Bronze-Faced Provider, keeper of Casvilar's murderer and gaoler of Thalion's wife and son. Envoys from the spared towns answered him in the same assembly. They recited the Table Oath, which every Nerethan ruler had sworn at the towns' unranked table since 457 BR: *no child for the table; no seat kept empty; no guest served by worth.* Then they read out the names of the Gateway hostage children whom Caleran itself had killed on its own festival ground in 609 BR to win the Red Star's favour against Zamaret, two years after their towns had handed them over as a pledge of peace. The names took some time to read. Thalion, it was noticed, stood while they were read.

The assembly did not vote, and Ilar's motion was never put again. Some said Thalion had stood because his own son sat under that oath's protection. When war with the queen came, a year later, Velsar began it.

## The road that outran Velsar, 354 BR

Velsar's power in the east now rested on Samyra. She supplied food, ships and credit; he supplied soldiers and protection against her neighbours' claims. His veterans liked none of it. Many had served under Casvilar, and their general was guarding Casvilar's murderer at the queen's table. They had begun to slip away to Thalion, who paid on the day.

In the spring of 354 Velsar set out to fetch them the other murderer. He marched on Avesh and found the gates open and the Vasharan governor polite. King Orvaz lent Velsar money and had no wish to see either Atherian finish the other. The guest, the governor regretted, had left the day before, up the Dawn Road, with a change of horses waiting at every station.

Velsar followed. At each relay house the hearth was warm and the man was a stage ahead; the riders of the road passed him from post to post faster than an army could water its animals. Velsar chased one man with an army through a whole summer. He turned back when the remounts he had bought on the way out were offered to him again at twice the price. He had fought no battle and lost no company. He came home with foundered horses and empty hands, and the camps made a marching song that named the stations in order. His brother Tarsen, who had hanged the organisers at Darsil, rode beside him the whole way and is in the song as well.

After that road Velsar needed the queen more than ever. Their children Talven and Lethia were born in 353 and 351 and joined Amesar, her son by Casvilar, and her nephew Meren in the royal household.

## The Salt Field, 353 BR

In the autumn of 353 Velsar answered the deserters with the rite they understood. On the salt pans below Sarqen, where the towns boiled the salt that stood on their oath table, he dedicated his arms before an altar of the Red Star and named Aurelius Thalion and Istran Melvo as his enemies. He had sent to Caleran for a witness no soldier could call his creature, and Seluc Varroth came east: the First Celebrant of the Common Sky himself, with Tolm's register in a chest. Varroth had lost his magazines in 364 and had since watched the priests of the Red Star, who blessed Thalion's boards, take his college's place in Thalion's camps. By his own ruling only a man who fought could close the Field, and Velsar was the one partner who would stand on one. He proclaimed that the Star had accepted Velsar, and he stayed.

Velsar had made himself Warden of a new field, and he meant it to force Thalion to come to him. A man who would not meet a named enemy in person, Velsar believed, could not keep an Atherian army.

Thalion did not come. He had grown up on the story of Vespen Harod, who lived twenty-five years in a tent on the muster ground while the enemies he had named lived comfortably at Avesh. Thalion's companies came to the coast behind his painted boards and fought Velsar's outposts, though his wife and son lived in the palace above the pans; the towns had the boy under their oath, he told his sister, and the queen was no fool. Varroth declared that a board could not contest a field. Velsar was bound to the salt all the same, because the man he had named was still at war with him, and a Warden could not leave his field while his named enemy contested it. The towns called it the Salt Field.

## The Fourth Collection, 352–351 BR

An army that could not leave its ground had to be fed on it. In 352 Melvo's ships closed Sarqen's outer anchorage, and the queen's officers turned inland. They took grain, oil, animals and young men from the towns for the Salt Field, by what each household could be made to give, and on the field itself the officers' tables stayed full while the Gateway levies went hungry. The towns gave it a name from the worst years of their history: the Fourth Collection.

That autumn the carters of Esharat and Belqara opened their sealed jars at the count and found them a third empty, every seal whole and the underside of the wax bitten, as their ancestors' jars had been in the years when the Provider's tables at Qarzeth never ran low. The queen's officers blamed thieves. The towns said the Provider had come back to a table that served by worth.

That winter a mule train came down from Avesh with a body packed in salt and a letter from the road command: the guest had fallen from a remount at a station far up the Dawn Road. Officers who had known the man said it was he. Others said that one salted corpse looks much like another, and that Orvaz had judged the Atherians ready for peace and made them a present. Varroth struck the sixth name from the register on the Salt Field. By the book six of the seven were dead, and the seventh ate at the queen's table within sight of the pans.

In 351 the queen's pilots made a bargain with something in the sea. Grain hulls from the Qahuri river lay waiting beyond Melvo's line, and the bargain bought a current to carry them in past his ships to the landing at Hazreth. The price was cargo surrendered and a disused galley basin opened to the sea. The sea took its cargo first. One hull in three was emptied over the side in the roads, and the carters of Hazreth stood on the mole and watched a winter's bread go into the water. Then the basin was opened. The wounded from the Salt Field and the harbour fights lay in its sheds, and carts had been assigned to move them. The queen's officers used the carts for the grain and left the wounded on the quay as the water came in. Some drowned in the sheds. A rower from Hazreth named Tobal lay two nights on the quay with the tide over his legs and lost one of them. The grain went up to the Salt Field and the officers' tables. The royal account of the passage did not name the people left behind, and the towns heard about the quay from the men who had lain on it.

Merath Nerethan died in the palace that winter, of the fever that came with the hunger. She had lived seven years behind the guarded stair. The blockade her husband had ordered killed her, and the queen wrote to tell him so in her own hand.

## The guest at the table, 350 BR

Every spring the towns renewed the oath at Sarqen's landing, on long boards with no high end, each family bringing what food it had. In the spring of 350 Mavren Seneth sat at that table for the twelfth time. Amesar, Samyra's son by Casvilar, was twenty-two and had watched his father's killer eat at his mother's table since he was ten. He stood up from the boards, walked down the table past the carriers and the fishwives, and killed Seneth with the knife he had brought to cut bread. He said afterwards that he had meant to end the war: Thalion had come for Seneth, and now there was nothing left to come for.

The towns had risen against a minister in 373 for laying a table to kill a guest. Now a son of the house had killed one at the table itself. Some elders wanted Amesar judged as Vardek had been. Others, descendants of Sapha of Esharat who had set the oath's rules, said the towns would not pay for their table with a child of the house, however grown. In the end they did not touch him. They put the house out instead. When the boards were laid again a week later, the seat where the queen had always sat was filled by Tobal of Hazreth, the rower left on the basin quay. No town in the Gateway country would seat a Nerethan as its ruler after that, and Amesar they would not seat at all. Without the towns the queen had no grain, no carters and no gates.

Amesar took passage on a fishing boat to Ilamar and entered the precinct disarmed, as Marovan Veyr had in 373. The towns sent to Thalion. The landing would be open to him, they said, if he came to it himself, sat at their table and swore their oath. His son had eaten at it eight springs running.

## The end of the Salt Field

Velsar's army was gone within the month, because nobody would feed it. He could not follow it. Thalion still contested his field, and a Warden may not flee. He stayed on the salt with his brother Tarsen, a few officers who would not leave him, and the First Celebrant. Thalion wanted him left there until he gave up his claim before the Red Star's priests, which would have ended the war with a confession. Melvo, whom Velsar had also named, thought it a shameful way to treat a soldier. He came ashore and walked out onto the salt in person with forty of his crewmen, and Velsar and his officers went to meet them. Velsar was killed with a sword in his hand on the field he had chosen, and Tarsen beside him.

Varroth walked out with them, sixty-four years old, with the register under his arm, because a contest needed its witness. A crewman who had never seen a Celebrant's cap cut him down. Melvo's men said afterwards that the old man had held a blade. The Common Sky's augurs said he had held a book. When the fighting was over nobody could find the register. Melvo's crews swore they had left it lying on the salt, and the salt-boilers of Hazreth say it is under the pans still. Thalion never forgave Melvo for that morning, and the sailors' songs never forgave Thalion.

## The table at the landing

Thalion crossed to Sarqen in the summer of 350 on Melvo's ships. Most people on the landing had never seen him: a heavy, short-sighted man of forty, plainer than his boards.

The towns laid the boards at the landing. Before he came ashore, Samyra Nerethan walked down from the palace with Talven by the hand, Lethia on her hip and her sister's son beside her, and sat at the table, not in the queen's seat, which Tobal held, but in a free place among the carters, as any stranger might. The oath forbade the towns to sell a stranger from their table, and it would bind Thalion too once he had sworn it. He had meant to take her to Caleran. He sat down three places from her, beside a salt-boiler from Hazreth. The boy on the bench between them was eight years old and had never seen his father, and he asked the queen, in the towns' speech, who the fat man was. Thalion ate what was put in front of him and swore: *no child for the table; no seat kept empty; no guest served by worth.*

The terms were made at the same table. The kingdom ended, and Sarqen passed under an imperial governor, who was to come down to the landing every spring and swear the oath before the towns would supply him. Samyra and her children would stay in the Gateway country as guests of the towns, and no one would take them from it. Meren went home with his father, recognised as Merath's son and an heir of both houses; the elders of Esharat asked that he come back to the table when he was grown, and he did. At the next count, the carters said, the jars at Esharat were full to their marks and the wax unbitten. Atherian writers called the meal the Concord of the Landing. The towns remembered it as the day the conqueror came to dinner.

## The settlement at Caleran

Thalion returned to Caleran through the eastern and highland routes. Every one of the seven was dead. He went down to the muster ground below the river stores, where Casvilar had died, and stood there bareheaded. It was the only field he ever stood on.

The Common Sky had no First Celebrant to speak, and its augurs would not speak in a dead man's place. Varroth's ruling of 367 said that only a man who had fought in the contest could close it. Thalion had fought in nothing, and the register in which the seven names should have been struck was lost in the salt. One man in Caleran had answered to his name on that ground and walked off it. Belisar Venn was eighty-four. Thalion sent to ask whether he would say the words. Venn sent back his grandson's practice sword, without a message. Some took it for a blessing and some for a challenge. Thalion hung it in the store-house and never said which.

The priests of the Red Star, who had blessed Thalion's painted boards for fifteen years, declared the Field closed and its charge taken into his command. Thalion gave them first place in every ceremony of victory and command from that day. The Common Sky's college chose a new Celebrant and walked second, and its augurs held ever afterwards that the Field of 370 had never been closed, only emptied.

Thalion made no pretence that the republic had come back. He governed from the store-house above the muster ground, where the Four had sat in 367, and left the senate the markets, festivals and water of the city of Caleran. The armies that had not dared disband for twenty years were disbanded in four, onto the Noon Roll lands, the estates of the written-in and farms whose owners had fled the fighting. Soldiers received land and pay on the day promised. Families driven off that land petitioned the victors for generations.

For the Aurin farmers who had fed two armies a year, peace meant one collector instead of several and roads full of carts instead of columns. Those conditions formed the opening of [[The Atherian Empire – Age of Glory|the Age of Glory]].
