# Food, Trade and Enslavement in Imperial Atheria

Imperial Atheria connected farms, workshops, mines and ports across the central known world. Its roads and shipping allowed large cities to draw food and materials from distant provinces. The system supported specialised crafts and substantial prosperity while exposing suppliers to decisions made far from their homes.

## Grain and the cities

Caleran required regular deliveries from the Aurin, eastern river provinces, Sarqen and maritime markets. Provincial towns maintained their own stores before meeting imperial assessments. Drying, turning and protecting grain from vermin employed large numbers of workers.

Public distributions supported citizens and stabilised prices. Eligibility depended on recognised residence and status. Migrants and enslaved people lived beside distribution points whose rations excluded them.

A failed harvest became an imperial concern when it threatened troops or major cities. Relief could save lives, as at Raveth, while loans and land transfers changed who controlled cultivation afterwards.

## Farms and estates

Most food came from households whose members combined cultivation with animal keeping, craft and seasonal labour. Great estates gathered adjoining land, water rights and dependent workers. Some belonged to private families, others to temples or the emperor.

Enslaved labourers worked on estates, in mines, workshops and domestic households. War and punishment supplied people to markets. Sale separated parents, children and spouses. Mining and large rural gangs imposed the harshest conditions. Skilled urban workers sometimes possessed greater freedom of movement, although their labour remained an owner's property.

Manumission allowed some former slaves to establish shops, marry freely and obtain civic standing for their children. Owners used it as reward and incentive. It changed individual lives while the violence sustaining the market continued.

## Trade and craft

Ports carried grain, salt, wine, timber, metal, cloth, ceramics, books and enslaved people. Merchants formed partnerships to distribute the risk of loss. Pilots and resident harbour workers possessed knowledge that an investor at Caleran could not replace.

Standard measures and dependable roads helped buyers compare goods. Taxes, tolls and official privileges shaped which route remained profitable. Smuggling prospered where a restricted cargo still had willing customers.

Craft districts produced ordinary tools alongside luxury objects. Apprenticeship transferred skill through households. Women owned or managed some shops and transport interests; freed families became prominent in trades that older aristocrats considered beneath them.

## Inns, baths and pleasures

Movement created a world of inns, eating houses, bath attendants, animal yards and roadside shrines. Travellers exchanged news and songs. Foods from one province became fashionable in another, then changed as cooks used local ingredients.

Baths provided washing, exercise, conversation and business. Festivals filled streets with temporary stalls and performers. Gambling, theatre and racing drew all social classes into shared excitement, though wealth determined comfort and protection from loss.

## The price of connection

A road could bring medicine and soldiers to a threatened town. It could also remove its grain, carry captives away and let an estate owner supervise land from a distant city. Imperial prosperity joined material abundance to the power governing its movement.

Households responded through cooperation, bargaining, concealment, migration and revolt. Their work made the monuments and intellectual life of the Glory possible.
