# The Three Collections and the Fall of Qarzeth

The **Years of Three Collections**, approximately 548–540 BR, name the most extensive child-taking campaigns of the Gateway Republic. After two lost wars, Qarzeth had recovered with a speed its enemies could not explain: its granaries stood full while the stores of its dependent towns emptied. Priests of the Bronze-Faced Provider taught that the plenty required an irreplaceable return, and civic officials carried the demand into towns that had once offered animals or money.

The collections became a principal cause of Caleran's final war against Qarzeth. Internal opponents supplied testimony, guided rescuers and helped the besiegers. Before the war Qarzeth's assembly surrendered the collectors' books to Caleran, and the sanctuary's doors would not open without them; hunger undid the surrender within a month. The capital fell as its people's stories say a hungry god falls: its carriers stopped, its dead were named, its face was broken and its survivors ate together. Caleran ended the sacrificial institution and then destroyed the city, murdering, raping and enslaving inhabitants during the sack. The lesser Gateway towns survived and inherited both histories.

## The civic sanctuary and its plenty

The Provider's sanctuary stood in Qarzeth's upper district. Its estates owned workshops, gardens, stores and serving chambers. Priests fed the city at festivals and financed public works, and leading households gained prestige by supporting it. These services made the institution part of civic life long before its last decades.

The plenty was real. After the defeats of 646 and 601, Qarzeth's storehouses filled faster than any harvest could fill them, and the city rebuilt fleets in seasons that should have taken years. The same years emptied the dependent towns. At Esharat and Belqara, jars sealed full at harvest stood half empty in spring with their seals unbroken. Around 551 the custodians of [[Ilamar before 350 BR|Ilamar]] recorded those findings and refused to certify the sanctuary's grain. Gateway farmers had a name for the thing: stores that emptied without a thief.

The cult taught that every provision demanded a larger return, and that the largest return was a child. Human sacrifice had occurred at changing frequency across the Republic's history. Families offered infants in moments of fear; officials took older captives, dependants and children bought from poor households. Wealthy patrons sometimes surrendered their own children and sometimes bought substitutes. By the 550s the sanctuary's doors opened only when a living name had been entered on the collectors' household lists, and soldiers enforced the lists in the dependent towns.

The Provider appeared to its worshippers in smoke, dreams and the long tables of its festivals. Priests shaped the doctrine, magistrates authorised the collectors, patrons paid and guards held the victims. In the last collection, people in the capital said the festival tables grew longer as the guests grew fewer.

## Ilya and Sapha

Collectors travelled with guards and household lists. Families hid children in orchards, cargo and relatives' houses. Neighbours informed for payment or to protect their own children. Punishments included confiscation, imprisonment and the seizure of another family member.

**Ilya of Qarzeth** worked in the sanctuary stores. Before the third collection she copied the names of imprisoned children onto packing strips, taking them from the collectors' own lists, and escaped in a grain cart. Her list contained errors and omissions, but it named holding rooms, officials and the districts that knew what was happening.

At Esharat, the midwife **Sapha of Esharat** organised carriers, fishing households and rural kin into a rescue route. They moved children beneath produce and through irrigation channels. The route saved dozens and failed many others. Collectors killed Sapha's husband during a search and took her eldest daughter, **Neral**. Neral was sacrificed before the rescuers reached Qarzeth.

The opponents disagreed about their purpose. Ilya wanted the sanctuary dismantled and the Republic preserved. Sapha wanted Qarzeth's authority broken. Belqara's leaders sought independence, while exiles called for the city's destruction. Their testimony nevertheless established that the killings belonged to a public institution protected by the Republic.

## Debate in Caleran

Escaped families carried their evidence to Atherian ports. The senator **Orsen Valeor** placed Ilya's list and the survivors beside him while demanding that the child-taking city fall. He sincerely opposed the sacrifices, and he also believed that a city which recovered from every defeat threatened every Atherian alliance. His coalition included people seeking rescued children, safer shipping, land, profit and military distinction.

Valeor spoke in a city that had killed Gateway children of its own. In 609, during the war against Zamaret Ordan, Caleran had offered Gateway hostages, children of about eight to sixteen, to the Red Star on its festival ground. Their houses had delivered them two years earlier as proof that Qarzeth wanted peace. When Valeor demanded Qarzeth's destruction, Gateway exiles in Caleran answered by reading out those children's names. Senator **Mera Castren** drew the opposite lesson from the same ground: Caleran had stopped, so Qarzeth could stop too. She proposed inspectors and the compulsory closure of the sanctuary, and she cited Ilamar's record of the sealed jars to argue that the towns, given protection, would starve the cult themselves. Her first draft had asked for permanent hostages; she struck the clause after the names were read. Gateway opponents of the cult supported parts of her proposal because annihilation would punish the families who had resisted. Valeor answered that the rooms would reopen after an Atherian withdrawal. The senate preferred the policy that needed no army, and around 528 Castren carried the vote.

## The eleven chests

Castren sailed to Qarzeth with a fleet and three demands. The city gave up the children then held in the sanctuary, and its warships and arms. The third demand was for the collectors' books. These were the household lists, kept quarter by quarter and town by town, on which a living child's name had to be entered before the sanctuary's doors would open. The assembly argued for nine days. Citizens who had hidden their own children voted with merchants who feared the fleet in the roads, and the books were carried down to the quay in eleven chests.

The sanctuary's doors stayed shut at the next opening and the one after. The city had eaten from the Provider's granaries for two generations, and few citizens could say how it would eat without them. Within a month the lower markets had no bread. The priests and the cult's patrons called the hungry up to the sanctuary district, and the crowd killed the two Harbour Judges who had sealed the surrender on the sanctuary stair. Collectors began new books that week in the poorest streets of the capital itself, since the towns' households were now beyond their reach. The doors opened. Sanctuary store-workers who had known Ilya took Castren and the chests out through the harbour at night and put them ashore at Belqara.

In Caleran Valeor said that the Provider had answered her for him. The senate voted for war, and its envoys carried no fourth demand. Castren kept the eleven chests at Esharat, where they were later set beside Ilya's list.

## Siege and rescue

The defeat came in the order the Gateway country still tells it.

**The carts stopped.** Between about 536 and 531 the carriers of Belqara, Esharat, Hazreth and Sarqen had camped on the rope-makers' ground and refused to carry grain and children into the capital. The council swore to their terms and broke its oath within the year, when the sanctuary's doors stayed shut for want of the towns' names. Collectors returned behind Meleq Ordan's crews, the camp's leaders were executed at their own doors, and the towns went over to Caleran. In the siege that began in 525, nothing came in by land or sea. For the first time in living memory, Qarzeth went hungry. The priests killed more children, taken now from the capital's own households, and the storehouses stayed empty. Hunger killed children, old people and enslaved labourers first in the crowded districts. Citizens who tried to overthrow the priesthood were killed in the streets below the sanctuary, and opponents of sacrifice defended their homes beside cult patrons when the wall was attacked. Belqara and Esharat supplied the Atherians under agreements promising their survival; Sarqen admitted refugees and repaired allied ships.

**The dead were named.** Ilya guided an Atherian detachment towards the holding rooms after the wall was breached in 520 BR. Soldiers killed the guards and found living children beside cremated remains. Because Ilya's list had been copied from the collectors' own lists, it matched remains, clothing and tokens to names, and the surrendered books at Esharat supplied the households of children her strips had missed. Parents recovered some living children and identified the belongings of others. The dead were later buried by name at Esharat.

**The face was broken.** Soldiers broke the Provider's bronze image and pulled down the serving chambers. Atherian annals credit a soldier with an axe. Esharat credits Sapha's carriers. Ilya's surviving fragment says that "it broke when the last name was spoken".

Across the rest of the city, Atherian soldiers murdered residents, raped captives and organised survivors for sale. Some rescued children were separated again during the distribution of prisoners, and captains from the wandering harbour-city of [[Marelith]] bought them and resold many to the ransom funds. Officers intervened unevenly, and commanders permitted looting and mass enslavement. Captured priests and officials were executed.

**The survivors ate together.** Some days after the sack the spared people of the Gateway country, rescued children and Atherian deserters among them, shared bread, fish and salt at Sarqen's landing with no one served by rank and no seat kept empty. Sapha set the rules and would not preside. They became the **Table Oath** of the spared towns: *no child for the table; no seat kept empty; no guest served by worth.* [[The Spared Towns and the Queen's Country#The table at the landing|The table at the landing]] describes the meal.

## Destruction and salting

Caleran's senate had decided that Qarzeth would never again become the capital of the Gateway country. Engineers marked the civic squares, elite gardens and selected waterworks. Cisterns were spoiled, channels broken and building stone removed.

The sanctuary's store compounds were handled differently. Grain contractors travelling with the army had the granary doors and bins sealed and set a guard on them, and the spared towns heard that Caleran meant to keep the stores that filled themselves. Valeor spoke against the contractors in the commander's tent and was told the matter was the senate's.

The towns settled it first. Salt came by boat through the captured harbour from the Gateway's own pans at Hazreth and Sarqen. It was the salt that had stood on the table at the landing. Carriers from the two towns laid it over several nights where the plenty had grown: the sanctuary, the gardens and the store compounds. Nothing grows on salted ground, and the towns meant that nothing should ever again be served from it. The soldiers on guard had carried children out of the holding rooms a few days before, and they let the carriers through. The commander reported the work to Caleran as done by his order. Atherian annals record the salting as the punishment of a rebel city. Gateway tradition calls it closing the table: "The salt of the table and the salt of the ruin were one salt."

The salting devastated the urban centre and left most of the surrounding country habitable. Villages, fields and lesser towns continued under new rulers. The Provider lost its sanctuary, its public offerings and its civic protection. Fragments of the cult survived among exiles and fraudulent claimants; one group of exiles opened a Provider house at [[Vorthara before 350 BR|Vorthara]] a generation later, and its table starved.

## Survivors and memory

Ilya spent her later life at Esharat matching rescued people with their relatives. Atherian histories made her a grateful witness to Caleran virtue. Her surviving fragments named children lost after the breach, cursed the soldiers who sold them and cursed by name the Marelith captains who bought them.

Sapha refused office in the Spared Town League. She trained carriers to recognise the marks traffickers used. Esharat later honoured her family while omitting the households she had threatened into cooperation.

Valeor lived to see the city he had condemned. He came out with the army as an old man to watch the children brought home, and found soldiers selling them on the quays. Through the summer of 520 he stood on Sarqen's landing and bought children back from the Marelith captains out of his own estate; Esharat's registers credit him with forty-one. He sold his town house to go on paying and died in rented rooms at Caleran about 514. Caleran raised a statue showing him with a rescued infant. Gateway survivors later broke its face. Survivors who knew both lists of names, the hostages of 609 and the children of the collections, said they broke it for both: he had bought back forty-one children and demanded the war that orphaned thousands. Mera Castren helped establish ransom funds from the surrendered books and continued defending the senate's right to govern the conquered country.

The spared towns renewed the Table Oath every year at Sarqen. In 457 they made Arenes, the first hereditary ruler of the country, eat at the unranked table and swear it before they recognised him, and every Nerethan ruler after him swore it at accession. The oath became a genuine moral inheritance and the root of the dynasty's legitimacy. Slavery, compulsory labour and political killing went on in the successor kingdom all the same.
