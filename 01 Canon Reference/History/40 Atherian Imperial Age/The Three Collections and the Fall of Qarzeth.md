# The Three Collections and the Fall of Qarzeth

The **Years of Three Collections**, approximately 548–540 BR, name the most extensive child-taking campaigns of the Gateway Republic. After two lost wars, Qarzeth had recovered with a speed its enemies could not explain: its granaries stood full while the stores of its dependent towns emptied. Priests of the Bronze-Faced Provider taught that the plenty required an irreplaceable return, and civic officials carried the demand into towns that had once offered animals or money.

The collections became a principal cause of Caleran's final war against Qarzeth. Internal opponents supplied testimony, guided rescuers and helped the besiegers. The capital fell as its people's stories say a hungry god falls: its carriers stopped, its dead were named, its face was broken and its survivors ate together. Caleran ended the sacrificial institution and then destroyed the city, murdering, raping and enslaving inhabitants during the sack. The lesser Gateway towns survived and inherited both histories.

## The civic sanctuary and its plenty

The Provider's sanctuary stood in Qarzeth's upper district. Its estates owned workshops, gardens, stores and serving chambers. Priests fed the city at festivals and financed public works, and leading households gained prestige by supporting it. These services made the institution part of civic life long before its last decades.

The plenty was real. After the defeats of 646 and 601, Qarzeth's storehouses filled faster than any harvest could fill them, and the city rebuilt fleets in seasons that should have taken years. The same years emptied the dependent towns. At Esharat and Belqara, jars sealed full at harvest stood half empty in spring with their seals unbroken. Around 551 the custodians of [[Ilamar before 350 BR|Ilamar]] recorded those findings and refused to certify the sanctuary's grain. Gateway farmers had a name for the thing: stores that emptied without a thief.

The cult taught that every provision demanded a larger return, and that the largest return was a child. Human sacrifice had occurred at changing frequency across the Republic's history. Families offered infants in moments of fear; officials took older captives, dependants and children bought from poor households. Wealthy patrons sometimes surrendered their own children and sometimes bought substitutes. By the 550s the sanctuary's doors opened only when a living name had been entered on the collectors' household lists, and soldiers enforced the lists in the dependent towns.

The Provider appeared to its worshippers in smoke, dreams and the long tables of its festivals. Priests shaped the doctrine, magistrates authorised the collectors, patrons paid and guards held the victims. In the last collection, people in the capital said the festival tables grew longer as the guests grew fewer.

## Ilya and Sapha

Collectors travelled with guards and household lists. Families hid children in orchards, cargo and relatives' houses. Neighbours informed for payment or to protect their own children. Punishments included confiscation, imprisonment and the seizure of another family member.

**Ilya of Qarzeth** worked in the sanctuary stores. Before the third collection she copied the names of imprisoned children onto packing strips, taking them from the collectors' own lists, and escaped in a grain cart. Her list contained errors and omissions, but it named holding rooms, officials and the districts that knew what was happening.

At Esharat, the midwife **Sapha** organised carriers, fishing households and rural kin into a rescue route. They moved children beneath produce and through irrigation channels. The route saved dozens and failed many others. Collectors killed Sapha's husband during a search and took her eldest daughter, **Neral**. Neral was sacrificed before the rescuers reached Qarzeth.

The opponents disagreed about their purpose. Ilya wanted the sanctuary dismantled and the Republic preserved. Sapha wanted Qarzeth's authority broken. Belqara's leaders sought independence, while exiles called for the city's destruction. Their testimony nevertheless established that the killings belonged to a public institution protected by the Republic.

## Debate in Caleran

Escaped families carried their evidence to Atherian ports. The elderly senator **Orsen Valeor** placed Ilya's list and the survivors beside him while demanding that the child-taking city fall. He sincerely opposed the sacrifices, and he also believed that a city which recovered from every defeat threatened every Atherian alliance.

Valeor spoke in a city that had killed Gateway children of its own. In 609, during the war against Zamaret Ordan, Caleran had offered Gateway hostages, children of about eight to sixteen, to the Red Star on its festival ground. When Valeor demanded Qarzeth's destruction, Gateway exiles in Caleran answered by reading out those children's names. Senator **Mera Castren** drew the opposite lesson from the same ground: Caleran had stopped, so Qarzeth could stop too. She proposed inspectors, permanent hostages and the compulsory closure of the sanctuary, and she cited Ilamar's record of the sealed jars to argue that the towns, given protection, would starve the cult themselves. Gateway opponents of the cult supported parts of her proposal because annihilation would punish the families who had resisted. Valeor answered that the rooms would reopen after an Atherian withdrawal.

Valeor's coalition included people seeking rescued children, safer shipping, land, profit and military distinction. Qarzeth surrendered weapons and selected hostages. Caleran then demanded that the inhabitants abandon the city and rebuild inland. The assembly refused. Opponents of sacrifice defended their homes beside cult patrons, and some were killed trying to overthrow the priesthood during the siege.

## Siege and rescue

The defeat came in the order the Gateway country still tells it.

**The carts stopped.** Between about 536 and 531 the carriers of Belqara, Esharat, Hazreth and Sarqen had camped on the rope-makers' ground and refused to carry grain and children into the capital. Their camp was broken and its leaders executed, but the towns went over to Caleran. In the siege that began in 525, nothing came in by land or sea. For the first time in living memory, Qarzeth went hungry. The priests killed more children, and the storehouses stayed empty. Hunger killed children, old people and enslaved labourers first in the crowded districts. Belqara and Esharat supplied the Atherians under agreements promising their survival; Sarqen admitted refugees and repaired allied ships.

**The dead were named.** Ilya guided an Atherian detachment towards the holding rooms after the wall was breached in 520 BR. Soldiers killed the guards and found living children beside cremated remains. Because Ilya's list had been copied from the collectors' own lists, it matched remains, clothing and tokens to names. Parents recovered some living children and identified the belongings of others. The dead were later buried by name at Esharat.

**The face was broken.** Soldiers broke the Provider's bronze image and pulled down the serving chambers. Atherian annals credit a soldier with an axe. Esharat credits Sapha's carriers. Ilya's surviving fragment says that "it broke when the last name was spoken".

Across the rest of the city, Atherian soldiers murdered residents, raped captives and organised survivors for sale. Some rescued children were separated again during the distribution of prisoners, and captains from the wandering harbour-city of [[Marelith]] bought them and resold many to the ransom funds. Officers intervened unevenly, and commanders permitted looting and mass enslavement. Captured priests and officials were executed.

**The survivors ate together.** Some days after the sack, at Sarqen's landing, the people the war had spared shared one meal: rescued children, carriers, farmers, freed slaves, people ransomed out of the capital and a few Atherian deserters. Each brought what they had. No one was served by rank and no seat was kept empty. Sapha set the rules and refused to preside. The rules became the **Table Oath** of the spared towns: *no child for the table; no seat kept empty; no guest served by worth.*

## Destruction and salting

Caleran's senate had decided that Qarzeth would never again become the capital of the Gateway country. Engineers marked the sanctuary, civic squares, elite gardens, store compounds and selected waterworks. Cisterns were spoiled, channels broken and building stone removed.

The salt came by boat through the captured harbour from the Gateway's own pans at Hazreth and Sarqen. It was the salt that had stood on the table at the landing. The spared towns asked that it be laid where the plenty had grown: the sanctuary, the gardens and the store compounds. Nothing grows on salted ground, and the towns meant that nothing should ever again be served from it. Atherian annals record the salting as the punishment of a rebel city. Gateway tradition calls it closing the table: "The salt of the table and the salt of the ruin were one salt."

The salting devastated the urban centre and left most of the surrounding country habitable. Villages, fields and lesser towns continued under new rulers. The Provider lost its sanctuary, its public offerings and its civic protection. Fragments of the cult survived among exiles and fraudulent claimants; one group of exiles opened a Provider house at [[Vorthara before 350 BR|Vorthara]] a generation later, and its table starved.

## Survivors and memory

Ilya spent her later life at Esharat matching rescued people with their relatives. Atherian histories made her a grateful witness to Caleran virtue. Her surviving fragments named children lost after the breach, cursed the soldiers who sold them and cursed by name the Marelith captains who bought them.

Sapha refused office in the Spared Town League. She trained carriers to recognise the marks traffickers used. Esharat later honoured her family while omitting the households she had threatened into cooperation.

Valeor died before the final assault. Caleran raised a statue showing him with a rescued infant. Gateway survivors later broke its face. Survivors who knew both lists of names, the hostages of 609 and the children of the collections, said they broke it for both. Mera Castren helped establish ransom funds and continued defending the senate's right to govern the conquered country.

The spared towns renewed the Table Oath every year at Sarqen. In 457 they made Arenes, the first hereditary ruler of the country, eat at the unranked table and swear it before they recognised him, and every Nerethan ruler after him swore it at accession. The oath became a genuine moral inheritance and the root of the dynasty's legitimacy. It did not end slavery, compulsory labour or political violence within the successor kingdom.
