# Nine Wells of Tergai

The **Nine Wells of Tergai** form a broken chain of deep water, seasonal pasture, graves, forts, shrines, and market fires north of Mazrakan. No single wall contains all nine; control means holding draw gear, roads, nearby grazing, and enough soldiers to stand at the well-lips.

## Tarkhan Memory

The Tarkhan call them the **Nine Mother-Wells**. Between approximately 1900 and 1650 BR, Tergai houses deepened older sources and linked them with cisterns, ramps and repair yards. Former Vathran tether workers supplied lifting knowledge; neighbouring camps brought labour, animals and food. Ahdari caravans also used the chain and buried relatives beside several waters, while Tarkhan tenure remained central to its maintenance.

Seasonal rights belonged to houses through labour, burial, hospitality and remembered use. In 951, the first raid season of the Long Reach, Grass-Tarkhan riders on the daughters of Grey Sister's line watered their horses at the central well without asking any fort's leave, joining western water memory to eastern horse memory. The hounds the Covenant had bound into the shafts came up spitting silt, and the grey horses, which do not shy from bound things, stood while their riders killed them at the well-lip. Tarkhan genealogists count Grey Sister's line among the wells' rightful drinkers from that day.

## Covenant Memory

Covenant pilgrimage calls them the **Wells of First Submission**. Official accounts say dry-belt clans willingly submitted to Mujalib's law and received ordered access to water. Pilgrims visit witness stones, fortified shrines, and a ceremonial surrender court.

## Conquest of the Wells

In 558 AR, the worst year of the dry-country drought that outlasted the Thin Floods, the guard of the Covenant's oasis wells, under its captain Zuhayr ibn Qays, seized the wells by violence. Defenders were killed, expelled, bound to draw water only on the conquerors' terms or absorbed under military rule. The tradition of voluntary submission grew up afterwards, and the conquerors' preachers made it holy.

Qahirid rule at Tergai therefore stands on an old dispossession, and the pilgrims' witness stones rise among Tarkhan graves. Pilgrims and frontier families inherited very different relationships to that history, and many arrived long after the conquest.

## The Hound Wells and the Horse War

Under Qahir ibn Ruzbeh the Covenant bound silt hounds, blind pursuit beasts brought out of the mapped mire of Hell, into the wet shafts of its frontier wells. Their keepers fed them under names sworn to the sovereign. When Sultan-Caliph Idris died in 931, two years after his heir had refused to take up his instruments, those names lapsed, and the unfed hounds climbed out and hunted the escarpment towns through the winter. The Covenant re-bound them to its new throne in 938. In 951 the Grass-Tarkhan horse broke the hound wells, and for the next half-century the Nine Wells were the hinge of what Iqashari registers call the Horse War. After 1067 Iqashar bought peace at the wells as often as it fought for it, through Mazrakan's horse fairs and Tarkhan companies hired on word-price.

## Present Geography

- Qahirid forts hold the southern and central road approaches.
- Iron-Horde companies guard imperial routes and maintain camps close to the wells.
- White-Horde banners conduct reclamation raids and attack pilgrimage supply.
- Tarkhan graves and seasonal pasture lie inside areas administered as Covenant security zones.
- Mazrakan factors profit from both pilgrimage and frontier war.
