# The Mundane Layer — Residue, Vermin, and the Delver Trade

**The canon ruling that gives the world its floor.** The Bestiary Framework wired the great creature families to the cosmology — correctly, for the great families. But a setting where every skeleton is a soul-routing crisis and every ruin is an existential wound cannot support ordinary adventure. This note establishes the missing tier: monsters you can just *fight*, dungeons you can just *delve*, and evil you can handle with a sword, a torch, and no theology.

## The Residue Principle

The world is old and soaked. Wherever death, magic, grief, or violence pooled — and the rites, wards, or years that should have drained it failed — **residue animates**. Residue has no agenda, no master, no message. It is not the Adversary's, not an Idol's, not the Undertow's (unless a specific story says so, which should be *rare* and should feel like a terrible discovery). It is the world's equivalent of mold in a damp house: a natural consequence of a supernatural ecology, dangerous, common, and fundamentally *handleable*.

**The theological safety-valve (important):** a restless corpse or a haunting is **not the person.** The soul routed — it went to the Weighing like every soul. What animates is the *shed echo*: the residue of grief, violence, or unfinished habit left in bone and place, like a footprint that keeps walking. This is settled doctrine in both churches (one of the few things they agree on), which is why destroying a barrow-shade is sexton-work, not soul-murder — closer to cleaning than to war. Delvers put them down; priests consecrate after; nobody needs an inquisitor.

## The Encounter Pyramid (design law)

- **TIER ONE — Vermin & Residue (the common world, ~three encounters in four).** No cosmological meaning. Clear it, get paid, go home.
- **TIER TWO — Aligned (uncommon).** Grobi bands, cult cells, a drake's territory, a vampire's household: factional, local, political — meaningful but bounded.
- **TIER THREE — Cosmological (rare, campaign-shaping).** Hollow-Kings, thin-space ruptures, Left-Hand architecture, held proofs. When the fabric of reality pushes back, it should be *news*.

A campaign that skips Tier One reads as exhausting; a campaign that never reaches Tier Three reads as small. The pyramid is the pacing.

## The Tier One Bestiary

**The Restless Dead (residue-animate):**
- **Rattle-bones** — skeletons risen where rites failed or graves soured: battlefield edges, plague pits, flooded crypts. Mindless, territorial, brittle. The classic dungeon population, and canonically *ordinary*.
- **Barrow-shades** — the standing echo of old interments; cold, slow, bound to their stones. The marches' commonest haunting.
- **Drowned walkers** — riverbank and shipwreck residue; heavy, patient, drawn to lights on water.
- **Keeners** — grief-echoes that wail in ruined places (the banshee's honest cousin); harmless until crowded, dangerous when joined.
- **Wall-wardens** — old defensive wards decayed into indiscriminate guardianship: animated masonry, armor, or statuary that no longer remembers whom it served. Every Atherian undercroft has a story.

**The Vermin:**
- **Grave-rats, tomb-spiders, corpse-beetles, bat-swarms** — ruin ecology; residue-fattened but natural.
- **Leakspawn** — oozes and slimes: alchemical runoff, Shell-seep, and Vorthara's exported mistakes. The liability courts call them *escaped inventory*.
- **Wargs, cave-bears, marsh-drakes** (the last no relation to the true drakes — a naming grievance the scholars have lost) — big fauna in the wrong place.
- **Harridans** — carrion-bird flocks grown large and bold around old death; the harpy of folklore, minus the folklore, usually.

**Deliberately unruled:** hoard-lurkers (the delvers' campfire mimic), hill-trolls (folklore insists; the Grobi fifth-stage rumor is a *separate* canon question), and everything else the trade swears to over beer. Some of these are real. The register doesn't say which.

## Where the Dungeons Come From

The world generates delving sites honestly: **barrow-fields** (pre-human and Folk); **Atherian undercrofts** (a thousand years of imperial cellars, waystations, and forgotten shrines under every old town); **Giant-era ruins** (glass-fused, half-taboo, Tier Three waiting under Tier One skins); **war-scars** (Shell-burned battlefields, collapsed siegeworks); **soured mines**; **Velian border forts** (three changes of ownership, three layers of dead); and **failed colonies** on every frontier. Most hold rattle-bones, vermin, a bandit season, and somebody's lost strongbox. That is the point.

Caleran is the largest urban expression of this rule. Its working waterworks, ossuaries, cellars, siege tunnels, and undercrofts contain ordinary residue and vermin far more often than active relics, hostile networks, or functioning Atherian systems.

## The Delver Trade

The artifact economy's foot soldiers, canonized: **barrow-runners, tomb-sweeps, salvage crews** — licensed (Bank charters, ducal writs, University sanction) and wildcat. Trade infrastructure: bounty boards at every march town; **salvage law** (finders-quarter to the landholder, contraband carve-outs for relics and name-tablets, which everyone honors approximately); guild-posts that sell maps of dubious lineage (see the Lantern-Brothers, in the folk-hero canon); and the trade's two iron proverbs — *"iron for bones, silver for shades, run from anything that talks"* and *"if the ground is worked stone, work elsewhere"* (imported from Castara, honored everywhere). Delving is dangerous, low-status, occasionally lucrative work — the world's adventuring class, without the word.

## What This Note Protects

The strange-dose rule, from the other side. Ordinary people meet the strange twice a lifetime; ordinary *delvers* meet Tier One weekly and Tier Three, if ever, once — and the veterans' stories about that once are how the trade teaches its children respect. Skeletons are evil, and it's all right: you can handle them. That sentence is load-bearing.
