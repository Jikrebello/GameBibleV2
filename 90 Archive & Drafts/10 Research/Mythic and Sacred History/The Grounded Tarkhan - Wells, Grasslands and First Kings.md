# The Grounded Tarkhan — Wells, Grasslands and First Kings

## An ancient Orphaned people

The ancient Tarkhan developed among human communities which survived the fall of Vathra outside the Glass refuge. Their ancestors included camel keepers, tether-town craftsmen, eastern herders, hunters, captive labourers, soldiers, fugitives and families which had never accepted Giant rule. They possessed no single founding camp. Shared speech, fosterage, assembly customs and repeated movement between western wells and eastern grass gradually made them recognisable to themselves and their neighbours.

The term **Tarkhan** belongs securely to the later ancient period. Earlier people usually identified themselves by a well, seasonal route, craft household or descent group. Southern records began using the broad name during the Hearth-Crown centuries. Northern singers later adopted it while disagreeing over its origin. Some derived it from a Vathran title given to released servants; others from a camp word for a person who had cut a tether. No surviving evidence settles the question.

This history ends in 350 BR. Horses remained scarce and politically limited throughout the period. Western households used camels, donkeys and carts. Eastern communities walked with sheep and cattle or moved goods by sledge and wagon. The mounted civilisation of the Grass-Tarkhan began only after the First Catch of 905–920 AR.

## Communities inherited from Vathra

Four populations contributed strongly to early Tarkhan formation.

The **Tergai well houses** maintained deep water in the central dry belt. Their claims rested on digging, draw gear, burial and seasonal use. Different houses controlled approaches, gardens and storage rooms around the same well. Collective defence therefore did not require one owner.

The **Elkari grass families** followed cattle, sheep and wild herds through the eastern seasonal lake country. They moved between cold-weather shelter and summer grass, using carts on firm ground and sledges after snow. Hunting remained important during herd disease and drought.

The **Taleri tether towns** grew around grounded Vathran works. Their inhabitants preserved masonry, metal repair, wind-cloth weaving and knowledge of old machines. Some towns contained the descendants of privileged sky households; others were founded by captives freed during the Unyoking.

The **Narashi caravan houses** held western wells and camel routes. Their maternal crowns predated the fall of Vathra and survived it unevenly. Narashi families joined both Tarkhan and Ahdari societies over later generations. The name described a historical network of houses rather than a separate permanent race.

These communities intermarried, exchanged foster children and absorbed outsiders. They also enslaved one another, fought over water and told hostile stories about each other's conduct during the revolt. Their eventual peoplehood grew through this difficult intimacy.

## Rebuilding after the sky fell, c. 2260–1900 BR

The first generations faced broken supply. Royal storehouses burned or lay beneath wreckage. Released animals scattered. Wells which had served large landing fields could support only a fraction of the displaced population. Families had to decide whether to remain near useful ruins or leave before others seized them.

At Kel-Orun, human survivors turned two lower reservoirs into public cisterns. Former royal servants claimed the surrounding apartments and attempted to preserve their privileges. Freed labourers expelled one household after discovering that it had hidden grain while neighbours starved. The victors divided the rooms, then retained several of the old family's water records because the measurements were accurate.

The eastern herds recovered slowly. Cattle and sheep had survived across many camps, whereas horses had been concentrated at royal estates. Communities ate some captured horses during the first famines and traded others to southern rulers for seed. Later songs condemned these choices as the work of cowards, although the exchanged grain kept children alive.

Violence continued after the Giant courts had fallen. Armed bands took animals from settlements whose defences had failed. Former tether officers tried to restore tribute under human rulers. One Giant cadet held an upland pasture for forty years by taking a child from each dependent household. A coalition of herders killed him bodily and found that several hostage families had served willingly in return for privilege. The settlement of their property divided the coalition after its victory.

## Household and belonging

An early Tarkhan household was a working and protective association. It might include relatives by birth, spouses, foster children, adopted strangers, apprentices, freed dependants, clients and slaves. Its members shared animals, shelter and obligations according to rank. The head of a wealthy house could command labour and negotiate marriage, but survival required experienced animal keepers, water knowledge and the cooperation of adults who could leave with part of the herd.

Fosterage joined distant camps. A child spent several years with another household, learned its route and acquired people obliged to protect them. Giant courts had used fosterage as hostage-taking. Human communities retained the practice while giving it reciprocal forms. Powerful houses still abused it by refusing to return a child or demanding service beyond the agreed period.

Adoption admitted a person to burial and revenge obligations. Manumission released an enslaved person from ownership, although freedom without animals or kin could leave the person dependent on the former master. Some freed families formed new camps around a craft. Others remained attached to the household which had held them.

Marriage moved animals, rights and relationships. Women of important camel and water houses often controlled breeding lines and portions of a well. Eastern households valued spouses who connected winter shelter to distant pasture. Bride seizure, captive marriage and concubinage also occurred. A marriage made under threat did not become voluntary because later descendants valued the resulting kinship.

## Camels, carts and northern skill

Camel husbandry became the most visible western inheritance of Vathra. The animals survived because human families had bred and used them throughout the ground network. A good camel carried water and stores between distant stages, produced milk and survived conditions which exhausted horses. Breeders selected animals for temperament, endurance and the ability to travel with young.

Eastern carts carried households, felt, food and tools. Wheelwrights travelled between assemblies and repaired vehicles with whatever timber the open country supplied. Sledges crossed winter ground and moved carcasses after communal hunts. Foot runners carried short messages more reliably than the surviving royal horses.

Veterinary knowledge linked the zones. Healers treated split pads, infected harness wounds, difficult births and herd fever. Some remedies came from Vathran animal houses; others belonged to older hunters and well communities. Healers travelled safely through certain feuds because both sides needed their skill, although no rule protected them everywhere.

The people preserved remnants of sky craft. Metal thread strengthened ceremonial cloth. Pieces of lift mantle covered graves or shaded public fires. A few Taleri families could make a courier cloth twitch or rise at a surviving anchor. They could not rebuild the network or carry a city.

## Assemblies and seasonal power

The principal assembly met around an **open fire** supplied by several households. Each participating camp brought fuel or dried dung, making the gathering's duration visible in the size of the shared pile. Elders arranged pasture, ransom and compensation. War leaders sought followers for particular expeditions. Singers recited genealogies and exposed people who had broken guest protection.

No assembly governed the entire northern country. A decision carried force among the houses which accepted it and among allies willing to enforce it. A powerful leader could gather several circuits by feeding followers and distributing spoil. The coalition shrank when the gifts stopped, a rival offered safer pasture or the leader demanded service beyond the original purpose.

Women exercised public authority most visibly as well keepers, herd matrons, healers, senior foster relatives and patrons of singers. A Narashi crown-mother could close a sanctuary to her own armed kin. An Elkari herd matron could remove breeding animals from a war leader and thereby end his campaign. These powers depended on property, reputation and allies; they did not amount to equal authority in every household.

## The chariot houses, c. 1750–1325 BR

The Age of Reed and Bronze supported a small number of prestigious chariot dynasties. Their horses grazed in sheltered eastern valleys and at stations supplied by Qahuri or river-city grain. Wheelwrights, metalworkers and enslaved grooms maintained the vehicles. A chariot could break poorly prepared infantry on firm ground and became useless among rocks, deep sand or winter mud.

**Boran Tall-Hand** built the best remembered of these houses around Daram Ford. His father had married into a Taleri craft family, giving Boran access to reinforced wheels. His mother held eastern pasture through Elkari foster kin. Boran protected convoys and defeated three rival lords; he also seized children from households which refused animals for his teams.

During the Breaking of the Bronze Roads, Boran accepted a palace contract to carry copper west. Poor floods had raised the price of food, and his porters begged him to abandon metal rather than grain when mud trapped the convoy. He ordered the grain dumped to preserve the ingots and horses. The porters killed him. They opened the wagons and distributed seed to nearby settlements, while rival captains divided the teams.

Boran's descendants kept one valley for two generations but never restored the chariot court. Later singers called the convoy the Feast of the Copper Wheels. Some praised the porters; others lamented a king murdered by servants. The event preserved a clear truth: royal horse power depended on people who could withdraw their labour.

## The making of a broad people, c. 1325–800 BR

The collapse of distant exchange drew communities towards shorter circuits. Tergai families met Elkari herders at western winter markets. Taleri smiths repaired camel gear for Narashi caravans. Marriages and fosterage carried words, melodies and burial customs across the region.

Southern writers began applying *Tarkhan* to these related northern populations. The word spread because it was useful in treaties, tribute accounts and market speech. It never erased local membership. A person could call herself Tarkhan among Qahuri merchants and name only her well and foster house at home.

Shared stories strengthened the identity. The Great Unyoking supplied an account of human beings cutting the apparatus which carried rulers above them. The Copper Wheels warned leaders that skill and food mattered more than display. Comic songs described Vathran kings too large to mount their own chariots and later patrons who claimed descent from horses they could not feed.

Common life also carried grief. Rock-cut laments named people killed on raids or lost in drought. A traveller might add a line beside the name of a relative from another generation. These writings turned routes into dispersed memorials and allowed people who shared no government to recognise one another's dead.

## Raid, feud and captivity

Raids took animals, provisions and people. Leaders divided shares among those who had sworn to join. Scouts selected a target; fighters waited near water or a return route. Success could preserve a hungry camp. It could also starve the children of the people whose herd was taken.

Captives were ransomed, exchanged, adopted, married under coercion, kept as slaves or sold to settled markets. Sexual violence occurred during attacks and captivity. Feud exposed the raider's relatives to retaliation, including people who had opposed the expedition. Compensation could end the killing when affected families accepted it. Wealthy houses sometimes forced poorer kin to accept a settlement which protected the offender.

One remembered raiding oath involved twenty-one reins bound at a route stone. Seven participants refused after their leader changed the target from an enemy camp to an undefended ally. Later praise poetry removed the seven names and celebrated the captured animals. A lament by a woman taken in the raid preserved the betrayal and the children sold afterwards. This episode remains a development lead; Package C can retain it as received history without assigning exact names before canon review.

## Cult, sky and the dead

Early Tarkhan worship differed among routes. Communities sacrificed sheep, goats, cattle or camels for rain, safe travel, healthy births and protection from enemies. High places retained Vathran stones and broken anchors. Some rites addressed local court persons or Menhir correspondences. Others gathered around empty objects which carried family memory.

Idol Masks exploited the same practices. Wrath appeared through feud standards which demanded blood beyond the original offence. Greed fed where ransom and water became occasions for owning people. Pride attached itself to dynasties claiming that their ancestors had ruled the upper sky. The presence of a Mask did not make every person at a shrine its conscious servant.

The dead were buried near routes and dependable water when possible. Grave goods included food, tools, animal gear and fragments of coloured cloth. Vathran royal burial had killed servants and animals beside Giant rulers. Some human prestige houses continued funerary sacrifice. Others cut the tether of a living animal at the grave, declaring that no dead chief would take the household's survival with him.

## Foreign service and the two clients, c. 900–690 BR

Northern households served Qahuri crowns, Arqeshite governors and lesser river powers as guides, camel carriers, scouts, archers and guards. Foreign rulers paid in grain, metal, cloth and captives. Service brought wealth and exposed families to wars far from their customary routes.

**Savan Uruz** and **Demer Khol** were cousins raised for several years in the same foster camp. Savan accepted an Arqeshite crown and garrison at an eastern water station. Demer controlled western camel stages with Qahuri support. Each claimed to protect the road from the other. Each raided households beyond the districts whose tribute he received.

Their rivalry lasted through marriages, hostage exchanges and temporary reconciliation. Savan employed Taleri smiths and Arqeshite deportees at his fortified camp. Demer supported poets, camel races and a sanctuary which admitted fugitives from Savan's taxes. He also sold captured eastern families into Qahuri estates.

When Arqeshite payments stopped during the succession crisis, Savan seized grain from his own soldiers' families. The soldiers killed him. Demer tried to recruit the unpaid companies and was defeated by former captives who joined his rivals. His last followers settled among people he had once attacked. The courts disappeared; their craftspeople and mixed households remained.

## The fall of Arqesh

Arqeshite rule divided Tarkhan interests. Client leaders gained weapons and food. Hostage families acquired education and office in river cities. Other communities lost pasture to stations, suffered deportation or supplied scouts under threat.

During the revolt after 706 BR, northern groups attacked remount stations and helped open roads for Arezan Tiravan's coalition. **Yalun of the White Camel** led families seeking hostages held at Durqesh. **Marat Two-Bows** wanted the station herds and accepted Vasharan gifts. Yalun released captives from several peoples; Marat sold captured grooms after the victory. Their temporary cooperation ended when Arezan recognised both men as local protectors.

The participation of Tarkhan forces did not give them Arqesh's empire. Vasharan rulers retained selected stations and paid northern houses for passage. Some communities benefited; others treated the new gifts as tribute owed by another foreign king.

## Rethmar and the Atherian approach

Rethmar developed around winter water, storage and a market where western circuits met. It was a gathering place rather than a capital. Permanent gardeners, smiths and water keepers lived beside seasonal compounds built by visiting houses.

In 381 BR, Lethren Ostar expected Rethmar's leaders to provide his Atherian army with water, camels and guides. **Kharuz of the Stone Wells** organised Tarkhan resistance. He drew Ostar's columns apart by promising separate negotiations and used foot archers against detachments moving between wells. **Tavisha**, an Ahdari water keeper, removed supplies and arranged false camps.

The victory killed Ostar and captured many of his followers. Kharuz prioritised the ransom of officers because weapons and animals would strengthen the wells. Tavisha argued that sick captives and water carriers should be released first. Their disagreement became public after a fever spread through the prisoner camp. The coalition accepted Tavisha's order for the sick, exchanged several officers and enslaved captives whose families sent no ransom.

The defence entered both Tarkhan and Ahdari memory. Kharuz became a model of northern strategy. Tavisha became a water keeper who could overrule a victorious war leader inside the camp she supplied. Neither founded a permanent kingdom.

## Tarkhan society in 350 BR

By 350 BR, the broad Tarkhan name joined many western well and eastern grassland communities. Their dialects remained mutually intelligible across most major routes. Fosterage, marriage, markets and song connected people more effectively than government.

The west relied on camels, defended water and caravan brokerage. The east relied on sheep, cattle, carts, hunting and seasonal grass. Taleri towns preserved craft concentrations around Vathran ruins. Narashi houses crossed the boundary between Tarkhan and Ahdari society. A person could change ecological life through marriage, drought or exile without changing ancestry at one moment.

Foreign observers knew famous houses and temporary coalitions. None possessed the remount depth, mounted archery or continent-wide messenger system of the later Horse Centuries. The old civilisation was fully human and technically accomplished on its own terms.

## Facts to preserve after review

- The Tarkhan are principally Orphaned humans.
- Their ancestors lived under, beside and against Vathra.
- Camels and ground transport carry their ancient civilisation.
- Chariot and horse households remain small and dependent on settled supply.
- The First Catch remains a later transformation.
- Raid, slavery, captive coercion and feud remain part of the history alongside hospitality, fosterage, craft and communal defence.
- No ancient confederation anticipates Qarayel's Horde.
