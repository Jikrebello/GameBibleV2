# Egypt, Phoenicia and Carthage — River Kingdom, Maritime Republic and Royal Successor

## Purpose

Egypt and Carthage should contribute to two connected World Egg histories rather than one composite civilisation. The Qahuri Sacred Basin can carry the long river-kingdom grammar: alternating unity and division, sacral crowns, irrigation, monumental legitimacy, foreign dynasties and cultural survival under changing rulers. The Qabir–Zanthir gateway already carries the Phoenician and Carthaginian grammar: mixed coastal settlement, competing ports, a maritime republic, dependent agricultural towns, child sacrifice and destructive wars with Caleran.

The later House of Nerethan can braid these inheritances. Its country descends materially from the spared gateway towns. Its royal household may adopt or inherit a river-crown tradition through marriage, exile or deliberate political borrowing. Such a claim would help explain Samyra’s later royal style without resurrecting the destroyed republic or turning the Qahuri and Gateway peoples into one ancient ethnicity.

## Egypt’s long political rhythm

Ancient Egyptian chronology alternates periods of strong central kingship with periods of regional power and competing dynasties. The Old, Middle and New Kingdoms were later scholarly divisions, each followed or preceded by substantial reorganisation. “Intermediate” did not mean that life ceased. Regional rulers, temples, towns, households and craft continued while control of the whole river changed.

The Nile supplied a long cultivated corridor whose annual cycles supported dense settlement. Royal administration, local officials, temples and village labour managed land, storage and public work. The court could move a capital, suppress a regional governor or enlarge temple patronage. Monumental building required resources and organised labour; it also sustained skilled crafts and a political image capable of outliving the ruler who commissioned it.

This history offers the Sacred Basin a valuable scale. Several towns can recognise a common river civilisation while obeying different crowns. A period of unity can produce roads, canal repairs and a shared calendar. Its collapse leaves regional houses with enough institutional memory to reunify the basin later. The resulting civilisation feels ancient because political failure changes it without repeatedly reducing it to nothing.

## Divine kingship and changing rulers

Egyptian kings bore several names and titles. The ruler could be identified with Horus and styled son of Ra, while local cults and royal theology changed across millennia. Kingship joined political command to the maintenance of cosmic and social order. A foreign dynasty could adopt pharaonic forms because the office remained intelligible to Egyptian subjects and priesthoods.

World Egg metaphysics requires careful separation. A Qahuri ruler may claim to be the earthly vessel, child or living face of a river power. The claimant remains human unless independently established otherwise. An Idol Mask can answer the cult and feed upon the architecture of royal praise. A Menhir manifestation may sustain a particular flood or boundary bargain without endorsing the dynasty’s justice. Political continuity, supernatural response and the human soul remain separate.

This separation makes foreign rule more interesting. A conqueror can adopt the crown’s ritual obligations, repair canals and receive a real bounded response from an existing power. Those acts establish a relationship with the basin; they do not rewrite ancestry or prove divine approval of conquest.

## Unity, fragmentation and restoration

The Middle Kingdom followed the reunification of Egypt by Mentuhotep II. Its rulers reworked earlier royal forms, established a new capital and expanded patronage of temples. Surviving art and literature suggest changes in the presentation of kingship and anxiety about disorder. The period supplies a strong World Egg restoration grammar: a ruler ends a generation of river conflict, repairs public works and deliberately invokes an older age while creating a new political settlement.

Later foreign and regional dynasties show further possibilities. Rulers of western Asiatic origin governed from the Delta during the Second Intermediate Period and adopted Egyptian titles. New Kingdom kings then expelled their rivals and built an empire beyond the river valley. During the Third Intermediate and Late Periods, Libyan, Nubian, Assyrian, Saite and Persian powers contested or governed Egypt. Saite kings relied substantially upon foreign mercenaries and trade. Persian rulers used pharaonic titles and worked through local institutions while remaining imperial conquerors.

The Ptolemies provide the closest comparison for the queen’s later court, though their period lies well after the Bronze and early Iron Age material. Greek-speaking rulers governed Egypt, supported Egyptian temples and priesthoods, and used several languages and forms of kingship. Their court belonged simultaneously to Mediterranean dynastic politics and an Egyptian territorial state.

For World Egg, the strongest sequence is:

1. independent basin towns inherit post-Giant canals and local shrines;
2. two or more river crowns compete over the cultivated corridor;
3. one house reunifies the basin and standardises public rites;
4. a foreign or mixed military dynasty adopts the crown and employs local officials;
5. regional houses re-emerge when that dynasty weakens;
6. later Covenant Al-Qahur inherits the landscape and some institutions without being the same state.

## Egyptian narrative traditions

Egyptian literature preserves people negotiating kingship, exile and divine conflict in forms useful to World Egg history.

### Sinuhe

Sinuhe is a court official who flees after a king’s death, rises under a foreign ruler, defeats a challenger and eventually returns to Egypt for an honourable burial. The tale joins fear, displacement, ability and longing for home. A World Egg river official could flee a disputed succession, acquire a household among Tarkhan or gateway people and return after the political order has changed. The burial desire should remain personal rather than become proof that every exile must return.

### Horus and Seth

The Egyptian succession traditions surrounding Osiris, Horus and Seth include murder, dismemberment, sexual humiliation, deception, divine judgement and territorial settlement. Their forms accumulated across centuries. The useful World Egg grammar concerns a disputed inheritance whose combat and legal argument are both physically dangerous. Human kings may stage or imitate such a myth. Idol Masks can inhabit rival royal images. The underlying Menhir or Idol persons remain distinct from the cultural family tree created by priests.

### The shipwrecked survivor and royal instruction

Egyptian tales of travel and shipwreck place officials far from court, where strange beings, hospitality and loss alter their understanding of royal service. Instruction literature presents ideals of speech, restraint and office while revealing the anxieties of hierarchy. These sources can give Qahuri history more than dynasties: scribal schools, household advice, comic failures and the dangerous prestige of returning from distant places.

## Phoenician continuity and competition

Phoenician civilisation grew from Late Bronze coastal societies. Cities such as Tyre, Sidon, Byblos and Arwad shared language and religious traditions while remaining political rivals. Their narrow agricultural territories encouraged maritime exchange, but food production and inland relations remained essential. Ports traded timber, metalwork, textiles and luxury goods and established communities across the Mediterranean.

The coastal network survived the Late Bronze disruptions through local continuity and changing routes. Phoenician expansion westward involved settlement, trade, intermarriage and conflict. Colonies were related to older cities without forming one permanent maritime nation under a single monarch.

The Gateway Republic already reflects this well. Its principal harbour grows among several lesser ports and cultivated towns. Proto-Ahdari migrants join older shore people and later maritime settlers. Civic identity becomes more important than a claim to pure descent. Its overseas communities possess interests of their own and may refuse the capital’s commands.

## Carthage as an African and maritime state

Carthage began as a Phoenician settlement and became a powerful city with a cultivated North African hinterland, maritime dependencies and relationships with Libyan and Numidian peoples. Its agriculture, taxation and rural labour mattered as much as shipping. Leading families competed for influence through elected office, command, wealth and patronage. Citizens, dependent communities, allied peoples and hired soldiers had different political standing.

Greek and Roman sources dominate the surviving literary account, which requires caution. Carthaginian armies included citizens, subject levies, allies and hired contingents. Calling everyone a mercenary obscures the political relationships within the state. The wars with Rome arose through rivalry, alliance systems, intervention in disputed cities, control of islands and maritime security. Their consequences included lost territories, indemnities, mutiny, rural grievance and eventual destruction.

The Gateway history already uses these structures. Further development should concentrate on the republic’s relationship with its country. The capital cannot sail without grain, repair timber, rope, animals, taxes and crews. Dependent towns therefore possess leverage during peace and suffer intensely when the council’s wars interrupt their work.

## Child sacrifice: evidence and disagreement

Ancient Greek and Roman writers accused Carthaginians and other Phoenicians of sacrificing children. Excavated tophets contain urns with cremated remains of infants and, in some cases, animals, accompanied by dedicatory monuments. Scholars continue to debate how many children were sacrificed, how many died naturally, how particular sanctuaries functioned and whether practice varied by period and place.

One substantial line of scholarship considers the combined literary, epigraphic and archaeological evidence sufficient to establish that child sacrifice occurred. Osteological interpretations of the infant remains remain contested. New work on individual sites can identify burial and cremation without always establishing the cause of death. The responsible historical conclusion is therefore narrower than either total denial or the claim that every infant in every tophet was killed.

Roman policy toward Carthage cannot be reduced to humanitarian intervention. The Punic Wars concerned competing power, security, allies, revenue and territory. Cato’s recorded advocacy of destruction presents Carthage as a renewed strategic danger. Roman writers used Carthaginian religious practice within hostile representations, but the decision to destroy the city in 146 BCE followed a long geopolitical conflict. Rome itself committed massacre, enslavement and dispossession in the sack.

World Egg may make a different and explicit choice. In the Gateway Republic, the sacrificial establishment kills living children and extends its demands to dependent towns. Families hide children, substitute captives, flee and organise resistance. Their testimony supplies a genuine reason for Atherian intervention. Caleran leaders also desire strategic supremacy, territory and wealth. The final coalition therefore contains people seeking to end the killings, people seeking liberation from the capital, and commanders who use both causes to justify conquest.

This layered motive strengthens the story. Destroying the sacrificial institution is a good act. Murdering and enslaving inhabitants during the sack remains evil. The spared towns may owe their survival partly to resistance against sacrifice while still remembering Atherian looting and coercion.

## Destruction, salting and the survival of a country

Rome destroyed Carthage in 146 BCE after a prolonged siege. Survivors were enslaved and the city was demolished. The familiar story that Romans sowed the site with salt lacks ancient evidence and belongs to much later legend. Some African communities had supported Rome or remained outside the city’s direct fate; Roman power expanded across a populated region.

World Egg has deliberately selected a more concentrated fictional outcome. The Gateway capital is burned, razed and heavily salted around 520 BR. Atherian forces murder and enslave inhabitants. The salting is an actual policy aimed at preventing the sacrificial metropolis from being rebuilt at its old seat. It damages surrounding land and remains visible for generations.

The lesser towns survive. Some had resisted child collection, some accepted terms, and some simply lay outside the final assault. Their farms, wells, storehouses, fishing grounds and households preserve the country’s material life. Refugees and returning captives join them. Second Harbour, later Sarqen, grows because it can feed and repair ships without occupying the accursed capital’s ground.

This outcome produces Samyra’s kingdom more convincingly than a complete cultural extinction. The crown inherits survivors with incompatible memories: rescue, betrayal, resistance, collaboration, enslavement and advantage gained after the old capital’s fall.

## Recommended relationship between the river kingdom and the gateway

The Sacred Basin and Gateway country should remain neighbours joined by caravan and coastal routes.

### The basin

An ancient Qahuri river civilisation develops among Orphaned communities and migrants from the Near-Glass world. Its **Twin-Reed Crowns** are a provisional historical complex rather than the modern state of Al-Qahur. Flood rites, canal offices and royal burials acquire several dynastic interpretations. A human ruler is never literally the Menhir or Idol named in a coronation title.

### The gateway

Coastal communities form through proto-Ahdari migration, older shore populations and maritime settlers. They develop the Gateway Republic and its dependent country. Their civic institutions, seafaring and sacrificial conflict remain independent of basin kingship.

### The royal successor

After the capital’s destruction, the House of Nerethan rules from Sarqen. Hadrima’s spared-town ancestry remains the dynasty’s material foundation. A future canon pass may give her maternal house a documented marriage connection to an exiled or junior Twin-Reed line. The relationship would provide access to river-crown ritual specialists and titles. It would not make Hadrima’s people foreigners in their own town.

Nerethan’s Delkosi household brings an Atherian maritime command. Their marriage then joins three usable inheritances: Atherian military recognition, gateway survival and a secondary basin royal claim. Later court writers can magnify that connection into dominion over both river and sea. Samyra’s opponents can dismiss it as borrowed costume, while Qahuri priests disagree over whether her household ever fulfilled the obligations attached to the title.

This proposal is compatible with the existing canonical dynasty but remains exploratory until a later integration decision.

## Supernatural architecture

The two regions should also possess different supernatural pressures.

### Sacred Basin

- A river-related Menhir or conjunction can govern actual flood boundaries through bargains with particular communities.
- Greed and Pride Masks may accumulate around the crown’s claim to own water, tombs and descendants.
- Ishkatari images and ruins may influence later royal symbolism without making the human kingdom a continuation of Ishkatar.
- The Boatman may appear in burial tradition under a local title while remaining the same psychopomp and outside the Star pantheon.

### Gateway country

- The bronze-faced provider is an Idol Mask whose promises encourage child sacrifice.
- Shore courts and sea Menhir relationships concern routes, weather, harbours and prices rather than civic sovereignty.
- Human opponents of sacrifice may receive ordinary assistance, deceptive bargains or no supernatural aid.
- The destruction of the image breaks one feeding architecture without destroying an Idol Throne.

The queen’s court can draw on both symbolic vocabularies. Similar crowns, boats or sun discs do not establish one supernatural identity.
