# Historical Seed Garden — Full Canon Harvest Review

> [!warning] Review scope
> This is an author-only authority audit. It changes no canon, chronology, culture article, protected question or public tradition. Its purpose is to determine what later Atlas work has already established before any further Garden material is promoted.

> [!info] Controlling distinction
> Every G-root already has a canonical public article at the `stories-told` layer: the tradition is told and its stated remainder or custom exists. That does **not** by itself establish the literal event, the tale's supernatural explanation or every named participant. Later authorial histories have nevertheless established the event cores behind many roots. Those later histories, not the Garden's older readiness labels, control this review.

## Governing Ruling

The Garden is not a queue of seventy-five equally unpromoted events. It is now a mixed-authority corpus:

| Current authority | Roots | Meaning |
|---|---:|---|

| `partial-framework` | **17** | Canon establishes the historical pressure, regional pattern or part of the event, but the root-specific company, sequence, names, site or full causal chain is not yet authorial fact. |
| `tradition-only` | **6** | Only the telling and remainder are canonical. A literal historical event still needs development, collision review and placement. |
| `protected-present` | **3** | The root touches a current or Forward-Rule outcome that must remain open. Earlier history may be established, but the protected verdict, rescue or result may not be fixed. |
| **Total** | **75** | Every root is accounted for exactly once. |

This ruling corrects a genuine control problem. [[Historical Seed Garden - Mythic Voice, Variety, and Localisation Review]] recorded seventeen `trace-ready`, forty-four `needs-regional-anchor`, eleven `needs-event-development` and three protected roots. That was a useful pre-Atlas readiness snapshot. It is no longer a reliable statement of present authority because the later historical Atlas, Great Saints and Sinners, heavenly, Unknown History, Patron, Menhir, Infernal, Idol, Last Houses and Packages C–D passes subsequently established many of those histories.

## Authority Rules for the Canon Pass

1. **Do not flatten the layers.** The public G-article records a received tradition. An authorial history records what happened. A culture or civilisation article should carry only the concise event core relevant to that people.
2. **Do not promote by implication.** A canonical era pressure is not proof that the Garden's exact company, number, name, route or relic history occurred.
3. **Do not demote later canon.** Where a canonical authorial article already says what happened, an older Garden `needs-*` label is historical workflow metadata, not a veto.
4. **Keep public uncertainty honest.** Event truth does not validate every miracle claim, number, moral gloss, later relic attribution or local ownership claim in the received tale.
5. **Preserve protected outcomes.** [[Protected Questions of the Historical Atlas]] controls G-17, G-23 and G-49. Earlier causes may be stated; their present verdicts may not.
6. **Harvest narrowly.** The next pass should add missing crosslinks and short event summaries to the right history or civilisation articles. It should not paste complete Garden capsules into public encyclopaedia prose.

## I. Giant Time, Chains and Exodus

### Author ruling — Giant hierarchy and the collapse of the court-world

The Five Imperial Horizons were not five neat territorial states. They were the largest hegemonic traditions within a crowded court-world resembling an especially unstable feudal or Holy Roman imperial order. Lesser kingdoms, estate courts, cadet houses, temple domains and mobile palaces could be internally independent while owing overlapping tribute, marriage, hostage, cult, military and infrastructure duties to one or more greater horizons. A court's imperial affiliation may therefore be plural, disputed or changed across generations.

The social order nevertheless possessed a clear vertical grammar:

1. rare Titan-Firstborn and the greatest ancient god-kings stood at the apex;
2. Ancient and Dynastic Giant houses—the beings later traditions commonly call demigods—formed the high ruling strata beneath them;
3. giant-blooded humans served as lesser dynasts, officers, priests, engineers and household intermediaries. They possess complete human souls and the Image despite being socially enrolled among Giant blooded peoples;
4. ordinary human populations formed the enslaved base of the dominant court economies, even where particular specialists, client communities, collaborators or fugitives acquired limited exemptions.

This is a hierarchy of descent, bodily power, legal privilege and political access, not a percentage scale of human soulhood. Every giant-blooded human remains wholly human; a true Giant continuity remains categorically distinct.

Giant flying-city technology followed the same hierarchy. The oldest and greatest aerial capitals incorporated living Titan bodies or organs into their Sovereign Works. Powerful Dynastic Giant courts inherited, divided, purchased or captured authentic ancestral fragments. Later and lesser imitators used weaker engines assembled from relics, blood rites and immense maintained infrastructure. These categories could overlap, but a city possessing a genuine conscious Titan fragment had capacities an imitation could not reliably reproduce. G-18's court remained politically lesser while retaining one such authentic engine, which made its forced Egg White crossing possible.

The founding rebels did not keep their progenitor's body in one capital. Major authentic organs were apportioned among several related flying courts, making the dismembered Titan the literal political geography of a lesser aerial civilisation. The courts shared descent and technical traditions but remained quarrelsome, internally independent and entangled in changing fealties. Ownership of a fragment conferred power and dynastic prestige, never moral legitimacy or a separate creaturely continuity.

Each living portion followed the fate of the city built around it. A crashed or buried city can now survive as a dungeon whose deepest machinery remains a conscious ancestral organ. Other fragments may still power wandering cities, lie beneath later settlements, have been captured by rival Giant systems or have crossed into an otherworld with their court. These are all pieces of one distributed Titan life, not a species of interchangeable magical reactor. The resulting ruin-and-survivor pattern uses the broad flying-civilisation grammar without importing another setting's names, cosmology or mechanisms.

Terms such as *heart*, *lung*, *eye*, *marrow* and *spine* are functional translations, not claims that Titan-Firstborn possessed enlarged human anatomy. Each Titan-Firstborn was a new created person born from mortal human blood and a bounded metaphysical parent, and its body expressed that raw conjunction differently. Their forms were Typhonic and chimeric: asymmetrical collections of flesh, scale, feather, horn, mineral growth, mouths, limbs and compound organs, sometimes with structures for which later anatomy had no proper name. They remained created and finite despite looking cosmically wrong. Every authentic separated organ remained part of the same distributed `titan-continuity`, never a new creature or an independent soul.

The closing crisis followed a connected cascade. The Lord's providential judgements, expressed through the Prophet's plagues and the natural catastrophes accompanying the liberation, broke the apparent invulnerability of the great imperial systems. Succession wars and Giant civil conflicts followed. Enslaved humans recognized the weakness and launched local revolts, defections and Spartacus-like insurgencies. Lesser courts then chose among repression, betrayal, negotiated opening, desperate neutrality and bodily escape. G-18 belongs to that background.

For G-18, the lesser court was governed by a Dynastic Giant family increasingly outnumbered by and materially dependent upon its human slaves. The court originated during the early wars in which Giant children fought their Titan-blood parents. Its founding dynasty overthrew and dismembered its own Titan-Firstborn progenitor, kept a still-living portion imprisoned and built a lesser civilisation around that captive ancestral engine. Later court religion recast the coup as a willing gift of power. The political irony is deliberate: descendants who celebrated liberation from their parent denied liberty to the humans beneath them.

The flying city ordinarily drew power from a vast breathing organ conventionally called the ancestor's **lung**. It was not a human-shaped lung, but a chimeric respiratory complex capable of sustaining aerial weight and drawing the city through different created conditions. The final Egg White crossing exceeded that ordinary capacity. The dynasty combined the ancestral anchor with a mass sacrifice of lesser human inhabitants, stripped useful anchor mass from poor districts and physically severed the remaining wards as dead weight. Some victims were killed for the rite; others were abandoned to collapse below. Human souls were not consumed as fuel: the rite exploited blood, terror, broken bonds and created power, while the dead remained human persons subject to the Lord's judgement. The captive ancestor remained conscious and unwilling throughout its long use.

The lung-city survived the crossing and still exists as a small, isolationist island-like realm in the Egg White. It is a minor chip in that vast political ecology, not a secret imperial power or an explanation for wider Patron history. Its Dynastic Giant house keeps largely to itself and the lung remains captive beneath the city. Rare, difficult routes and stories of Giant treasure nevertheless attract human adventurers, thieves and would-be liberators. Such expeditions create local encounters without making the city a familiar destination or major participant in known-world politics.

The city's undead workforce arose in three overlapping waves governed by rank and economic value. Before departure, the court murdered and carefully soulbound its most indispensable engineers, scribes, physicians, navigators and household specialists as permanent equipment. During the crossing, emergency seals caught many dying workers imperfectly and forced their damaged corpses back into service. After arrival, the dynasty made selective post-mortem binding a continuing labour policy for original survivors and later purchases.

These are not mindless residue zombies: each is a genuine `bound-human-soul`, an imprisoned human person whose decaying or preserved body is compelled by court seals. Treatment remains unequal even in undeath. Valuable specialists receive repaired bodies, speech, memory access and narrow household privileges because their expertise is useful. Quarry, hauling and maintenance dead are patched cheaply, kept cognitively suppressed and replaced when their bodies fail. Their continued operation obstructs the lawful soul-road and remains objectively evil. Breaking the correct bodily and office bindings may release a captive soul; merely destroying the visible corpse does not guarantee safe release.

The city supplements this finite workforce by purchasing occasional living mortals from the larger Egg White economy. Those arrivals include skilled slaves, debt-captives, abducted travellers and people sold under legal descriptions the lung-court chooses not to examine too closely. Value determines whether a mortal is kept alive for ordinary work, bound carefully before death, caught at death or allowed to become a degraded labour corpse.

No free human community is recognized within the present city. A mortal may enter lawfully only as a personally invited and closely supervised guest under temporary hospitality. Anyone else is classified as an intruder and may be expelled, killed or enslaved. The court's purchases pass through controlled outside intermediaries; there is no open foreign quarter, public market or ordinary mortal immigration.

### First harvest package ruling

This first bed should **not** be handled as seven uncertain legends. Four roots already possess authorial event histories (G-18, G-62, G-66 and G-69), two are partly harvested (G-01 and G-03), and only G-02 remains wholly event-development work.

4. add reciprocal links among public tradition, authorial truth and civilisation destination.

## II. Scattering, Hearths, First Kings, City-States and Stars

### G-06 working rebuild — the Old Mother and her daughter

The **Old Mother** began as an ancient mortal witch and follower of Hecatara. Through a long-forgotten act of deep ritual magic she achieved a form of occult self-apotheosis: her body and mode of presence became closer in operation and appearance to a Menhir court-being, while her continuity remained one complete human soul. She is therefore an immortal or quasi-divine human power, not a true Menhir, court-born person, saint or created goddess. Her survival is a transgressive delay and binding of the human soul-road rather than release from judgement.

Authorially, use `bound-human-soul`. Her soul is not divided among copies. It is caught into one extended continuity comprising the Old Mother, her transformed vessel, the House and a private web of acquired threshold anchors.

Her apotheosis began as a direct bargain with Hecatara. The first rite granted longevity, passage and a subordinate route-duty in exchange for a toll or service not yet specified. Over the following centuries the Old Mother used additional rites, thefts, substitutions and exact compliance with loopholes to move the burden of her continuity out of that original dependency and into the House and her growing threshold web. She eventually gained practical independence and a small acquired portfolio of **perilous petition**. This portfolio resembles a narrow court jurisdiction but is not true `yolk-sovereignty`, does not make her Hecatara's equal and grants no authority over the soul-road.

Mortals seek the Old Mother because she can use ancient craft, secret knowledge, household powers and stolen or accumulated correspondences to grant difficult boons: transformations, cures, concealment, escape, objects, information or improbable opportunities. She neither grants unlimited wishes nor changes reality outside the powers, materials and routes she can actually command. Every boon has a price, boundary and failure condition. Careless wording lets her satisfy the spoken request while displacing its cost, excluding an assumed protection or creating a debt the petitioner did not understand. Debts may require service, delivery, silence, fosterage, surrender of an object, fulfilment of a later request or return to the House. They cannot give her moral ownership of a human soul, alter judgement or make soulbinding righteous.

Unlike a Menhir, the Old Mother still possesses human moral comprehension. She knows when a term is cruel, when a petitioner is confused and when she is exploiting desperation. She may warn a courteous or clever petitioner, deliberately trap an arrogant one, or grant unexpected mercy; each is a moral choice rather than automatic domain pressure. Unlike the Creditor, she works personally and idiosyncratically through individual bargains instead of maintaining a standardized contract empire. The old Hecatara bargain remains part of her history and may leave a claim, scar or vulnerability without presently governing her existence.

The original mortal body, first price, exact method of emancipation, limits of the acquired portfolio and means of final release remain unresolved. Her Hecatara alignment explains her command of adjacency and thresholds but does not grant moral authority over roads.

The **House That Heard Them Laugh** is the Old Mother's mobile threshold-extension, conveyance and sensory instrument rather than an independent sovereign. It hears because the Old Mother is present through it even when her visible body has gone elsewhere.

The House has no single stable invitation shape. It may walk on animal or chimeric limbs, travel with or as a flying household vessel analogous to a great mortar and pestle, resemble an ordinary elder's cottage or present edible walls and welcoming smells when it intends to lure hungry children or careless travellers. Some appearances are historical and some later accretions, but mutable access fits its route-law. The Old Mother herself appears and disappears according to those paths. She is not consistently friend, foe or quest-giver. She honours exact hospitality, age, courage, useful work and intelligent speech; she punishes presumption, mockery, theft and careless promises. The same visitor may receive food, a warning, an impossible task or preparation for the cooking pot according to conduct and jurisdiction rather than moral alignment.

Wolf and Lantern enter the House while travelling together shortly before the War of the Empty Halls. They are being hunted by an improvised company of their own victims: members of several households who compared accounts of theft, fraudulent charms, betrayed confidences and abused guest-right, then banded together to catch them. The company is neither a formal court nor a blameless mob, but its grievances against the pair are substantially true. Wolf and Lantern petition the Old Mother for shelter beneath her roof and safe passage through the forest. She accepts their request and keeps the pursuers from reaching them, but the agreement does not promise that the travellers will be free to leave her House. Their historical ordeal should use the broad Baba-Yaga grammar without copying a named folklore episode.

The pair receive shelter, food and several plainly stated courtesies, then commit three escalating breaches while believing themselves unheard. First they mock the Old Mother's age, apparent deafness and supposed stupidity, congratulating themselves on having cheated a helpless elder. Second they plan to rob the House after their hostess sleeps and laugh over which objects each will steal. Third they turn their cruelty upon the Daughter, insulting her appearance and speaking of her as something they could deceive, use or harm without consequence. The House hears all three.

Each breach produces one corresponding trial. The interior paths close, the ordinary supper room unfolds into a much larger and more dangerous household, and the Old Mother can truthfully say that she has kept both her promise and the petitioners safe from their pursuers. The danger now enclosing them is the unpaid consequence of what they failed to ask and how they behaved after receiving it. The three trials should mirror the offences in sequence and grow from humiliating danger into a genuinely consequential choice; they must not function as three arbitrary monster rooms.

**First trial — Borrowed Years.** In answer to their mockery of the Old Mother's age and apparent helplessness, the House takes the youth and bodily strength of both men. They become bent, shaking, short of breath and painfully slow while their minds remain clear. In that condition they must complete dangerous household work across rooms and stairs built for their younger bodies. Neither strength, threats nor clever magical fraud can substitute for the work. The task can be finished only by patience, careful coordination and each rogue carrying the other when his body fails. Abandoning or mocking the weaker partner makes the borrowed age permanent. Successful completion restores enough strength to face the second trial; whether either man retains a lesser bodily mark is reserved for the final cost.

**Second trial — The Treasury That Takes.** In answer to their plan to rob their host, the House opens a treasury containing objects each rogue genuinely desires. The Old Mother permits each man to carry one treasure to the next room. The permission is real, but the burden grows in proportion to possessive desire: gold becomes heavier than millstones, a coveted weapon drags its bearer towards the floor, and every stolen object already carried by the pair acquires the same weight. The threshold cannot be crossed through concealment, substitution or an argument that permission erased prior theft. They pass only by abandoning the offered treasures, their stolen property and the weapons or tools bound up with their betrayals. Objects honestly given or earned may remain light, although which possessions meet that test is not yet fixed. The trial leaves them materially poorer and deliberately unarmed before they face the Daughter.

**Third trial — The Bride of Teeth.** In answer to their boast that the Daughter was something easily deceived, used or taken, she receives their words as a proposal. She first appears in the youthful and attractive shape they presumed to judge, then reveals the terrible second expression they mocked. If one of them meant to take her, she says, one must now become hers as servant or consort. The choice is real enough to bind the man who accepts it. Having surrendered their weapons and dishonest tools, the rogues cannot defeat her; nor can they pass by flattering her beauty, denying what they said or treating her monstrous form as proof that she is not a person. They must formulate an honest third term which recognizes her freedom to refuse and offers a consequence equal to their offence. The exact accepted alternative remains to be chosen.

Instead of bargaining over which man must possess or be possessed by her, Wolf and Lantern finally ask the Daughter what she wants. She chooses **one night beyond the threshold**: passage into the ordinary mortal forest until dawn, with both rogues serving as her escorts and hosts. They must extend to her the guest-right they repeatedly abused and accept responsibility for her conduct without treating that responsibility as ownership of her person. The Daughter accepts this freely as the alternative term. Crossing the House's outer door therefore completes the enclosed trial but begins its most dangerous consequence. Until dawn they must preserve the duties of host and guest, protect mortals from any appetite or caprice she displays, protect her from anyone who attacks a welcomed stranger, and resolve conflict without the weapons and fraudulent tools surrendered in the treasury.

The Daughter chooses to spend that night at a nearby village revel. She demands ordinary mortal pleasures rather than an occult errand: music, abundant food and the right to dance until dawn. In her youthful presentation she can pass as a strange and striking traveller, but Wolf and Lantern must persuade the village to receive all three, preserve her incognito and uphold reciprocal guest-right. They are caught between her predatory appetites and the villagers' capacity for drink, boasting, desire and violence. A breach by either side could release her terrible second expression and turn the festival into a massacre. The rogues must therefore practise the restraint, truthful speech and respect for another person's boundaries which they lacked inside the House.

The festival creates two opposed crises. First, a powerful local man—either a war-leader or the son of a leading household—refuses the Daughter's refusal and attempts to seize her. Wolf and Lantern must defend the personal boundary they themselves mocked, despite being unarmed and despite the political danger of striking or humiliating him. The Daughter begins to reveal her second expression and considers devouring not only the offender but his wider household. Her real grievance does not make collective predation just, so the rogues must protect their guest while preventing her retaliation from becoming a massacre.

Lantern resolves the immediate danger through wordplay and misdirection rather than assembly judgement or force. He declares that the Daughter favours a man capable of emptying the greatest cup at the revel, then identifies the village well as the cup from which every household drinks. The offender accepts the apparent contest and tries to drink the well dry. Whether helped by festival drunkenness, stubborn literalism or later comic exaggeration, he persists until he falls inside. The well confines him without killing him. The Daughter is sufficiently entertained by the reversal to spare his household and return to the dancing.

Later audiences understand the trick's irony more clearly than its victim does. The man tried to treat a woman's refusal as an obstacle to be overcome and instead became captive inside an appetite no body could satisfy. Villagers rescue him after dawn, wet, sick and humiliated. By then the Daughter has returned beyond the threshold and Wolf and Lantern have already left. Some later tellers make the well literally bottomless or the drinking magically compelled; neither embellishment is required by the historical event.

Later, a village child who has wandered from parental guardianship approaches the Daughter without fear. The Daughter is intrigued by the child's courage and simultaneously entertains the possibility of eating them. This reverses the first crisis: Wolf and Lantern must now protect a vulnerable villager from the guest whose safety they just defended. The child's lack of fear does not constitute consent, and parental neglect does not make the child unclaimed prey.

The child innocently offers the strange woman festival food or asks her to dance, names her as a guest and thereby invokes the hospitality the village has extended. Lantern recognizes the protective meaning before the Daughter bites and states it formally, buying time to find the child's guardians. The wording checks the Daughter but does not mechanically erase her appetite or make her morally safe. When she presses against the edge of the protection and continues to toy with the possibility of eating the child, Wolf offers his own flesh as a freely given substitute. The Daughter bites two fingers from his knife hand, accepts the offering and releases the child. Wolf can learn to fight again, but never with his old speed or grip. Later art, bridge marks and tale cycles identify him by the shortened hand; versions which restore it belong to heroic embellishment.

They escape by the skin of their teeth through manners, wit and the completion of all three trials rather than superior force. At least one must acknowledge an earlier betrayal, perform dangerous household work and find truthful forms of address that neither flatter falsely nor deny the personhood and authority of those they mocked. Their survival does not make them innocent and does not make the House good. Later tellers use the ordeal to teach children and boastful travellers: be polite to elders, do not repay hospitality with theft, attend to the exact words of a host, mind the promises made by your tongue and keep enough wit to recognize when laughter has become cruelty.

The **Daughter in the historical ordeal** is a genuine nonhuman court-person with a youthful or attractive presentation and a far more dangerous second expression. She is separate from both the House and the Old Mother. The Mother's rules restrain the Daughter's predation as well as the behaviour of guests.

That Daughter is not the only figure later entitled *daughter*. Across centuries the Old Mother also adopts mortal foster-daughters and witch-apprentices, shelters changelings and recognizes lesser court-beings as household kin. Some human apprentices leave and become formidable witches with legendary cycles of their own; they retain complete human souls regardless of bodily alteration or court craft. Nonhuman court-daughters retain their separately classified continuities. Later tellers compress this household tradition into one endless succession of hag or changeling daughters, often attaching the acts of one to another. This uses the broad daughter-apprentice grammar without importing another setting's person or cosmology.

The Old Mother's human continuity, bargain-to-independence progression and portfolio of perilous petition are fixed. Wolf and Lantern's request for shelter and safe passage is also fixed. Their pursuers were an improvised company of people they had separately wronged. Mockery of the Old Mother, a plan to rob the House and cruelty towards the Daughter form three escalating breaches and generate three corresponding trials. Borrowed Years ages and weakens them while they complete dangerous household work through patience and mutual aid. The Treasury That Takes then forces them to abandon coveted treasure, stolen goods and the tools of their betrayals. The Bride of Teeth holds their objectifying boast as a proposal; when they ask what she desires instead, the Daughter accepts one night at a village revel under their escort and guest-right. There Lantern sends a powerful coercive man to drink the village well dry; the man falls in, and the irony amuses the Daughter enough to prevent retaliation. Later, a child's innocent hospitality delays the Daughter's appetite, Lantern formalises the protection and Wolf sacrifices two fingers from his knife hand to secure the child's release. Her original price, exact emancipation and the pair's longer aftermath may remain separate development questions rather than gaps in this event.

### Separate development tangent — rival boon-patron and awakened familiar

This pair has **no role in G-06 or the historical ordeal of Wolf and Lantern**. The concept is retained for its own later stories and dossier.

A second powerful boon-broker and their beast-shaped companion operate independently of the Old Mother. The rival began as a mortal human sorcerer or occult petitioner, then followed a different route to self-apotheosis. They bound their complete human soul into an Egg White anchor, accumulated a demiplane, proxies and dependable sources of upkeep, and eventually became a major **Patron** in the practical extradimensional sense. Patron is their present economic and ecological role rather than a replacement species: their continuity remains `bound-human-soul`, not a native `astral-pattern` or newly manufactured divinity.

The rival offers services overlapping the Old Mother's wider portfolio, so the two powers may compete for clients, access, reputation and the future obligations created by successful petitions outside this tale. Where the Old Mother works through intimate encounters, household trials, riddling omissions and singular craft, the rival presents a polished service and converts boons into leases, collateral and compounding obligations. Neither style is automatically safer.

The rival's beast-companion began as an ordinary mortal animal and familiar. It was carried through the sorcerer's Egg White ascent in uninterrupted creaturely continuity, altered by the same anchor and initially dependent upon the new Patron's power. Long exposure, accumulated power and active participation in bargains enlarged its awareness until it became a genuinely autonomous nonhuman person. This development created no human soul and detached no piece of the Patron's human soul. The companion's present continuity should be treated as an individually continuous transformed beast with `astral-pattern`, not as a construct, proxy or decorative fauna.

After awakening, the companion bargained for and accumulated a contractual share in the Patron's estate, access and power. It is now a junior co-owner able to advise, restrain, undermine or in some circumstances abandon the Patron. Their disagreements sometimes give petitioners a warning or way out, but do not make the companion reliably benevolent. The pair may use the broad dramatic grammar of a malicious wish-granter and an intimate companion who complicates the bargain, but they must be original World Egg persons. The form and temperament of the beast, the precise division of power and the origin of any rivalry with the Old Mother remain unresolved.

### G-07 working ruling — one Bent-Key crisis

The false capital accusation, archive theft, linked exposure of debt and judicial corruption, siege, foreign widow's killing of the besieging commander, prison opening and disputed civic reconstruction all belong to one historical crisis at **Verion**, around 1000 BR. The date remains approximate within a generation, but the event belongs to Verion's development as an eastern Atherian assembly port where shipping households, visiting crews, military suppliers, judges and public records met. It is separate from Erenost's earlier grain seizure, the raid for the stolen sounding-weight and Caleran's royal and republican foundations.

The Bent-Key Fellowship was one real company assembled initially to steal a debt tablet rather than a composite roster created by later singers. Individual recensions may foreground the separated witnesses, widow, locksmith, riots or amnesty and may misorder episodes, but those differences do not divide the history into separate events.

The **debt thief** devised the separated questioning. His immediate purpose was not disinterested justice: forcing the two elders to answer concrete questions apart would delay the execution, occupy court officials and create an opening for the fellowship to enter the archive. While listening for signs that the diversion was working, he noticed that their accounts differed over the tree or courtyard landmark beneath which they claimed the adultery occurred. The inconsistency exposed coordination around the accusation without a shared witnessed event. His selfish tactic therefore produced a just result he had not initially sought. The accused woman's refusal, testimony and willingness to survive public examination remain indispensable; the thief did not save a passive figure by cleverness alone.

Verion's archive kept its principal debt, judgement, property and grain records on inscribed clay tablets. Many recent tablets remained unfired on shelves so clerks could correct, replace or recycle them under witnessed procedure. The fellowship entered seeking the tablet whose enforcement would sell the debt thief's sister's children. What they found were repeated names, matching seizures and grain transfers linking the false accusers, bought judges, creditor houses and military suppliers.

During the uprising the fellowship and rioters attempted to destroy part of the debt system by burning the archive. Fire destroyed shelving, containers and records on perishable materials, while baking many clay tablets into harder and more durable evidence. Some tablets shattered, were removed or were deliberately pulverised; others survived the fire precisely because of the attempted destruction. The later distinction between records destroyed to stop imminent enforcement and records preserved to prove powerful wrongdoing arose from this unintended firing. A burned tablet does not itself cancel a debt or establish guilt: release, amended record, witnesses and settlement remain necessary.

The **locksmith** killed an archive guard during the original break-in. The killing enabled the theft and cannot be removed from the fellowship's later legal reputation, even where accounts dispute whether the guard struck first or understood the conspiracy he protected. After the victory, officials of the emerging settlement demanded the archive key. The locksmith heated and bent it rather than give any new ruler the same uncontested access claimed by the old houses. The damaged key could no longer operate the lock in the ordinary way. It became an admission that the city's repair had passed through theft and murder, not a declaration that murder itself conferred authority. Later amnesty fires inherited the gesture but require their own lawful release, corrected records, witnesses and material remedy.

The falsely accused woman did **not** spend the remainder of her life seated outside Verion's rebuilt hearing hall. She survived the accusation and public examination, gave testimony during the crisis, refused every magistracy or honour offered afterwards and left the city once travel was possible. Her destination and later life remain unknown. Reformers installed an empty witness-seat outside the hall in a later generation and invoked her memory when establishing appeal procedure. Civic storytelling gradually placed the living woman in that seat, transforming her refusal to remain into a loyal lifetime of service. The memorial could still warn those summoned inside, but it also allowed Verion to conceal that its celebrated survivor chose exile over reconciliation.

The **disgraced soldier** became the founder of Verion's post-crisis dynasty. Command during the siege restored his reputation and placed armed followers, grain distribution and negotiation with defecting mercenaries under his influence. What began as emergency service hardened into personal office and then hereditary rule. His descendants presented the fellowship as a lawful patriotic force acting under their founder's leadership. Official accounts reduced the debt thief's family motive, omitted the locksmith's killing during the archive break-in and treated the widow's independent operation as service to the new ruler. The dynasty's later stability does not retroactively make that account true.

The siege grew directly from the archive exposure. Creditor and shipping households implicated by the stolen records lost control of part of Verion, withdrew people and portable wealth, and hired a foreign mercenary commander to restore their judges and property claims. They promised payment from the city's military grain, confiscated houses, debtor sales and future harbour privileges. The commander therefore besieged Verion on behalf of a local restoration faction rather than attacking for an unrelated foreign war. His camp joined displaced oligarchs, paid soldiers, dependants and coerced personnel. Killing him broke the coalition's command and opened negotiation or dispersal; it did not by itself defeat every interested household or cleanse the city.

The fellowship's **foreign widow** was born outside Verion but had lived in the port for years as part of a merchant household. Her spouse resisted an unlawful seizure of reserved grain by the same creditor and shipping network and was killed by its enforcers; the surviving records disguised that killing as lawful recovery or disorder around the stores. The widow retained commercial contacts, knowledge of warehouses and provisioning, and familiarity with more than one harbour language. Discovery of the archive link joined her personal revenge to the city's wider crisis. She acts for her adopted city as well as her dead spouse, but neither motive sanctifies every later deception or the personal killing and beheading of the commander.

She entered the mercenary camp through an invitation framed around private protection and political alliance. The widow allowed the commander to believe that a foreign-born merchant woman abandoned by Verion might prefer the security of his victorious household. She did not explicitly promise marriage or sexual access. Instead, she relied upon his lechery, vanity and presumption to supply that meaning, accept a private meeting and relax or remove his safeguards. His expectation was chosen rather than magically compelled; the ambiguity of her approach is part of the deception but does not create consent to what he intended.

During the private meal she encouraged the commander to drink, remained sober enough to act and waited until he had dismissed his guards and fallen asleep. She killed him with his own ceremonial sword and severed his head with the same weapon. Removing and carrying the head supplied proof to both the besieged city and rival officers who might otherwise conceal his death. The act was planned assassination performed by the widow herself. His vulnerability and political guilt do not turn killing a sleeping man into an impersonal or morally costless deed.

This ruling does not make the fellowship innocent or credit it with inventing city law. Criminal self-interest helped begin the theft; the locksmith's killing of the archive guard and the widow's planned killing and beheading of the sleeping commander remain part of the victory. The disgraced soldier's effective siege command founded a dynasty which suppressed the company's criminal beginning. The woman's refusal and testimony remain the bodily and legal centre of the crisis rather than an excuse for a male founder epic. Exact personal names, the soldier's earlier disgrace and ultimate individual fates may remain for later dossiers without preventing promotion of the event.

### G-08 working ruling — one road, composite surveyor

The Road That Refused Its Last Stone preserves one real Atherian trunk road developed during the Age of Stars, but its apparently single surveyor is a composite biography. Several generations of surveyors, cord-bearers, stone crews and league officials began, extended, widened and remeasured the route under changing governments. The tale compresses their useful judgements, career compromises, protests and continuing commissions into one lifetime so that cumulative institutional complicity can be remembered as a human moral argument.

The earliest episode in which three boundary stones settled a grazing dispute may belong to one founding surveyor. Later straightening, land seizure, settlement removal, labour levies and punitive expeditions belong to successive expansions. Later imperial language retrojects the terminology of the empire onto league commissioners, military magistrates and republican governors. The road's real development is therefore institutional rather than biographical: each regime inherited a useful corridor, enlarged its logistical value and imposed part of the cost upon communities with less civic power. Labour deaths and destroyed settlements are historical, while the tale's claim that the paving was deliberately laid over the victims' graves is a later moral image rather than an established engineering policy.

The best-attested refusal concerned one late official milestone. A mixed labour crew raised the prepared stone but withheld the dedication which would have named the commissioning authority and represented the newest extension as its achievement. This was a small act with a practical opportunity: inscription drafts, tools and access all passed through the workers' hands. It did not magically invalidate the road or prevent the state from measuring distance. Soon afterwards the commissioning league or road office fractured during a wider administrative collapse. Records, pay and inspection ceased before the inscription could be completed or the responsible workers securely identified. Worker action created the blank stone; institutional failure preserved it.

Later road traditions condensed both causes into the single surveyor's last refusal. Official histories preferred the opposite simplification, treating the stone as an ordinary unfinished work and excluding worker intent. Neither version is complete. The road remained materially useful to armies and merchants, and the absence of one dedication did not dissolve its violence or ownership claims elsewhere.

The road was the **Aurin trunk road**, running inland from Caleran's river-mouth corridor through country inhabited by Enathi, Serathi and Vardeni communities. Its first measured sections joined and regularised older local paths, boundary tracks, river landings and routes between fields. Successive Compact cities, Ortheian hegemonic officials and the recovered Caleran republic extended, straightened and militarised different segments. Later imperial administration inherited the corridor but did not create its oldest works.

The road moved food, market traffic, messages and travellers as well as troops, assessments and coerced workers. That usefulness explains why successive governments maintained and extended it even when they rejected their predecessors' claims. Older routes remained useful where the official alignment cut across settlement boundaries. Straight approaches displaced houses and fields; local resistance to seizure or labour levies sometimes brought punitive columns and burning. Enathi, Serathi and Vardeni inhabitants were not passive terrain through which an Atherian road passed: their existing roads and labour made the system possible, while their unequal civic standing made several communities especially vulnerable to removal and conscription.

The final sabotage was carried out by one mixed coerced labour gang including Enathi, Serathi and Vardeni workers alongside Atherian debt-labourers. Some possessed inscription or setting skills; others moved the stone, delayed supervisors, destroyed drafts or kept silent afterwards. Their cooperation crossed cultural and legal categories without creating a permanent secret order. No single planner or spokesman can be securely recovered, and no later people inherits exclusive credit. The composite surveyor legend is partly the result of elite and literary traditions preferring one remorseful educated protagonist to collective action by workers.

The long absence of official milestones farther along the inherited corridor has a material history rather than a single ritual cause. Successor governments changed reference points and alignments, reused or quarried older stones, and ceased maintaining marginal sections when the central road office failed. Local travellers then raised ordinary cairns and informal waystones at forks, water sources, abandoned settlements and dangerous stretches because the route still carried traffic without dependable official marking. These markers were practical, local and accumulated over generations; they were not one coordinated anti-imperial custom.

The beloved claim that children began the cairns, gave them private destinations and rebuilt them whenever officials cleared them belongs to the later tale. It turns the contrast between official measurement and local knowledge into a memorable image. Some communities may now teach or reenact that story, but the original child protest is not authorial history. The grounded causal sequence is: inherited local paths became a state trunk road; military and fiscal usefulness drove repeated expansion; unequal status enabled coerced labour and displacement; one crew withheld a ruler's dedication; administrative collapse left it unfinished; later neglect removed the wider official marker system; travellers supplied practical local markers; tradition personified the whole history as one surveyor and a company of defiant children.

### G-56 working ruling — one bounded succession crisis

The Empty Seat Facing Outward preserves one real authority crisis in the principal outward camp shortly after the Prophet's death. Its governing scriptural grammar is the approach to Canaan and the divided report of the scouts: the people stand before inhabited country containing Giant-blooded powers, fortified nations and frightening kingdoms; reconnaissance makes both the danger and the obligation to leave refuge concrete; courage, fear and competing interpretations of obedience then divide the camp. This is an author-facing pattern rather than an in-world copy of the biblical episode.

A recognisable Joshua-like figure stood at the centre of the disagreement: a trusted scout-captain with real courage, competence and a legitimate personal commission from the Prophet. Before his death, the Prophet appointed this captain to lead reconnaissance and guide the first safe movement beyond the Glass. The mandate authorised him to identify a viable route and protect those who followed; it did not transfer revelation, grant government over every household or make inhabited territory a divine allotment. He argued that the Lord's command to leave the Glass required movement into the inhabited lands despite the strength of their rulers. Other powerful commanders, judges, provisioners and household coalitions did not all deny the command; they disputed its timing, route, military method, acceptable cost and the scout-captain's authority to bind everyone to his reading. Some feared the Giant-blooded remnants and organised kingdoms outside. Others believed caution, continued refuge practice or slow reconnaissance was the more faithful course. Still others accepted departure but wanted different captains and destinations.

No universal successor emerged, and the scout-captain did not become an ancestor of Atherian conquest. He made repeated outward journeys, returned to the Near-Glass continuum and entered the stream of leadership memory which later became distinctly Jirahar. His answer to the end of the Glass refuge was not to seize and permanently rule the first defensible territory. He organised continuing circuits of reconnaissance, escort and return which kept dispersed households informed and connected while new routes, dangers, water sources, graves and neighbouring communities were learned.

This mobile life consciously preserved something of the Prophet's own movement during exile without pretending that the captain inherited the Prophet's voice. A leader could go ahead, protect a company and judge immediate danger, then return with testimony rather than convert successful guidance into a territorial crown. The practice was sustained for practical reasons as well as sacred memory: no single new settlement could safely support the whole remnant, routes and seasons changed, separated camps needed messages and escorts, and movement reduced dependence upon any one stronghold or ruler.

Later Jirahar road companies, portable courts and Keepers of the Path inherited different parts of this precedent. They may remember the scout-captain as an ancestral leader of the road, but none of their mature offices need have existed under its later name during his lifetime. His legacy is institutional and devotional rather than proof that one bloodline founded the Jirahar or that the still-mixed Near-Glass continuum was already a completed people. The more aggressive southern founders were rival captains who used knowledge opened by his reconnaissance while rejecting or enlarging its limits.

Some companies left, fought, occupied works and founded the earliest southern war-settlement networks. Other households remained in the Near-Glass camp continuum and continued familiar routes, offices and refuge practices. The scout-captain belonged to this latter institutional ancestry without thereby making the not-yet-formed continuum already Jirahar. Further departures, returns, intermarriages and internal household divisions followed over generations. The confrontation therefore supplies the emotional and political beginning of the first fission without turning one meeting into the instant creation of Atherians, Jirahar and Ahdari.

The scout-captain was not the commander later remembered in [[G-71 - The Five Staffs and the Empty Granary]]. A rival outward commander used the reconnaissance, routes and public urgency created by the scout-captain's mission to justify a more coercive expedition. That rival treated delay as collective death, seized grain allocated to households remaining near the Glass and converted emergency shares into military settlement allotments. The distinction matters: a legitimate commission to find and protect a way outward created knowledge and opportunity which another office-holder then enlarged into powers the Prophet had not granted. The later expedition's material success explains why the abuse was defended instead of promptly punished.

The exact duration of three days, the original chair, the singer's verbatim rebuke, the dramatic overturning and the boastful soldier pulled away by his mother are later narrative forms. Empty places in later assemblies commemorate the non-inheritable commission; they do not authenticate one surviving chair or a universally continuous rite.

### G-57 working ruling — a regional memory of murder under trust

The Gate Opened during Supper is not the recoverable chronicle of one first southern foundation. It is a regional conquest archetype formed from several betrayals during the river-gate settlement period, when armed Glass-descended companies sought winter ground, food, service contracts and defensible crossings among existing Orphaned communities. Some were admitted as auxiliaries or protected guests and later helped a local faction overthrow its rivals. Some opened a gate to companions outside. Some hosts attempted to disarm, detain or divide settlers first. At least one remembered killing occurred during a communal meal, but massacre, hostage-taking, enslavement, captive rebuilding and the later mixed defence of the settlement need not all belong to the same night or town.

Its primary author-facing historical and legendary grammars are the **Massacre of Glencoe** and the **Sabine women**. Glencoe supplies the moral category of murder under trust: armed outsiders accepted, fed and housed under an established obligation later attack the people protecting them, and the breach of hospitality makes one massacre culturally distinctive among many acts of violence. The Sabine foundation tradition supplies the more difficult civic afterlife: violence against invited guests is placed at the beginning of a polity whose later population genuinely descends from and belongs to both sides. G-57 does not reproduce either event's participants or exact sequence; it uses their joined structure to explain why a mixed city can condemn its foundation without denying its descendants' belonging.

The composite survived because these events damaged the practical trust on which movement and exchange depended. A host who admitted travellers risked importing an armed claimant; a travelling company which surrendered its weapons risked detention or murder. River settlements, caravan households and mixed successor towns therefore developed a controlling **shared-meal peace**. Once host and traveller publicly ate from the same prepared meal, neither could attack, detain or secretly admit armed companions against the other until the guest had been formally released. Crossing a threshold, declaring companions and depositing weapons could prove that protection was intended, but none replaced the witnessed shared meal as the central act. Violence after that meal carried aggravated compensation, feud and civic consequences. These controls arose through repeated negotiation and retaliation rather than one legislator's decree.

Later Jirahar road companies became especially visible users, interpreters and carriers of these protections. The underlying rule was not an ethnic immunity for Jirahar and never prevented hostility, persecution or ordinary criminal jurisdiction. It made violence against a traveller already accepted under guest-right a distinctive breach of public trust, regardless of the traveller's people. Jirahar portable courts and route records helped communities prove that protection had been offered and that obligations had been broken.

One old repaired river gate became the principal physical focus of the wider cycle. Its bricks, mortar and repair surfaces preserve hand and finger impressions from several building phases. Some appear to be deliberate maker marks; others may be incidental traces left while materials were shaped or set. Neither chronology nor surviving evidence can identify one row specifically as the work of captive masons, and no impression can be labelled an innocent or guilty founder. Later mixed communities selected this materially layered gate as the memorial focus because its visible hands made the region's otherwise dispersed history graspable.

The dark supper, weapons left outside, ceremonial pressing of clean hands and children's question are later commemorative forms practised at this gate. Their value does not depend upon proving that it was the site of the first or largest betrayal. The monument anchors a true regional history without converting the composite tale into the chronicle of one night.

### G-58 working ruling — Sermora's twice-founded harbour

The Harbour Founded Twice belongs to one principal ancestral harbour among the islands and pilot communities which later formed Sermora. The drowned-hand reef, dangerous entrance and dependence upon local pilots fit the material geography of that harbour. Southern ship households did not discover an empty anchorage: an existing Orphaned shore population possessed fishing grounds, seasonal dwellings, landing knowledge and the practical means to guide vessels safely through the reef.

The historical core returns to the original TF-05 sequence. A damaged proto-Atherian convoy required food, timber, labour and a protected anchorage in which to survive the winter and repair its vessels. Harbour elders granted the newcomers a seasonal quarter under toll and guest terms. The captains cut a defensive ditch and raised a stockade, then used armed control of the harbour grain store to extend their stay and dictate passage. Temporary refuge became occupation through a comprehensible mechanism: whoever controlled stored food, the repair yard and the guarded shore could make departure impossible for dependants and resistance costly for local households.

Opponents were killed, sold, forced inland or compelled into rowing, repair and harbour labour. Other shore households entered the protected market, allied with particular captains or joined the occupying settlement through marriage and advantage. Braiding therefore began under unequal and sometimes violent conditions rather than through one reconciliation. Local pilots, net-makers, warehouse families, settler dependants, captives and refugee crews nevertheless became materially interdependent over the following generation.

The second foundation was a successful liberation struggle, not a second providential storm. When resistance and harbour defections threatened the occupation, the settlers' parent league sent a punitive fleet to restore tribute, secure the grain store and prevent a valuable naval quarter from becoming independent. Local pilots knew the drowned-hand reef and its changing passage. Returned exiles, escaped captives, shore fighters and members of settler households unwilling to accept renewed league rule cooperated to misdirect, strand and attack the punitive ships. The victory broke external enforcement but did not expel every descendant of the first convoy. A mixed harbour council released remaining captives, reopened access to the water and rebuilt the damaged anchorage as an independent community.

The harbour was therefore founded twice in two genuinely historical senses: the first landing created the fortified maritime settlement through occupation, and the defeat of the punitive fleet created an independent shared polity. Neither foundation was innocent. Some first settlers opposed later abuses; some local houses profited from the captive market; liberation fighters also killed men who had not personally begun the occupation. The later population was genuinely Braided without making every Sermoran citizen a descendant of two original groups or turning this harbour into the sole ancestor of the capital islands.

The Houses of the First Mooring inherited the dispute over founder status. Southern-descended houses emphasised the landing, stockade and construction of a defensible ship base. Pilot, shore and liberation-descended houses emphasised the reef victory, opened water and independence from the parent league. One civic calendar begins with the first arrival; the other begins with the punitive fleet's defeat. No family may lead both launch days, preventing one house from converting participation in both memories into exclusive hereditary title to the whole foundation. The division shapes ceremonial precedence and arguments over political legitimacy without itself creating the mature Doge or Admiralty.

The later tale's thirteen boats, single shore headwoman, captive sons and heroic daughter are narrative compression. The claim that she rescued her oppressors' grandchildren during a second storm belongs to a conciliatory recension which displaces the harder history of revolt, naval destruction and negotiated independence. It may survive as one telling, but it does not control the promoted history.

### G-60 working ruling — *The Son-in-Law and the Stolen Gods*

The Bride of the Dry Road is to be rebuilt around its stated Genesis 31 grammar with a male protagonist rather than an inverted female foundress. A young man from a Near-Glass office-holding and route-literate family entered a powerful Tarkhan-facing route household as a politically useful son-in-law after a cycle of caravan seizure, well reprisal and captive exchange. His position combined marriage, service and hostage-like dependence: he was protected and given a place within the household, but his father-in-law retained leverage over his labour, children, movement and promised share of the herds or caravan wealth.

The son-in-law made two political marriages into different branches of the route coalition. The women were not substituted sisters: each marriage settled a separate relationship and brought its own witnesses, property expectations and kin obligations. The arrangement widened peace but also placed the husband between branches which did not agree about water, precedence, herds or succession. Both wives acquired real interests and relationships within the joined household rather than existing only as rewards for his service.

Over years the son-in-law became indispensable as a herdsman, organiser, negotiator and keeper of witnessed terms. His labour enlarged the household, while the route patriarch repeatedly reinterpreted wages, marriage portions, stock divisions and departure rights in his own favour. The marriages were real and the family formed within them was real; neither fact made the unequal agreements or later attempts to retain them as property just.

One wife was unmistakably the husband's favourite. She bore fewer sons than the other marriage branch but received more of his private confidence, affection and later political preference. During the departure she secretly took the household's portable idols of its gods. These were genuine objects of domestic and dynastic worship, not merely decorative tokens or disguised account seals. Their holiness, custody and association with the household's continuity made their removal a grave religious and political injury, but the authorial account does not establish that a god inhabited them, spoke through them or could be contacted through them like a communicative artefact. The theft therefore gives the father-in-law a serious additional cause for pursuit without requiring the idols to possess independent supernatural agency. She did not take them as a calculated inheritance device. Although she chose to leave with her husband, she did not yet understand or trust the nature and power of his God sufficiently to surrender the familiar protections of her father's gods. Fear led her to carry both allegiances with her in secret. When her father's pursuit overtook the caravan and searched it, she hid the idols inside her travelling saddle-seat, remained seated over them and invoked the customary privacy owed to a menstruating woman. Her condition and the taboo were genuine grounds on which a senior man could not demand an intimate search; she used them deceptively to conceal what lay beneath her. The idols therefore escaped discovery through social intelligence rather than supernatural intervention. Certain that nobody in his household had taken them, her husband publicly declared before the assembled kin that the unknown thief must die. He did not know that he had condemned his favourite wife. She later died young in childbirth. Descendant traditions connected that death to his rash words, but the authorial history does not confirm that the declaration operated as a supernatural curse or divine sentence. Before her death she entrusted the idols and their secret to an attendant or an older child within her own smaller branch. Her sons consequently received them as inherited sacred property and as a hidden connection to their mother's people. Their later discovery entangled continued worship, possession of stolen household goods, their mother's deception and the public death declaration their father had made. The husband destroyed or ritually buried the idols once they surfaced, rejecting their worship and ending their use as portable cult objects. He nevertheless refused to execute, expel or disinherit sons who had not committed their mother's theft. This was a defensible refusal of inherited punishment, but the more numerous elder branch understood it through his established favouritism and argued that his rules changed whenever the beloved wife's children stood to suffer. The inheritance leverage was not her original motive, but the idols became a durable source of religious and succession conflict before their destruction. Later descendants could remember her fear as piety, cunning or treachery. The larger body of sons from the other marriage supplied more labour, fighters and seniority claims; the smaller favoured branch received disproportionate access to the father's plans and portable wealth. That imbalance becomes a source of later family conflict rather than proof that either wife or set of children was divinely preferred.

The story's crisis remains its refusal to surrender people accepted under protection. When fugitives or vulnerable dependants sought the son-in-law's guest-law, his wife's senior kin demanded their return. He refused by citing the household's own witnessed obligations. Armed relatives killed his acknowledged peace-kin during the confrontation, destroying the alliance which had originally secured his marriage and residence. The killing supplied an immediate danger and a legal breach; accumulated exploitation supplied the longer reason to leave.

The son-in-law departed with both wives, their children, servants, protected fugitives, allotted animals and the records needed to establish what had been promised. His father-in-law pursued, claiming theft of household people, stock and the concealed household objects. The confrontation ended in a witnessed boundary settlement rather than a clean battlefield victory: the broken alliance was acknowledged, disputed property was divided or left under compensation claims, and neither house could cross the marked boundary to seize the departing dependants. The new travelling household gained lawful separation without proving every possession innocent or eliminating later feud.

This is one mixed road household, not the biological origin of the Jirahar, Ahdari, Tarkhan or any later people. Different traditions can remember the protagonist as a cunning herdsman, faithful protector, contract-reader or dangerous thief. The present red-veil and road-mother recension is a later gender inversion and no longer controls the authorial event. The promoted public article is to be titled *The Son-in-Law and the Stolen Gods*: the son-in-law remains the principal Jacob-pattern protagonist, while the traditional language of "stolen gods" preserves the favourite wife's decisive secret without asserting that the idols were themselves gods. The event is ready for promotion.

## VI. Reclaimed from the Reserve

### G-71 working ruling — the requisition that became policy

The Five Staffs tradition began with one identifiable abuse rather than an accidental accumulation of unrelated drought stories. A rival of G-56's commissioned scout-captain used the captain's reconnaissance, viable southern routes and the public fear of remaining too long near the Glass to demand an expeditionary grain reserve before rear-camp provisioners had completed their dependant counts. The commander believed that delay would lose the road and perhaps the season; he also converted a contested emergency power into command over who would receive the future settlement's land and stores.

The first expedition materially succeeded. That success protected the commander from removal and allowed his expedition council to turn the exceptional requisition into a repeated policy over several seasons. Grain assigned to households remaining near the Glass was progressively reclassified as seed, marching provision and inheritable military settlement allotment. The policy therefore outlived the original moment of urgency and continued after its lethal effects on the rear population could no longer be denied.

Repeated requisitions reduced food security across a wider ring of rear settlements. The remembered two camps were nevertheless real: their poor connections, weak transport and high number of children, sick people, elders and other dependants left them least able to replace diverted grain. They suffered the clearest and most concentrated famine deaths. Other camps endured rationing, flight, disease, household dispersal and isolated deaths without collapsing to the same degree. The traditional phrase "two camps starved" therefore preserves the worst cases while compressing the policy's wider human reach.

Later tradition compresses the initiating seizure, repeated appropriations, changing office-holders and delayed hearings into a single empty granary scene. Three remembered staff-bearers preserve identifiable historical participants: the expedition commander who ordered the first requisition, the chief provisioner who objected because the dependant accounts were incomplete, and the presiding judge who later heard the challenge. Their actual insignia, tenure and relationship were less tidy than the tale suggests. The testimony staff and household-muster staff were later ritual additions. They personify the many witnesses whose evidence accumulated over the crisis and the uncounted dependants whose absence from the first calculation made the seizure possible; they do not preserve two lost founders or permanent officers.

The final account and judgment were genuinely heard inside the stripped provisioning granary. The chief provisioner selected the place so the missing stores and empty bins stood as material evidence rather than metaphor. The commander, provisioner and judge were personally present or represented by authorised staff-bearers; received accounts disagree over whether the commander himself returned. Those three staffs therefore preserve a historical hearing even though their speeches were later formalised. The testimony and household-muster staffs were added to commemorative reenactments to give the dispersed witnesses and absent dead a place on the floor.

The judge ruled that the commander had exceeded a temporary mandate by taking the grain before the required provision account was heard. The judgment ordered the commander's removal and restitution to the harmed households. It did not pretend that restitution could revive the dead or recover grain already eaten and sown. The successful southern expedition council refused to remove its commander, surrender the resulting allotments or accept the rear court's practical jurisdiction. The commander remains a real culpable actor, while the council and officials who renewed the policy share responsibility. Its success does not vindicate it: settlers gained a real future partly because politically weaker dependants were made to bear risks they had not accepted. The completed event is ready for promotion.

### G-72 working ruling — captivity regularised as fosterage

The first Hostage Children cohort was not created by a balanced ceremonial exchange. The proto-Atherian fort and the Folk great clan already held children and adolescents taken during reciprocal raids, household seizures and retaliatory cattle operations. With both communities facing damaged food access and another destructive season, their leaders converted those existing captives into recognised truce sureties. The agreement restrained immediate killing and sale, assigned each child to a named household and allowed limited messages or visits, but it did not retroactively make the original seizures consensual.

Later renewals added some deliberately exchanged elite children and adolescents, producing the material from which southern genealogies constructed a dignified story of willing students and equal heirs. The actual cohort remained socially uneven. High-status captives could become honoured fosterlings or prospective marriage partners; poorer children and dependants were more likely to labour as servants and to suffer punishment for events beyond their control. Folk accounts which remember abduction and fort accounts which remember negotiated fostering therefore preserve different phases and classes within the same bounded institution.

The youth at the centre of the breach was born to a fort household and captured while still young enough to be raised within the Folk clan. He completed the clan's adulthood rites and married a woman of the household or close kin-group which had fostered him. Those acts made him a Folk man with adult obligations in the clan's law; they did not erase his birth relations or knowledge of the fort. When the truce allowed him to return as a liaison, interpreter or acknowledged former captive, fort authorities continued to treat him as a subject by birth.

The fort genuinely lacked enough winter meat after repeated raids and failed access to its usual supplies. Its commander openly planned to break the truce and seize sufficient cattle from the clan's winter herd to carry the garrison and attached households through the season. This was not a private enrichment scheme or a mistranslated treaty clause. The commander understood that he lacked lawful authority under the truce and treated the fort's necessity as a sufficient reason to transfer hunger to its ally. When the youth learned of the plan, he warned his wife so the clan could move or defend the herd. The fort then denied that Folk rites could release him from birth allegiance while citing his initiation and marriage as proof that his warning was knowing treason. His execution arose from this deliberately selective account of his double standing, not from uncertainty about whether he had passed the warning.

The commander convened a short military hearing during the night. Only fort officers were accepted as judges and witnesses. The youth's wife and Folk foster-kin were refused standing on the ground that they belonged to the party he had aided, even though the prosecution relied upon his obligations to them as evidence of treason. He was executed at dawn before the clan could answer or demand an exchange. The later requirement that both kin-groups witness the punishment or movement of a hostage, ward or diplomatic child directly answers this one-sided use of jurisdiction.

The first Folk retaliation was not authorised by the full clan council. Younger warriors struck before the elders had decided how to answer the execution. They attacked fort-associated settlers in exposed fields and killed or attempted to kill captives and southern dependants held within the clan's own hall, treating shared origin as collective guilt. Once those deaths had occurred, the elders refused to surrender their fighters or accept a judgment controlled by the fort. By protecting the raiders and adopting their violence as the clan's answer, the leadership acquired responsibility for the reprisal even if it had not ordered the first blow. The escalation therefore mirrors the fort council's protection of its commander without making the two original acts identical.

Surviving hostages and former fosterlings on both sides coordinated the escape of those now threatened with collective punishment. They used shared childhood signals, household knowledge and weak approaches learned during captivity and fostering. One group opened the clan's oak palisade from within; another opened the fort's glass-bright door. Rather than deliver either settlement to an attacking force, they moved children, captives and other noncombatants toward neutral ground beside the river. Their intervention prevented annihilation without preventing substantial killing, and it made the people whom both sides accused of divided loyalty indispensable witnesses to both crimes.

No durable settlement was concluded at the river. The rescue interrupted the immediate cycle of killing, but the truce itself collapsed and neither leadership accepted the other's jurisdiction or full account of the dead. Some former hostages returned to one of their two households; others married across the border, entered neighbouring communities or left the region. They founded families and carried testimony, not a coherent third nation or neutral river polity.

The paired-witness rule arose only generations later as mixed descendant households and successor border courts confronted repeated disputes over wards, hostages and diplomatic children. They used the remembered one-sided execution to require recognised witnesses from both kin-groups before such a person could be punished or forcibly moved. Oak and green-glass beads became a later legal memorial of the clan palisade and fort door opened during the rescue. They are not surviving tokens from an original successful peace rite. The bounded event is ready for promotion.

### G-73 working ruling — one rower became the harbour king

The Oar-King was one historical person rather than a composite invented from unrelated careers. He was born among a Folk coastal population, captured during a raid or local maritime war and sold into a proto-Atherian fleet. He began his known career as an enslaved rower aboard a payless warship or armed transport damaged by storm, battle or both in foreign water. The officers took the only seaworthy tender, loading the pay chest, charts and portable valuables while deliberately leaving chained rowers, wounded soldiers and lower crew aboard a vessel they expected to founder. He participated in or led the breaking of the remaining rowing restraints, rationed spoiled stores and brought the survivors to safety.

The survivors initially formed a temporary bench council representing rowers, free sailors and wounded soldiers rather than acclaiming a new absolute captain. They selected the future Oar-King as speaker and ration-master because he distributed the remaining food across former rank, understood Folk coastal signs and persuaded the crew to follow birds and currents which the former navigators had dismissed. His office remained answerable to the council during the first escape. Only later voyages, victories and the arrival of followers who knew him solely as commander converted emergency trust into personal captaincy. Need made him a coordinator before success taught him to treat necessity as a continuing source of authority.

The inhabited harbour later associated with his crown did not simply refuse water to harmless castaways. Its rulers offered winter admission only if the armed fleet surrendered its ships and weapons and dispersed its members among established households as bonded labour. The harbour authorities understood those demands as necessary protection against an increasingly large mobile war-band. Former rowers and captives reasonably recognised that the terms could dissolve their hard-won freedom and return them to coercive service. The Oar-King was justified in refusing that admission. Refusal did not give him lawful ownership of the harbour or its people, and the danger embodied in the proposed terms cannot excuse the conquest, dispossession and captive sale which followed.

The fleet appeared to withdraw, then returned before dawn through a difficult coastal channel whose birds, shoals and currents the Oar-King understood through his Folk maritime upbringing. No supernatural navigation or internal liberator is required. The assault forced the harbour and killed defenders as well as inhabitants caught in the fighting. Because the original officers had escaped with the pay chest and the enlarged fleet remained unpaid, the Oar-King compensated his followers with captured houses, stored goods and shares in the sale of prisoners. Older harbour families were expelled, subordinated or retained under new names and obligations; specialists, especially pilots and shipworkers, were compelled to serve the new regime. The distribution made permanent settlement materially attractive and transformed an emergency following into a conquering citizen body.

The king's oldest political companion was another formerly chained rower who had helped organise the first emergency bench council. He supported the future king through the escape and early voyages but continued to understand their leader as the holder of a temporary, answerable mandate. After the harbour conquest he demanded that emergency command end, that the fleet leave after winter as originally argued and that captive people and occupied homes be restored or compensated where possible. His challenge threatened not only the Oar-King's pride but the houses, sale shares and political standing already distributed among the conquerors. Because he could personally testify to the council's founding terms, he remained more dangerous than a later rival who merely wanted the crown.

The king answered through a real ceremonial boat race but did not openly ram his companion. Agents weakened the rival vessel's steering gear before launch. Once the boat reached the outer course it could not answer the current and struck the reef beyond easy rescue. Prepared witnesses and the king's public grief established the official account as an accident. He then carried the dead companion's recovered oar before his crown and claimed that his oldest friend had blessed the permanent settlement. The murder thus appropriated both the man's death and his authority: the object displayed as proof of continuity belonged to the person who had most clearly denied that continuity.

The concealment did not remain only a dockside suspicion. A genuine prophet of the Lord later entered the Oar-King's court under a bounded commission concerning this ruler and crime. He told of a rich captain who possessed many vessels but took a freed rower's only small boat to impress or provision a foreign guest, destroyed its owner and carried away the surviving oar as proof of friendship. The Oar-King condemned the captain and pronounced restitution and judgment upon him. The prophet answered that the king himself was that captain, then named the sabotaged race, the stolen authority represented by the dead man's oar and the captive households used to finance the new crown. The parable did not reduce the conquest generation's other victims to metaphors for the companion; it used the murder the king had hidden to expose the same possessive logic across his rule. The prophet inherited none of the Great Prophet's universal authority, founded no prophetic dynasty and acquired no general right to govern the harbour. His accurate exposure of the concealed murder and the moral truth of the accusation, rather than a spectacular public miracle, authenticated the particular commission.

The king confessed publicly. He returned the companion's oar to the dead man's household, freed captives who had not yet been sold or transferred beyond his power and prohibited future military payment through the sale of human beings. These acts were real repentance and materially changed some lives. He nevertheless refused to abdicate or attempt a wholesale restoration of conquered property, arguing that removing the new households would repeat dispossession and destabilise the port. That practical difficulty did not erase his interest in keeping the settlement or make existing victims whole. The prophet warned that, because the crown and households remained founded through violence, violence would remain within the king's own house. Repentance therefore prevents neither political consequence nor later judgment.

The warning was fulfilled politically after the Oar-King's death. Rival heirs inherited competing parts of the fleet, conquest allotment houses, dock following and relationships with older harbour families. Their succession war damaged the port and prevented any claimant from converting the founder's emergency authority into an uncontested hereditary crown. The settlement which ended the fighting abolished or emptied the kingship and restored a civic magistracy partly modelled upon the remembered bench council, though voting rights and property remained unequal and many founding dispossessions were never reversed.

A ceremonial oar subsequently preceded the harbour magistrate. Because the king had returned the companion's actual oar to the dead man's household, the civic object is a later substitute or claimed relic whose authenticity is not required. Dockworkers and descendants of captive or displaced harbour families followed with a lament naming people sold or lost during the conquest generation. The procession does not turn the Oar-King into either an immaculate liberator or a wholly invented tyrant: it makes the polity carry the crew he freed, the harbour he conquered, the friend he murdered, the repentance he attempted and the injuries he left unrepaired. G-73 is ready for promotion.

Refugees, escaped captives, criminals, pilots, dispossessed fighters and defeated soldiers joined his following across subsequent voyages. They never formed one permanent signature company with a closed heroic roster. Later songs attached victories, escapes and navigational feats belonging to other captains of the recovery age to the Oar-King, enlarging his fleet and range, but the rise from enslaved rower through captain and judge to the conqueror-founder of one older inhabited harbour belongs to the same man. The polity he established became Atherian-speaking and entered the heroic-maritime Atherian world, while Folk and Ättar-related traditions continued to claim or condemn the founder through his birth. His later exercise of power must therefore be judged as the transformation of a former captive into a ruler who protected his own following while permitting or ordering captivity and sale for others, not as the accidental merging of a virtuous slave and an unrelated tyrant.

## Pre-350 BR Canon Promotion Record — 15 September 2026

The author approved the reviewed pre-350 BR roots for canon promotion, ending the interactive development pass at the close of the Age of Stars. G-02 through G-08, G-56 through G-60 and G-71 through G-73 now have explicit public historical bases in their Atlas articles. Their received tales remain visible but no longer control a contradictory historical claim. G-01 remains deliberately uncertain and was not promoted beyond its existing authority.

The promotion also updates the Age of Chains, Exodus, Scattering, Hearths and First Kings, City-States and Stars summaries; Southern Settlement and Maritime Atheria; the early river-league overview; relevant culture-history navigation; the myths MOC; and the Atlas coverage ledger. G-60 is displayed as **The Son-in-Law and the Stolen Gods** while retaining its stable filename and former-title alias. No G-10 or later-period root was developed or promoted in this pass.

The following limits remain controlling:

- heroic speeches, exact names and later ceremonial objects are not authenticated merely because the underlying events are fixed;
- one household, fort, harbour, company or road gang does not become the origin of an entire people;
- successful conquest, settlement, ritual or emergency policy does not establish divine approval;
- G-03's abandoned old-cult faction is presumed to have perished in the Glass, but no evidence proves total extinction or a surviving descendant line;
- the Old Mother remains an ancient human witch transformed by occult apotheosis, not a Menhir goddess, and the gods stolen in G-60 are genuine cult images without established person-like indwelling or speech;
- the Oar-King's confession and partial restitution do not erase murder, captivity, dispossession or the gains he retained.
