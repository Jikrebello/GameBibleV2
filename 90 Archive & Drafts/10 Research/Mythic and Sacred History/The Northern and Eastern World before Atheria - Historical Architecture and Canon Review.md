# The Northern and Eastern World before Atheria — Historical Architecture and Canon Review

## Scope

This review proposes a connected history for the lands north and east of early Atheria, from the Great Unbinding to Aurelius Thalion’s settlement around 350 BR. It brings the proto-Jirahar, proto-Ahdari, Tarkhan, Qahuri basin societies, Arqeshite river cities and Gateway country to the same level of historical preparation already completed for Caleran and maritime Atheria.

The proposal draws upon three separate comparative studies:

- [[Mesopotamia and the Levant - Cities, Kingdoms, Collapse and Imperial Return]]
- [[Arabia Iran and the Steppe - Wells, Caravan States and Open-Country Powers]]
- [[Pagan Arabia and the Open-Country Nomads - Camps, Raids, Cult and Memory]]
- [[Egypt Phoenicia and Carthage - River Kingdom, Maritime Republic and Royal Successor]]

The architecture is exploratory. It introduces a regional sequence, working political groupings and historical relationships for review. It changes no canonical article, date or identity in this delivery.

## Existing canon that governs the proposal

### Human formation

The Prophet dies around c. 2165 BR after approximately a century in the Glass. No human office inherits his universal commission. Southern war-settlement leagues separate first. The Near-Glass camp continuum divides gradually between c. 1950 and 1750 BR into proto-Jirahar and proto-Ahdari formations.

Tarkhan peoples remain Orphaned descendants of communities which survived outside the Ark. Contact with the Near-Glass camps involves trade, marriage, raid, fosterage and conflict. It does not turn the Tarkhan into Ark descendants.

### Three river worlds

The proposal distinguishes three river histories.

1. **Ishkatar and the Serpent’s Spine** belong to the fallen Giant world. Ishkatar’s central river was destroyed during the Great Unbinding. Sarrakai canals, Naga royal claims and Titan-derived dynasties remain part of that ancient history.
2. **The Qahuri Sacred Basin** survives within the later known world. Its human communities develop new river kingdoms and institutions after the Unbinding. Al-Qahur’s later Covenant sacred status does not create the basin or its agriculture.
3. **The eastern riverlands of Arqesh** lie in country later absorbed by Atheria and inherited in part by eastern Eclessia. Their canal cities and mortal empire possess a separate development.

Ishkatari refugees within the One Glass People may transmit skills, images and memories into later Ark-derived societies. Such inheritance does not make a Qahuri or Arqeshite kingdom the lawful continuation of Ishkatar.

### Established later events

- Qiryath develops between c. 1400 and 800 BR.
- Arqesh destroys the First Qiryath in 742 BR and falls around 690 BR.
- The Second Qiryath is consecrated around 660 BR.
- The Gateway Republic forms from mixed coastal populations, develops a dominant capital between c. 1200 and 850 BR and fights Caleran from approximately 650 to 520 BR.
- The Gateway capital is destroyed around 520 BR. Lesser towns survive.
- The House of Nerethan arises among those towns and rules until Thalion’s annexation in 350 BR.
- The First Catch and the mounted Grass-Tarkhan revolution occur after 905 AR. Early Tarkhan history cannot anticipate them.

This sequence makes the Bronze collapse a major regional transformation. It does not become a second Great Unbinding, a universal dark age or the origin of every later people.

## The historical world at the Prophet’s death

The Near-Glass camps face a succession crisis after c. 2165 BR. Judges can settle disputes, provisioners can distribute stores and commanders can protect a road. None can speak with the Prophet’s commission. The southern departures weaken the remaining camps by taking craftsmen, animals and ambitious military households. They also reduce pressure on some stores and leave abandoned equipment for communities able to recover it.

Outside the refuge, the northern world has already endured a century of change. Western dry-country settlements repair wells abandoned by Giant clients. Open-country Tarkhan families rebuild herds and routes around seasonal lakes. Qahuri cultivators occupy surviving channels whose headworks no longer answer to a central crown. Eastern canal towns contain freed labourers, former overseers, burial households and armed retainers competing over grain and water.

These societies know the Great Prophet in different ways. Some meet refugees or hear the liberation warning. Others preserve only reports of a human leader whose God broke Giant claims. Several rulers appropriate the story. One calls his seizure of a royal granary an imitation of the Exodus; another condemns escaping labourers as thieves pretending to be the Prophet’s people.

## Proto-Jirahar development

### Road courts before tower cities

The proto-Jirahar emerge among camps which keep judgement, burial and teaching portable. Their earliest courts meet at wells, route shelters and grave grounds. A judge travels with copies of decisions and the names of families entitled to protection. A household separated by drought can therefore appeal to people who do not control the surrounding land.

This institution gains importance because local rulers repeatedly fail. A defended settlement may protect its own cistern and exclude travellers. A moving court has little coercive force, yet its recognition across several roads allows a wronged family to seek compensation elsewhere. Merchant households and carriers support the system because predictable judgement makes movement possible.

The first towers arise as storehouses, refuges and visible meeting points. Their cisterns, workshops and burial galleries matter more than height. Several towers become schools. Others become fortresses ruled by families who use sacred custody to justify tolls and forced service. Early Jirahar history should include both forms.

### Heroic age of judges and keepers

The Age of Reed and Bronze gives the Jirahar local judges, caravan captains, singers, midwives and defenders whose lives later become one heroic sequence. They save particular communities. Some also commit serious wrongs: a deliverer executes surrendered raiders, a keeper abandons a distant shelter to preserve the central tower, and a judge’s disastrous vow costs a member of his own household.

Their supernatural encounters remain bounded. A Menhir court may honour one route price and ignore the justice of the people paying it. An Idol Mask may appear as the memory of a beloved judge and demand the authority once given voluntarily. No pre-Redeemer saint manifests among them.

### Qiryath and centralisation

Between c. 1400 and 800 BR, one tower settlement becomes Qiryath, the principal sanctuary and place of learning. It gains influence through teaching, sacrifice, stored texts, arbitration and its position among routes. Other towers retain authority. Qiryath’s centrality creates arguments over appointment, taxation and the relative standing of road households.

The city’s rise should involve several generations:

- a keeper house secures the sanctuary hill;
- displaced scholars bring Bronze-age texts after a palace fall;
- builders expand cisterns and burial galleries;
- a ruler or council attempts to turn sacred custody into territorial kingship;
- other towers refuse complete submission and preserve separate courts.

This history prepares Qiryath’s refusal of the Arqeshite royal image. The city already knows that possession of a sanctuary can become a claim to rule.

### Arqeshite conquest and exile

Arqesh needs Qiryath’s road and skilled households. Its demand for priestly hostages prevents the sanctuary from organising resistance; its demand for the royal image makes public submission visible. The siege of 742 BR ends with the upper tower broken, inhabitants killed and leading households deported.

Exile changes both sides. Jirahar engineers, musicians, scribes and metalworkers serve in imperial cities. Some acquire property and influence. Families argue over food, worship, marriage and service under foreign authority. Arqeshite subjects encounter Jirahar law in workshops and neighbourhoods rather than only through elite debate.

When Arqesh falls, the return decree serves a successor’s political interests. The rebuilding of Qiryath around 660 BR neither gathers every exile nor restores the previous kingdom. Returners dispute property with people who remained around the ruin. Established diaspora households choose not to abandon children, spouses and livelihoods born in exile.

By 350 BR, the Jirahar already possess the four conditions which later define their history: a sacred city, independent towers, a substantial diaspora and legal forms capable of surviving without sovereignty.

## Proto-Ahdari development

### Defended wells and territorial coalitions

The proto-Ahdari branch develops where a court that can travel is insufficient. A settlement must protect a particular well, orchard or caravan stage. Households bind themselves through witnessed contributions, guest protection, blood-price and obligations to rescue people taken on the road.

Coalitions remain local. One guards three wells and a market season; another joins a cultivated basin to upland pasture. Their leaders include water keepers, war captains, merchants, shrine households and elders able to secure marriages. Authority grows from maintaining a place and enforcing agreements there.

Older Orphaned desert people participate as equals, dependants, rivals and captives according to circumstance. Some provide the deepest knowledge of water and weather. A Near-Glass pedigree can carry prestige without granting automatic command.

### Several Ahdari historical branches

The northern fission should lead into several later formations rather than a single proto-state.

| Branch | Early historical ground | Later inheritance |
|---|---|---|
| **Iqashari town belt** | market wells, fortified road towns and caravan taxation | urban administration and later Qahirid political heartland |
| **Qahuri basin communities** | irrigation villages, river cults, regional crowns and grain storage | the Sacred Basin and later Al-Qahur |
| **Qazmari strait communities** | ports, fortified water and western crossings | later independent Sealed-Blood country after Mujalib |
| **Gateway coast** | mixed proto-Ahdari, shore and maritime households | the Gateway Republic, spared towns, House of Nerethan and later Zanthir |
| **Road and camp populations** | seasonal protection, camel breeding and service between states | later Ahdari clan and military-household formations |

The name **Ahl-e-Ahd** remains inappropriate for these early populations. It belongs to the later religious-political civilisation formed after Mujalib’s revelation.

### The Bronze Roads and their breaking

Proto-Ahdari merchants and carriers join the Bronze exchange world because their wells connect river, coast and open country. Early transport relies heavily upon donkeys, ox carts and human labour. Camels grow in importance through selective breeding and longer dry routes.

The collapse around c. 1450–1325 BR strikes the road coalitions unevenly. Palace officials seize animals and stores. Carriers refuse contracts whose payment has lost value. Raiders attack weakened convoys. Drought forces some families to abandon wells, while towns on shorter routes prosper.

One political consequence should be the end of several hereditary protector houses. Communities replace them with assemblies or rival patrons because the houses failed to protect guests while preserving their own stores. Elsewhere, a captain saves a route and converts temporary command into a dynasty.

By c. 1050 BR, the dry belt contains old towns, mobile coalitions and cultivated basins with distinct identities. This is the world from which later Covenant civilisation will draw, not a cultural preface awaiting revelation.

### Camps, raids and cult

The mobile road populations possess a history of their own. Camps contain herding households, dependants, slaves, poets, craftsmen and people who maintain relationships with oasis kin. Seasonal movement follows pasture and water. A household may cultivate dates at one stage, carry goods during another season and join a raid after drought.

Raiding is organised through leaders, shares and sworn participation. It kills herders, removes the animals on which families depend and carries women, children and defeated men into ransom or slavery. Captive concubinage and forced marriage are real forms of sexual coercion. Feud and blood-price can protect vulnerable kin or spread one killing among relatives who did not commit it. Poetry makes courage, generosity, betrayal and shame public enough to alter alliances.

Sacred seasons and protected markets create temporary peace across these divisions. Their violation becomes especially destructive because travellers have laid aside weapons and gathered dependants in one place. Local worship includes animal sacrifice, high-place prayer, stones and portable standards, but no single desert pantheon. Some Near-Glass descendants retain fragmentary Lordward practice; other communities address court persons, Menhir correspondences, Patron anchors, empty images or Idol Masks. Each supernatural relationship requires its own later ruling.

Human sacrifice may belong to a particular oasis cult or royal vow. It does not define all open-country religion. Later Covenant chroniclers can preserve a real atrocity while exaggerating it into the universal custom of their pagan ancestors.

## Proto-Tarkhan and Orphaned open-country history

### Survival outside the Glass

Tarkhan ancestors survive the Giant collapse in the northern dry belt. Some descend from herding dependants and transport households. Others come from hunters, fugitive labourers, small client communities and armed bands guarding broken roads. They repair wells and recover animals without participation in the Glass refuge.

The western circuits become associated with wells, camels and small stock. Northeastern communities follow sheep, cattle and wild herds through open grass, moving by foot, sled and cart. Families at the boundary use both patterns.

### Bronze-age prestige houses

The Age of Reed and Bronze allows a limited number of horse-drawn chariot houses. Horses remain scarce, and the vehicles require wheelwrights, metal fittings, fodder and suitable ground. The chariot chief’s political power depends upon settled craftspeople and distant trade.

These houses provide a heroic layer without anticipating the later mounted hordes. Their champions can break infantry at a favourable site and fail completely in stones, dunes or winter mud. A household may lose its status when its horses die or its enslaved wheelwrights escape.

The Breaking of the Bronze Roads destroys many such concentrations. Later Tarkhan remember the age in songs about copper axles, proud teams and rulers who had to be lifted into their vehicles. Grass-Tarkhan horsemen after 905 AR regard some of the stories as comic accounts of ancestors who had not yet learned to ride.

### Coalitions and river empires

Tarkhan communities trade animals, wool, salt and transport for grain, metal and worked goods. They serve eastern kingdoms as scouts and auxiliaries, raid canal settlements and shelter fugitives. A river king may recognise one family’s pasture to secure a border, while another campaign deports the same family’s relatives.

Arqeshite expansion divides the open country. Some houses accept gifts and guide imperial armies. Others join subject revolts or move beyond dependable roads. The coalition which helps destroy Arqesh includes Tarkhan fighters, but no Tarkhan nation inherits the empire.

By 350 BR, Tarkhan society remains politically plural. Foreigners know several well confederacies and famous open-country houses. None resembles Qarayel’s later horde, and horses have not reorganised the whole civilisation.

## The Qahuri river crowns

### Formation

The Sacred Basin is cultivated before the proto-Ahdari arrive. Orphaned villages, former Giant dependants and surviving canal households maintain separate stretches of water. Near-Glass settlers enter through work, marriage, armed protection and seizure. Their descendants become part of Qahuri formation without replacing the older population.

The working term **Twin-Reed Crowns** describes a recurring political idea rather than two permanent states. Upper-channel and lower-basin rulers claim complementary titles. A strong monarch sometimes holds both; during weaker generations several houses divide them.

### Government and ordinary life

River kings depend upon canal councils, village headmen, temple estates, fishing communities, boat households, soldiers and scribes. Grain supports garrisons and trade with the desert. Orchards and gardens cluster where secondary channels can be maintained. Festivals mark the opening of water, planting, harvest and the remembrance of destructive floods.

Royal workshops support stonework, metal craft, linen and boats. Households celebrate river races and night markets during safe water. Corvée and slavery supply part of the work. Neglected gates can drown fields or deny a district’s crop, making control of water an instrument of punishment.

### Dynastic rhythm

The proposed basin history contains four broad phases:

1. **c. 1750–1450 BR:** competing reed crowns and one period of reunification establish a shared calendar and royal burial tradition.
2. **c. 1450–1200 BR:** poor floods, palace rivalry and road collapse divide the basin. Soldiers and provincial houses acquire power.
3. **c. 1200–850 BR:** a mixed or foreign military dynasty reunifies much of the basin, adopts local royal rites and employs established canal officials.
4. **c. 850–350 BR:** regional crowns, priestly estates and foreign interventions repeatedly divide authority. No lasting unity prevents the Gateway Wars or Arqeshite expansion from affecting the basin’s trade.

The modern city and sacred government of Al-Qahur arise much later under the Covenant. They inherit canals, pilgrimage places and political memories from this ancient world.

## The eastern cities and Arqesh

### Before empire

The eastern riverlands should contain several canal cities by the Age of Reed and Bronze. Their economies combine grain, livestock, pottery, textiles, metal and overland exchange. Kings sponsor temples and astronomical observation. Merchant households establish resident quarters in foreign towns.

The Bronze Roads collapse reduces long-distance exchange and breaks one or more palace houses. A city at a strong crossing survives by shortening its trade and accepting refugees. This city becomes Arqesh. Its early rulers present themselves as preservers of water and ancient learning.

### Transformation into empire

Between approximately c. 950 and 800 BR, Arqeshite kings reorganise military service, canal taxation and provincial government. Victories bring skilled deportees and tribute. The army absorbs defeated soldiers. Royal art displays siege, punishment and the transfer of peoples as proof that resistance is futile.

Arqeshite officials govern through both terror and utility. They repair major canals, protect selected trade routes and employ scholars from several subject peoples. This combination makes imperial rule durable and morally compromised. A family may owe its livelihood to an imperial workshop while mourning relatives killed when its home city resisted.

### Fall and successor settlement

The empire’s late kings compete over succession and the control of royal water. Failed flood regimes weaken provinces already carrying military demands. Revolts spread. A coalition of river cities, upland rulers, Tarkhan houses and expanding Atherian leagues destroys the principal capitals around 690 BR.

No participant can dominate the whole inheritance. A successor ruler issues returns and restores sanctuaries to deprive old governors of support. Arqeshite officials and military settlers remain in many districts. Some serve the new regimes. Others create local dynasties.

This settlement explains why eastern Eclessia later contains Arqeshite canal beds, names, law and mixed ancestry beneath Atherian institutions.

## The Gateway coast and the queen’s inheritance

The Gateway Republic retains its existing canonical history. Its earliest communities form between about 1700 and 1200 BR, placing them inside the Age of Reed and Bronze and the Breaking of the Bronze Roads. The collapse should help explain their maritime independence: smaller ports and mixed households preserve shipping after older palace partners fail.

The capital’s later dominance, c. 1200–850 BR, rests upon cultivated dependants, storage and control of naval service. Its child-sacrificing establishment becomes powerful over generations. The practice kills living children and punishes families and towns which refuse collection.

Caleran’s final war combines sincere opposition to sacrifice with strategic rivalry and appetite for conquest. The destruction of the capital around 520 BR ends the principal institution and kills or enslaves many inhabitants. Heavy salting prevents rebuilding at the old site. Lesser towns retain food production, labour and community memory.

The House of Nerethan emerges from this inhabited country. Existing canon fixes Nerethan of Delkos and Hadrima of Esharat as the founding couple. A later integration may connect Hadrima’s maternal family to a junior Qahuri river-crown house. Such a connection would be one strand of ancestry and ritual knowledge. The crown’s authority would still depend upon the spared towns, Atherian recognition and working supply.

Samyra could consequently claim dominion through three histories:

- descent from the house which rebuilt Sarqen;
- inheritance of the former Gateway country through its surviving towns;
- a contested river-crown relationship acquired through Hadrima’s maternal line.

Her claims would exceed the territory she securely ruled, as royal claims often do. Their religious imagery cannot make her a descendant of an Idol or Menhir.

## The shared heroic age

The northern heroic corpus should contain several cycles with different cultural owners.

### River king and wild companion

An eastern city remembers a violent young king befriended by a herdsman from outside its walls. Their victories kill a dangerous Giant-descended ruler and violate a court boundary. The companion dies. The king travels towards an Egg White route after being promised escape from death and returns empty-handed. He spends his later reign repairing the city wall and freeing some of the labourers he had earlier enslaved. The dead remain dead.

This uses the Gilgamesh grammar and existing continuity rules. The ruler’s journey does not discover a true road to immortality.

### The foundling of the silt basket

Competing Arqeshite traditions give an early ruler a royal mother, an enslaved mother and no known mother. All agree that a canal gardener or water worker raised him and that he first entered the palace through service. His later conquests turn the uncertainty of birth into a providential legend. The account can draw upon Sargon and other foundling traditions while remaining distinct from the Great Prophet and Caleran’s wolf-fostered siblings.

### The two claimants of the river crown

Qahuri royal tradition remembers two relatives contesting a murdered king’s inheritance through combat, sexual humiliation, deception and adjudication by several shrines. Human rulers later perform parts of the story during accession. Menhir and Idol imagery accumulates around the roles. The authorial event should remain a human dynastic struggle with genuine supernatural interventions from separately classified powers.

### The champion outside the crown

An open-country champion repeatedly rescues a river or basin king whose ambition creates avoidable wars. Their alliance ends when the ruler sends the champion’s child into an impossible campaign. Tarkhan, Arqeshite and Qahuri singers assign the story to different frontiers. This uses Iranian heroic tension between royal and warrior traditions without importing a national sacred history intact.

### The last bronze convoy

A mixed company of carriers, sailors, enslaved porters and guards tries to bring tin, grain and seed through the collapsing road system. Palace commanders seize the metal and abandon the food. The survivors distribute seed to smaller towns and lose the prestige cargo. Later royal song remembers a failed treasure convoy; household festivals remember the meal planted from its sacks.

These are historical-development leads, not Garden promotions. Full names, sites and final truth remain for the next regional writing stage.

## Supernatural history

The northern world should contain active greater powers without converting politics into their puppet theatre.

### Menhir

- River and flood manifestations enforce particular boundaries and prices.
- Desert courts control thresholds, seasonal routes, shelter or exposure under their own laws.
- Storm and sea courts alter voyages and coastal wars.
- A ruler’s successful bargain proves that the bargain worked. It does not grant moral title to subjects or land.

### Idols

- Greed feeds through royal ownership of water, crops, tombs and debt.
- Pride feeds through living-divinity claims, imperial images and the insistence that one city possesses universal kingship.
- Wrath intensifies heroic vengeance and imperial terror.
- The Gateway provider feeds through child sacrifice and promises of civic preservation.
- A Mask may survive the fall of its cult architecture or compete with another cultural expression of the same Idol. No succession is proposed here.

### Patrons and Giant remnants

- Escaped Giant courts and Egg White Patrons may trade technical knowledge, false ancestry or limited route access.
- Human kingdoms can mistake a copied ancestor for a surviving dynast.
- Genuine High Giants may survive individually into the earliest heroic period through established mechanisms. Mortal heroes can kill their bodies.
- Titan-Firstborn remain subject to the existing distributed-life rule and cannot be permanently slain by ordinary heroic violence.

### Heaven and saints

No pre-Redeemer saint manifests. Angelic or Phoenix action remains limited to an established commission. The regional Bronze collapse is chiefly the result of climate, political choices, warfare and failed exchange. It is not a second universal judgement announced by a new prophet.

## Cultural memory by 350 BR

### Jirahar memory

Jirahar teachers arrange road judges, early towers, Qiryath, exile and return into a sacred historical sequence. Tower versions disagree over which communities preserved the oldest practice. The sequence’s moral centre concerns fidelity without sovereignty and the danger of turning custody into possession.

### Ahdari and Qahuri memory

Well houses remember founders who defended water and guests. Basin courts remember periods of unity under good floods. Later Covenant historians reinterpret both through the language of Mujalib’s Covenant, sometimes making ancient agreements more theologically complete than they were.

### Tarkhan memory

Songs preserve chariot lords, broken herds, fostered enemies and coalitions around shared danger. Genealogies absorb unrelated champions. Later horse cultures convert carts into steeds and slow seasonal movements into sweeping conquests.

The pre-350 BR history in this dossier now joins a separate study of the post-Fall transformation. [[Turkic and Mongol Horse Empires - Confederation, Conquest and Successor Worlds]] supplies the comparative record, while [[The Tarkhan Horse Centuries - World Egg Comparison and Canon Review]] develops the First Catch, remount generations, banner coalitions, Black Wind and Partition. The later study preserves these chariot and open-country memories as Tarkhan antiquity; it does not move mounted Horde society before 905 AR.

### Arqeshite and eastern memory

Successor courts keep Arqeshite scholarly and legal forms while condemning particular kings. Subject peoples remember deportation and siege. Atherian histories emphasise the leagues’ role in the empire’s fall and minimise the river and Tarkhan coalitions without which their campaigns would have failed.

### Gateway memory

The spared towns distinguish their country from the destroyed capital. Some families honour the Atherians who broke child collection. Others remember the sack, enslavement and salted ground. The Nerethan court incorporates both histories into a claim of restoration.

## Collision and identity rulings

1. The Breaking of the Bronze Roads is centuries later than the Great Unbinding and does not repeat its heavenly severance, Giant civil wars or Glass refuge.
2. The Qahuri basin is neither Ishkatar’s Serpent’s Spine nor the eastern Arqeshite riverlands.
3. The Twin-Reed Crowns are human states. Their kings possess complete human souls unless a separately identified individual belongs to another established continuity.
4. Early chariot prestige does not create mounted Grass-Tarkhan culture before the First Catch.
5. Proto-Ahdari coastal ancestry does not make the Gateway Republic a Covenant state.
6. Jirahar tower development does not give Qiryath sovereignty over every road court.
7. Arqeshite restoration decrees may benefit Jirahar communities without proving the successor ruler righteous.
8. Hadrima’s possible river-crown connection would add one maternal relationship. It cannot replace the spared towns as the foundation of Nerethan authority.
9. The Gateway child sacrifice remains a practice of particular institutions and supporters. It never establishes hereditary guilt for survivors or descendants.
10. Similar royal images do not merge Menhir, Idol Masks, Patrons, Giant remnants or the Boatman.

## Questions for the next review

The research supports most of the architecture without a further source pass. Four choices would materially shape canon integration:

1. Should **the Twin-Reed Crowns** become the accepted ancient name, or remain a historian’s label over several Qahuri dynasties?
2. Should Hadrima’s maternal family possess a genuine junior river-crown descent, a politically useful adoption, or a wholly fabricated later genealogy?
3. Should the foundling ruler belong to early Arqesh, a predecessor canal city or a rival city later absorbed into Arqeshite royal history?
4. How much direct supernatural intervention should survive as authorial fact within the shared heroic cycles: one well-attested intervention per cycle, or several competing powers whose acts can be separated by evidence?

These choices affect named dynasties and mythic biographies. The regional chronology, social development and separation of the three river worlds can be approved independently.

## Recommended direction

Adopt the complete regional periodisation and the separation of the three river worlds. Develop the Twin-Reed Crowns as a historian’s collective label, allowing several dynasties and periods of division. Give Hadrima a genuine but junior maternal relationship to one late basin house; the connection supplies ritual knowledge and prestige without territorial ownership. Place the foundling ruler in a predecessor city whose history Arqesh later appropriates. Limit each major heroic cycle to one or two authorially fixed supernatural interventions, leaving later divine family trees as cultural interpretation.

The next delivery should be a canon-promotion plan followed by chronological integration. It should begin with the shared historical spine and regional articles, then expand the Jirahar, Ahdari-Qahuri, Tarkhan and Arqeshite strands in parallel. The Gateway history requires contextual links and the approved Hadrima ruling rather than another rewrite.
