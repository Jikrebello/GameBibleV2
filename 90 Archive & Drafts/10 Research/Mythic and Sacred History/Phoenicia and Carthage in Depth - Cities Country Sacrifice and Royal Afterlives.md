# Phoenicia and Carthage in Depth — Cities, Country, Sacrifice and Royal Afterlives

## Purpose

The Gateway Republic needs a history of its own people, countryside and institutions. Its conflict with Caleran matters because two developed republics struggle over islands, clients, food and naval power. The child-sacrificing establishment gives the final war an additional moral cause, but it does not reduce the entire civilisation to one rite or make every Atherian act righteous.

The later queen's kingdom grows from the country which survives the destruction. The capital is sacked, burned and salted so heavily that no city can be rebuilt on its ground. Lesser towns, farms, fisheries, temples and refugee communities remain. Their descendants create a new political order over generations. Samyra can claim that inheritance without claiming that the old republic escaped destruction.

## Phoenician cities before Carthage

Phoenician-speaking communities developed from the coastal societies of the Late Bronze Age Levant. Tyre, Sidon, Byblos and Arwad shared language, craft and religious forms but remained competing cities. Their narrow territories encouraged shipping and exchange, although agriculture and inland relationships remained essential. Craftsmen worked timber, ivory, metal, glass and dyed textiles. Merchants and settlers travelled through a Mediterranean already inhabited by other peoples.

The collapse of many Late Bronze palatial states around 1200 BC did not erase the Phoenician cities. Their relative continuity helped them expand maritime networks. Trading stations and settlements in Cyprus, North Africa, Sicily, Sardinia and Iberia developed through different combinations of commerce, migration, intermarriage, coercion and local alliance. “Colonisation” therefore names several processes rather than one centrally directed national plan.

This is the proper ancestry for the Gateway coast. Early proto-Ahdari-speaking mariners should form several rival ports after the northern fission. They settle among older coastal communities, marry locally and maintain relationships with home harbours. No single mother city founds every settlement. A later capital wins primacy by organising convoy protection, cultivated dependants and common naval contributions.

## Carthage as city and country

Carthage's traditional foundation date was 814 BC, although the literary foundation cycle and archaeological history must be distinguished. The city eventually became the dominant western Phoenician power. It possessed excellent harbours, maritime connections and an agricultural hinterland. Its power rested upon North Africa as well as islands and overseas dependencies.

The countryside produced grain, olives, wine, fruit and livestock. Carthaginian agronomic knowledge was respected after the city's destruction; the Roman Senate ordered Mago's agricultural work translated. Rural wealth belonged unequally to citizens, elite estates, Libyan communities and subject towns. Taxes and military demands imposed on African subjects became a major source of grievance.

The Gateway Republic should likewise be a **country organised around a dominant city**, not a fleet floating above an empty shore. Lesser towns grow grain, press oil, cure fish, quarry stone and supply crews. Inland communities maintain their own languages, shrines and leading families. Some gain citizenship or commercial privilege; others pay heavier taxes and provide soldiers without equivalent political power.

## Republican government

Carthage was governed through annually elected suffetes or judges, aristocratic councils, magistrates, generals and a popular assembly. Aristotle and later writers admired parts of the constitution, although the surviving descriptions are incomplete and filtered through Greek and Roman categories. Civil and military authority were not identical. Generals could build powerful regional followings, while councils and judicial bodies sought to supervise them. Commanders might be punished severely after failure.

The Gateway version should possess:

- two annually chosen **Harbour Judges** who preside over civic business and sacred obligations;
- a **Council of Houses** drawn from leading landholding, shipping and temple families;
- a larger assembly of recognised citizens which elects commanders and decides contested measures;
- military captains holding broad authority overseas but vulnerable to recall, trial or execution;
- dependent towns with obligations but unequal access to the capital's decisions.

This distribution produces the Republic's strength and weakness. Defeat can be debated without ending the state. Successful commanders can also accumulate ships, soldiers and client towns beyond easy civic control. Political competition encourages expansion because victory brings office, spoils and prestige.

## Households, mixture and military service

Carthaginian armies combined citizen formations, Libyan and other African troops, allied contingents, Numidian cavalry, Iberians, Gauls and hired soldiers. Calling the entire army mercenary obscures the different relationships involved. Some fought for pay; others served through alliance, subjection or their own political aims.

Gateway forces should be described with the same precision. Citizens crew the core fleets and serve in moments of extreme danger. Subject towns provide infantry or ships by quota. Inland allies fight for negotiated rewards. Captive specialists and paid foreign companies also serve. A commander who fails to pay one category may still retain the loyalty of another.

Marriage connects leading Gateway houses to inland, island and distant coastal elites. Children of these households may possess several languages and inherit competing obligations. Their mixed ancestry later helps the Nerethan kingdom claim a country broader than the old capital's citizen body.

## Religion and the tophet problem

Phoenician and Punic religion included temples, household practice, vows, offerings and cults associated with deities such as Baal Hammon and Tanit. Greek and Roman authors accused Carthaginians of sacrificing children, especially during crises. Excavated tophets contain urns with cremated remains of infants, young children and animals. Scholars continue to dispute how many children were sacrificed, how many died naturally and how the sanctuaries functioned across time and place.

Two errors should be avoided. Treating every urn as certain proof of a killed child overstates the evidence. Dismissing all sacrifice as enemy propaganda ignores literary, epigraphic and archaeological arguments supporting some deliberate killing. The historical debate belongs in the research layer.

The World Egg ruling is firmer. The dominant Gateway sanctuary **does kill children**. Families vow children during war, famine, infertility or commercial danger. Some dedicate animal substitutes. Wealthy households sometimes attempt to substitute purchased or dependent children, leading reformers to accuse the elite of demanding sacrifices which they evade themselves. Temple and civic officials punish towns which refuse collection.

The rite sustains a dependent Gluttony Mask presented as the city's provider. Its answers are real within their narrow terms: a convoy arrives, a siege rain falls or stores resist rot. The Mask neither creates the sea nor guarantees civic survival. Each apparent success strengthens the institution that feeds it.

Victims must remain people within the history. They have parents, siblings, nurses and communities. Some parents consent in grief or zeal; some are coerced; some resist and are killed, imprisoned or driven into exile. Later Nerethan memorial practice preserves names from several rescued collections without pretending that remembrance restores the dead.

## Rivalry before Caleran

Carthage spent centuries competing with Greek cities, especially in Sicily. These wars involved local communities, tyrants, alliances and changing control of ports. Rome and Carthage also cooperated through early treaties before becoming principal rivals.

The Gateway Republic should have a similar prehistory with Atherian maritime societies. Gateway and Atherian merchants share harbours, fight pirates and recognise spheres of trade. Their settlers also compete for islands and grain ports. Several local cities invite one power against another. The eventual wars grow from a long relationship rather than ancient racial hatred.

## The three republican wars

### First war: ports and crossings

The first war begins when rival parties in an island port appeal separately to Caleran and the Gateway capital. Both republics claim to defend an ally. The real issue is control of a strait, winter harbour and grain route. Caleran builds or commandeers a fleet at great expense. Storms and poor seamanship kill more men than several battles. The settlement strips the Gateway Republic of islands and imposes a large payment.

The war changes Caleran. Naval obligations fall unevenly upon allied towns. Contractors, commanders and ship-owning families grow rich. Veterans expect land. The Republic learns that it can sustain war far beyond the Aurin basin.

### The payment revolt

Gateway officials cannot immediately pay troops and subject contingents returning from the war. Libyan grievances and Carthage's Mercenary War supply the grammar. Soldiers seize a camp; dependent towns join when tax collectors attempt to recover the indemnity from them. Prisoners are tortured and mutilated. The capital answers with starvation, crucifixion or its World Egg equivalent, and exemplary killing. Rebel factions also massacre captives and families associated with loyal towns.

The capital survives after a commander combines negotiation, betrayal and field victory. Caleran exploits the crisis by seizing another island despite the earlier settlement. This creates enduring hatred without making the Gateway government innocent of the revolt's causes.

### Second war: the alliance-breaking invasion

A leading Gateway military house builds a new base among western or outer-coast dependencies. Its most capable heir invades Caleran's alliance system, crossing terrain considered impassable with a mixed army and specialised beasts. Several subject peoples defect from Caleran; others remain because local rivals fear the invader more than the Republic.

The commander wins spectacular battles and kills tens of thousands. Captives are ransomed, recruited or enslaved. Yet battlefield victory does not dissolve Caleran's alliances. Walled communities, naval supply and stubborn allied governments deny the invader the political collapse sought. Caleran avoids another catastrophic battle, attacks the commander's supply base and carries war into the Gateway homeland.

Defeat leaves the Gateway Republic with restricted military power and a heavy settlement. The leading commander later attempts civic reform, attacks corruption and creates enemies among both wealthy houses and foreign rivals. Exile ends the reform.

### Final war: destruction of the capital

The defeated republic recovers economically while neighbouring client rulers take land under Caleran protection. Gateway resistance to one encroachment gives Caleran the formal cause for war. The Senate is divided. Some want inspection and disarmament; others believe no settlement can make the rival safe. Opposition to the sacrifices has also become politically powerful, especially among families who sheltered escapees and sailors who saw the sanctuary collections.

The resulting cry that **the child-taking city must fall** expresses a genuine moral horror and a policy of annihilation at the same time. Senators who sincerely want the killings ended stand beside creditors, land-seekers and commanders seeking glory. Gateway diplomats surrender weapons and hostages, then refuse an order to abandon the city and rebuild inland. The population manufactures new arms and resists for several years.

The final assault becomes street-by-street killing. Survivors are enslaved. The principal sanctuary is destroyed, ending the civic collection system. Caleran then burns the central district and carries out a deliberate, materially effective salting of the capital's ground. This differs from the later legend about historical Carthage: in World Egg history, the salting actually occurs and is so extensive that the old capital never returns.

Caleran does **not** destroy the whole country. Towns which surrendered early, opposed the sacrifice or supplied the besiegers receive charters. Other towns survive because the army needs grain and ports. Rural communities remain. Some are punished, dispossessed or burdened with reparations; some profit by acquiring the capital's trade.

## What survives the sack

The following continuities make the later royal country possible:

- farmers, fishers, herders and craft households in lesser towns;
- port installations outside the destroyed capital;
- inland languages and local cults older than the Republic;
- refugee quarters in neighbouring cities;
- mixed families connected to Caleran and Qahuri houses;
- agricultural and navigational knowledge;
- hostility towards both the old sacrificial establishment and the conquerors;
- memories of citizens enslaved or scattered after the siege.

The old constitution does not survive intact. Harbour Judges in two spared towns retain municipal functions. The Council of Houses fragments. Former subject communities refuse restoration of the capital's privileges. Caleran governors rule unevenly and often depend upon local brokers.

## From spared towns to the queen's kingdom

The strongest route combines local continuity with a later dynastic arrival.

1. **Immediately after the sack**, an alliance of spared towns administers food, refugees and damaged ports under Atherian supervision.
2. **A generation later**, former Gateway officers, rural notables and mixed merchant houses compete over rebuilding. No one can restore the old capital.
3. **The House of Nerethan** rises at Sarqen through marriage, convoy defence and the ability to negotiate taxes with Caleran.
4. **Nerethan of Delkos and Hadrima of Esharat** join a locally rooted house to a junior Qahuri maternal connection. Hadrima brings river-crown ritual knowledge and relatives, not a deed to the country.
5. Their successors adopt royal government because the postwar league cannot command the whole coast. Municipal councils and town privileges continue beneath the crown.
6. **Samyra** later claims dominion as protector of the spared towns, heir to the Gateway country and descendant of the house which made Sarqen governable. She may invoke Qahuri royal imagery through Hadrima while remaining the queen of a distinct people and territory.

Her claim should explicitly include the promise that the child collections will never return. Opponents can accuse the dynasty of using the rescued dead to legitimise monarchy. That accusation may be partly true without making the old sacrifice tolerable.

## Memory after destruction

Caleran triumphal history says the Republic liberated the coast from child-killers. It minimises enslavement, appropriation and the deliberate ruin of a whole city. Gateway exile histories call the sacrifices inventions or crimes of one isolated priesthood. Both distort the past.

The spared towns preserve different memories. One honours an Atherian captain who opened a sanctuary prison. Another curses the same army for selling its dockworkers. A third claims its ancestors opposed sacrifice generations before the war. Excavated offering grounds show that practice varied between towns and over time.

Samyra's court inherits these incompatible stories. Its royal cult can present the dynasty as the country purified and restored. The best narrative tension lies in the fact that the kingdom really did end an old civic horror and also profits from a conquest that killed, enslaved and dispossessed thousands.

## World Egg boundaries

1. The Gateway provider is a dependent Gluttony Mask, not a Menhir, Patron or true god.
2. Destroying the sanctuary ends its public feeding architecture but need not destroy the Mask.
3. No guilt passes through ancestry. Descendants inherit consequences, property and stories, not culpability for a rite they did not perform.
4. The Republic's proto-Ahdari ancestry predates Mujalib and does not make it a Covenant polity.
5. Samyra's kingdom descends from the spared country, not an untouched continuation of the republic.
6. Qahuri influence enters through particular marriages and rites. It does not merge the Gateway and Sacred Basin peoples.
7. Caleran opposition to sacrifice supplies a genuine cause for war, not an exclusive or cleansing motive.
