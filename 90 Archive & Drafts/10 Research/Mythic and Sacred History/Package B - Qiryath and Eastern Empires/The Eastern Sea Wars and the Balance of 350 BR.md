# The Eastern Sea Wars and the Balance of 350 BR

> [!warning] Exploratory development
> This account names the proposed eastern wars and their participants so that their causes, campaigns and consequences can be judged. It changes no canonical history. Dates before 350 BR remain provisional except where they meet already established Atherian events.

## Two powers approach the same coast

The eastern sea wars grew from the expansion of Vasharan and Caleran power along the same coast. A Vasharan road commander governed several districts from a fortified station, collecting tribute, raising troops and guarding the route to the royal heartland. Subject rulers and city assemblies continued beneath him. The Caleran republic assembled naval leagues whose members supplied ships, money and protected anchorages. Vasharan governors punished a city's refusal as rebellion; Caleran commanders treated withdrawal from a league as defection.

Several old communities occupied the contested coast. **Tasar** was a cultivated harbour city with its own assembly, landholding families and ship crews. **Avesh**, farther inland, controlled the stores and road stations joining the coast to Arqesh and Vashar. The islands and channels of **Ilyessa** provided pilotage, fishing and sheltered passages. Villages in the hills supplied timber, pitch and food to the nearest station and suffered requisition whenever its ruler changed.

Qiryath lay inland from the main fighting. Its road houses carried travellers across both political spheres, while Jirahar residents lived in Vasharan, coastal and Atherian cities. War therefore divided related households. One cousin might provision a Vasharan station, another serve as an Atherian interpreter and a third try to move children away from both armies.

## The successor coast, 684–650 BR

The coast inherited an earlier division from the death of Eryndor III of Ortheia. Eryndor's conquest had joined Ortheian infantry, city levies and allied ships under his personal command. When he died at Arqesh in 684 BR, that army possessed no accepted adult ruler. His half-brother and the child carried by his widow Parena Tiravan became nominal heirs while generals and royal relatives seized the provinces, fleets and treasuries needed to enforce their claims.

**Serevan of Delkos**, commander of the allied fleet, took the islands and several eastern harbours. His court combined Ortheian officers, local pilots, former Arqeshite officials and households attached to the conquered ports. It preserved Eryndor's claim to kingship while rewarding cities that supplied ships. **Demeran Kavos** controlled Ortheia and the western army. **Arezan Tiravan** held Vashar and most of the Ubar basin. **Neresh of Qemaret** established southern garrisons through agreements and marriages with Qahuri crown towns.

Their Wars of the Inheritance moved fleets and soldiers through the coast until about 650 BR. Demeran's agents eventually imprisoned and killed Parena and her child. Serevan sheltered some of her attendants and used their testimony against him. Coastal cities learned to bargain with commanders who claimed the same royal inheritance and issued incompatible grants of land, citizenship and harbour privilege.

The successor kingdoms did not endure as a single political system. Their people and practices remained. Delkosi officers entered maritime republics and royal courts; Ortheian veterans held land among eastern cities; mixed ship crews carried Atherian, Qahuri, Vashari and Arqeshite techniques. The Gateway Wars began within this already connected sea. Later rulers remembered the successor age whenever they claimed that a fleet commander could preserve order after a king's death.

## The western coast during the Gateway Wars, c. 650–520 BR

Caleran's long war with Qarzeth shaped the later eastern conflict. The republic learned to maintain large fleets, collect naval contributions and keep distant crews supplied. Its commanders acquired clients among island cities and merchant households. Qarzeth's destruction in 520 BR released ships, veterans and political ambition into an eastern sea already disturbed by Vasharan expansion.

Vasharan kings had meanwhile incorporated the Ubar riverlands and strengthened Avesh. **Kavran I Tiravan** established grain stations between Arqesh and the western commands. His officials demanded animals, timber and labour along routes whose communities possessed older rights of pasture and water. Resistance remained local because the coastal cities distrusted one another as much as the royal governor.

Trade continued throughout these tensions. Atherian captains bought Vashari horses and river grain. Vasharan officers hired Atherian shipwrights and physicians. Coastal young people entered foreign service and returned with money, spouses, injuries and political opinions. Such relationships later made intervention possible.

## The revolt of Tasar, 514–508 BR

The immediate crisis began with a succession dispute at Tasar. The harbour magistrate supervised the civic warehouses, assigned public berths and negotiated the ships and money owed to Vashar. Two civic households claimed the vacant office. Governor **Mazdan Aresh** supported the weaker claimant in exchange for an increased ship levy and the surrender of three hostages. Tasar's assembly rejected these terms. When Mazdan's soldiers entered the council house to install his candidate, fighting spread through the harbour quarter.

**Liora of Tasar**, a magistrate from a ship-owning family, crossed to the Atherian port of **Velathene** and requested aid. She promised harbour access, timber and membership in a defensive league. The Caleran Senate authorised a limited expedition under **Eryon Telmar**, who already commanded ships left from the final Gateway campaigns. Several allied cities joined for their own reasons: Ilyessan pilots wanted protection from Avesh; merchants wanted access to the Ubar trade; veteran crews expected pay and captured goods.

Telmar's fleet broke the royal blockade of Tasar in 513 BR. A combined force then seized the lower city of Avesh. Warehouses, government records and private homes burned together. Rebels killed Mazdan after he surrendered, despite Liora's promise that he would be taken alive. Vasharan families in Tasar were attacked, and several hundred people fled inland.

Xarun I answered with the road-command armies. Pack trains brought grain from Arqesh; highland estates supplied horses; coastal subject cities provided ships. The Vasharan siege isolated Tasar by land while smaller vessels prevented regular Atherian resupply. Disease spread through the crowded city. Liora ordered household grain seized for the garrison, and poor districts suffered first.

The walls were breached in 508 BR. Xarun executed the principal leaders and sold many defenders and inhabitants. Neighbourhoods associated with the revolt were deported to the Ubar canals and eastern estates. Liora disappeared during the final fighting. Atherian sources place her death at the gate; a Tasaran family later claimed that she lived in exile. The conquest restored Vasharan administration but destroyed the belief that coastal privileges were secure under negotiated tribute.

## The failed punitive voyage, 503 BR

Xarun sought to punish the Atherian cities which had supported Tasar. He appointed **Daryat Vezh**, an Aveshan noble with experience in coastal levies, to lead a fleet against Velathene. The expedition assembled ships from several subject ports. Their hulls, rigging and crew practices differed, and the royal command had little experience directing them as one fleet.

Daryat planned to take water at **Cape Saren**, then strike the Atherian grain anchorage before the defenders could gather. A local pilot warned that the late-season wind made the cape dangerous. The admiral proceeded because delays were consuming food and pay. A storm separated the transports from their escorts. Ships wrecked along the cape, and surviving crews reached land without enough water or siege equipment.

Velathene's defenders attacked the scattered camps. Some Vasharan contingents surrendered; others were killed while trying to launch damaged vessels. Coastal allies accused Daryat of giving the safest berths to Vashari crews. He returned to Avesh with fewer than half his ships and was executed after a royal inquiry. His household lost its estates, although ship captains who had concealed stores escaped punishment by blaming the dead commander.

After the disaster, Xarun spent six years preparing another invasion. He built coastal stores, hired pilots, took hostages from subject ports and placed the land army and naval transports under separate commands. These measures addressed the failures in water, supply and local knowledge that had destroyed Daryat's expedition.

## Xarun's great invasion, 497–491 BR

The second invasion aimed to break the Atherian alliance system. Xarun expected frightened cities to withdraw once a royal army stood on their land. His forces included Vashari cavalry and household troops, Arqeshite engineers, Qahuri and Ubar grain carriers, coastal marines, subject fleets and hired companies. The host moved west through Avesh while the fleet carried heavy stores and siege material.

The Atherian response was organised through the **League of the Eastern Oar**, an alliance of Caleran commanders, island cities and threatened ports. Eryon Telmar held the land crossings. **Nerica Aven**, a ship-owner's daughter who had commanded convoy escorts during the last Gateway war, directed the combined fleet. League membership required ships or money. Cities which tried to withdraw faced confiscation, the dismantling of walls and the installation of friendly councils.

Xarun won the early campaigns. His army burned two settlements which refused provisions and enslaved survivors from a fortified island taken after a short siege. Several cities submitted. Others supplied both sides while waiting for the outcome. Atherian accounts later presented a unanimous defence of liberty; surviving contribution lists reveal coercion, evasion and sharply unequal burdens.

The decisive struggle occurred around the **Narrows of Ilyessa** in 493 BR. Nerica used local pilots and smaller vessels to draw the royal fleet into divided channels. Fireships damaged the Vasharan supply line while storms prevented the heavier transports from regrouping. Xarun's land army survived the battle, but its regular food, replacements and messages had travelled with the defeated fleet.

Telmar avoided a general land engagement. His forces guarded wells, removed fodder and attacked foraging parties. Shortage and disease spread through the royal camps. Xarun ordered requisitions from towns which had submitted, causing some to revolt behind him. The main withdrawal began in 491 BR. Wounded soldiers and dependent camp families were abandoned when animals failed. Atherian pursuers killed stragglers and sold captives.

Xarun retained Avesh and the roads east of it. The invasion had failed to conquer the Atherian leagues, yet it prevented them from taking the Vasharan western command. Both powers proclaimed victory over different parts of the outcome.

## The two liberations of Tasar

Tasar's later civic calendar contained two liberation days. The older marked the expulsion of Mazdan's soldiers in 514 BR. The newer marked the reduction of royal control after Xarun's failed invasion. Political factions celebrated one and neglected the other.

The city's poorer families preserved a different account. They remembered grain seizure under Liora, deportation under Xarun and ship levies under the Atherian league. Their winter song names all three governments and asks which of them brought bread. In this tradition, a liberation earns its name when households can eat, return home and keep their children from another levy.

Later tellers joined the two festivals, their rival processions and the winter song into the **Tale of the Two Liberations**. Families chose different heroes according to the quarter, fleet or exile community from which they descended. The tale preserves the divisions within Tasar after the banners of both great powers had left its streets.

## Orvaz I and the war of clients, 486–431 BR

Xarun's successor **Orvaz I Tiravan** abandoned plans for a direct conquest of the principal Atherian leagues. He used money, grain, marriage and sanctuary to divide them. Exiled city rulers received houses at Avesh. Vasharan timber reached coalitions opposing Caleran leadership. Atherian commanders supported provincial rivals within the western road command.

The League of the Eastern Oar survived the invasion that had brought it together. Caleran senators kept the fleet in service to protect trade and prevent another royal attack. Member cities increasingly paid ship money for decisions made at Caleran. A council that refused its assigned contribution could lose ships, walls or its chosen magistrates. The league consequently extended Atherian power over cities it had first defended.

Vasharan road commanders used local troops and taxes to defend the empire and accumulated enough wealth to treat their commands as family possessions. In 462 BR the governor of Avesh joined three commands against Orvaz. The king offered safe conduct to divide them, then executed two surrendered leaders. **Rathava of Daryam** condemned the broken promise in the palace garden. Orvaz left him alive, destroyed his estate and sold its labourers.

No treaty settled the whole coast. Agreements recognised particular cities, returned named hostages and fixed tolls for a few years at a time. Sailors and caravan masters learned which seals mattered at each harbour. Mixed coastal households often possessed property or relatives under both governments and moved what they could before the next breach.

## Marovan Veyr and the eastern commands, 414–397 BR

The later Atherian commander **Marovan Veyr** built his early reputation among ports whose alliances had been shaped by this older struggle. He secured winter harbours, suppressed raiding coalitions and established clients near the Vasharan sphere. His campaigns belong to the established history of the Caleran republic and remain distinct from Xarun's invasion.

Veyr did not conquer the Vashari highlands or the Ubar basin. He strengthened Atherian influence among coastal settlements and over the routes approaching Velos. Vasharan governors accepted temporary boundaries when they needed freedom to deal with rival houses inland. Some coastal rulers held Atherian civic titles and Vasharan court ranks at the same time.

Jirahar road families carried messages, ransomed prisoners and moved dependants through the unsettled marches. Other Jirahar served openly in the armies. Some houses concealed refugees, some sold information to both governments and others used military contracts to ransom relatives. Later traditions that describe the road houses as neutral observers omit these divided choices.

## The Caleran civil wars and Orvaz II

**Tiraz III Tiravan** died in 403 BR. His son **Orvaz II** spent the first years of his reign defeating a cousin supported by eastern horse houses. The struggle restricted Vasharan action during Veyr's final eastern commands.

When Teren Casvilar and Veyr went to war, Orvaz supplied money, pack animals and limited sanctuary to Veyr's clients. He withheld the central army. Veyr's defeat near Velos in 374 BR damaged the western network while leaving the empire intact. His murder in the gateway country the following year ended his personal command; former clients divided between Casvilar, local rulers and Vasharan patrons.

Casvilar's assassination opened another Caleran succession struggle. Orvaz traded with several parties and delayed a commitment until Darion Velsar allied with Queen Samyra. If they prevailed, their two governments would control the southern approaches to Vasharan ports. Orvaz then supplied credit, animals, refuge and intelligence while keeping the central army near Kavresh. This limited intervention preserved his own succession and allowed him to bargain with the eventual victor.

## The balance in 350 BR

Thalion's victory and the annexation of Sarqen produced a strong Atherian government at the western and southern end of the old contest. In the proposed settlement of 350 BR, Thalion secures the Atherian-speaking ports and recognises selected coastal clients. Orvaz retains Avesh, Arqesh, Qiryath, the Ubar riverlands and the Vashari core.

The agreement leaves Atheria with the fleets, island obligations and coastal clients assembled during the wars. Vashar keeps the inland roads and river provinces, although its strongest governors can bargain with the king. Second Qiryath remains under Vasharan supremacy and continues to govern its sanctuary and communal affairs.

Independent harbours and difficult inland tracks leave routes outside the firm control of either state. Merchants cross through negotiated ports, exiles find patrons on the opposite side and border villages change masters without moving. By 350 BR, the coast contains mixed families, graves from several armies and communities that celebrate incompatible liberations.

## Material conditions of the wars

### Food and water

Vasharan armies depend upon grain from the Ubar and selected Qahuri suppliers, animals from the highlands and stored food at road stations. Atherian fleets draw upon island granaries, coastal markets and the fiscal obligations of league members. Naval defeat matters because inland armies consume food faster than local requisition can replace it.

### Ships and animals

The royal fleet is a collection of subject contingents. Its variety provides numbers and local skills while making unified command difficult. The Atherian fleet develops common signals and maintenance practices during the Gateway Wars. Vashari cavalry remains valuable inland and expensive to feed near a stripped coast.

### Labour

Free citizens, allied crews, debt dependants, prisoners and enslaved workers build ships, repair roads, carry stores and dig siege works. Victorious inscriptions credit kings and commanders. Pay lists name rowers, porters, carpenters and animal drivers, while burial pits record people omitted from accounts of the victories.

### Information

Pilots, translators, traders, hostages and road-house travellers carry knowledge between the powers. False reports ruin expeditions as effectively as lost ships. Governments reward informants and punish communities accused of guiding an enemy, often upon evidence supplied by local rivals.

## Supernatural pressures

Human rulers decide to revolt, invade, compel allies and execute prisoners. Supernatural powers influence particular beliefs and moments without directing the campaigns.

The dependent Pride Mask called the **Witness of Dawn** exploits Vasharan royal imagery. Dreams of ordered nations around a radiant king strengthen Xarun's existing conviction that submission proves truth. Xarun and his commanders still choose the aims, routes and punishments of the invasion.

Atherian crews dedicate weapons and campaign rites to the Menhir **Astaroth**. A genuine correspondence may reveal how force will travel through a particular hull, bridge or formation. Commanders use this knowledge as practical military information; the response supplies no judgement upon the justice of their war.

Coastal people tell of winds and channels answering older Menhir relationships. Victory songs claim such an answer at Ilyessa, while the surviving campaign sequence already records the pilots, divided channels and storm that shaped the battle. Whether a Menhir response also occurred remains unresolved.

## Received memories

Vasharan palace histories describe a defence of truthful order against rebellious governors and oath-breaking cities. Atherian civic histories describe free cities repelling eastern monarchy. Tasaran songs remember two liberations and three forms of requisition. Jirahar accounts follow divided households, rescued captives and the routes which armies failed to close.

Vasharan inscriptions record the road commands and royal victories while omitting some surrendered leaders and punitive sales. Atherian contribution lists reveal which allied cities supplied ships under threat. Destruction layers, burial grounds, household correspondence and road-house memories preserve the losses experienced in the coastal settlements. Together these sources expose the interests and omissions behind the later public histories.
