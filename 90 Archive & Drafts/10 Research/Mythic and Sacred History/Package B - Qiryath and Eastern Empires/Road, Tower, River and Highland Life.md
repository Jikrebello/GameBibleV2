# Road, Tower, River and Highland Life

> [!warning] Exploratory development
> This dossier develops ordinary life within the proposed Package B history. The broad Jirahar social framework is canonical; its ancient households, economic changes and eastern neighbours remain provisional here.

## A connected eastern world

Qiryath, Arqesh and Vashar belonged to a connected eastern region inhabited by several peoples. Pack roads crossed the Reqa shelf, the Ubar carried grain and brick through the river cities, and highland routes supplied timber, ore and horses. Coastal ships joined these inland routes to Atherian ports and the Gateway country. Each district retained its own government, languages and household customs.

The same journey changed character several times. A cargo of Vashari copper might descend by mule to Durqesh, travel in an Arqeshite river boat to Nemeru, pass through a Jirahar broker at Qiryath and reach an Atherian ship at Tasar. At every transfer the owners had to agree upon weights, pay local fees, find interpreters and decide who would bear a loss. Conquest sometimes replaced several tolls with one royal tax, then added military requisitions and travel warrants.

## Jirahar road households

The earliest road house was an extended community whose members recognised the same marriages, debts, burials and promises of protection even when they travelled on separate routes. It included married couples, children, widowed relatives, converts, dependants, servants and sometimes enslaved people. Several carts or pack strings could separate for years and still appeal to the same household elders when one of those obligations was disputed.

Work gave many houses their public names. Bronze-Pan people repaired vessels and scales. Night-Rein households handled animals and difficult routes. Funeral washers prepared bodies and negotiated access to burial grounds. These reputations passed through kin, apprenticeship and adoption. A person born to one house might spend most of life practising another trade.

Routes were seasonal. Families timed movement around pasture, harvest, rain and local fairs. Permanent relatives maintained rooms in tower settlements. Travellers carried news, songs and small goods between districts. Wealthier houses owned animals and financed cargo. Poorer families travelled as hired workers within foreign caravans.

Meals depended upon mobility. Flat breads, lentil stews, onions, dried fruit, cheese and salted meat survived travel. A family's cooking pot often outlasted its cart. Repairs accumulated as visible bands of different metal. Giving a newly married traveller a sound pot meant that the household expected a life beyond the next departure.

Evening camps supplied entertainment and courtship. Young people raced pack animals, traded verses and learned dances from neighbouring communities. Elders used games of memory to test route sequences. Sharp humour discouraged boasting: a celebrated guide might be welcomed home with a song listing every puddle he had mistaken for a spring.

## Tower life

Tower settlements became towns when stores, workshops and households gathered around a visible refuge. The tower served as a watchpoint and signalled that travellers could find defended water, stored food and recognised judges there. Its guest court heard disputes involving people who did not belong to the resident neighbourhoods. Much of the inhabited space lay beneath or around it: cisterns, kitchens, stables, classrooms, burial galleries, workshops and rooms enlarged over generations.

Permanent residents grew food, worked crafts and served travellers. Gardeners possessed detailed knowledge of water and shade. Porters understood the value and destination of goods entering the yard. Teachers copied sacred readings and practical accounts. Bakers and brewers supplied pilgrimage crowds. Children born in a tower might never travel farther than the next village.

Public councils included delegates from the settled neighbourhoods, the families that kept the sanctuary and representatives chosen by road houses using the principal routes. Military protectors attended when defence or pursuit was at issue. During a siege, a guardian who commanded the walls could challenge the judges. During famine, a storekeeper could decide which travelling families received food. Sanctuary households could turn control of festival dates into influence over the market.

First Qiryath enlarged this pattern. Its smoke quarter housed potters and metalworkers. Garden terraces supplied fruit and herbs. Foreign guest houses kept their own ovens. The pilgrim season brought crowded streets, music, animal sales, religious teaching, theft and arguments over lodging.

## Four ancient ways of Jirahar life

Four broad forms of Jirahar communal life, later known as the **Four Ways**, acquired recognisable forms during these centuries.

**Tower communities** combined fixed settlement with obligations to people elsewhere. Their citizens included farmers, scholars, builders and shopkeepers as well as travellers.

**Road Companies** grew from transport, repair and travelling adjudication. Some owned their own animals. Others worked inside Qahuri, Tarkhan, Arqeshite or Vashari caravans.

**Sea Companies** developed later in eastern ports. They carried grain, pilgrims, passengers and small valuable cargoes. Shipboard danger encouraged rules about divided command, salvage and burial at sea.

**Turning Companies** worked at the places where a journey changed direction or means of transport. They transferred goods between river craft, pack roads and watershed crossings and guaranteed that a cargo accepted by one crew would be recognised by the next. They became especially important under the Vasharan relay system.

The Ways overlapped. A tower household might finance a sea branch. A road family could settle for three generations after acquiring land. War might force a prosperous neighbourhood back into movement.

## Language and guarded speech

The Orakha supplied sacred recitation and a common language of law. Everyday speech changed through contact. Arqeshite words entered engineering, music and river trade. Vashari terms entered horse work and imperial government. Atherian expressions reached the coast. Children in commercial households commonly spoke several languages with different relatives and customers.

Road cant allowed travellers to identify a dangerous official, describe an unsafe camp or tell children which adult could be trusted without informing outsiders. It also allowed powerful elders to conceal abuse, debts or profitable information from less connected members of their own households. Its value depended upon who controlled the guarded words and why secrecy was needed.

Official governments repeatedly misunderstood multilingual life. Arqeshite officials classified households by the language used in an interview. Vasharan governors treated a recognised court dialect as proof of political loyalty. Atherian writers later described all speakers found on a road as rootless Jirahar, obscuring settled farmers and members of other peoples.

## Arqeshite river households

Arqeshite society centred upon courtyard households, neighbourhood temples and water obligations. A household might include a married core, several generations of kin, apprentices, servants, enslaved workers and clients renting nearby rooms. Women managed food, textiles, small exchange and family property under rules varying by city and status.

Farmers lived by the timing of gates and channels. They cleared silt, reinforced banks and watched upstream decisions. Village crews elected or inherited local water responsibilities. Imperial rule placed assessors above them and made failure to perform labour a political offence.

City work was specialised. Nemeru produced cloth and dyed thread. Arqesh housed scribes, armourers, court craftsmen and large grain stores. Khalbar supplied transport and military equipment. Durqesh joined river goods to highland pack roads. Brewers, potters, brick makers, leatherworkers, boatwrights, physicians and sellers of cooked food served every city.

Writing supported both ordinary exchange and imperial power. Contracts recorded loans, marriage property, apprenticeship and fields. Store tablets followed grain and rations. Deportation lists classified people by skill. A scribe could protect a widow's property in one case and prepare a household transfer in the next.

Arqeshite schools trained boys and some girls from scribal and elite households. Pupils copied signs, measures, proverbs and model disputes. Their exercises mocked lazy scribes and impossible teachers. Advanced study included mathematics, astronomy, medicine, divination and several languages.

## Land, debt and labour

Landholding across the Ubar included village fields, royal estates, temple property, private holdings and plots assigned to soldiers. Rights overlapped. A farmer might own a house, owe labour on a canal and cultivate land whose harvest was divided with a temple.

During famine, wealthy households lent grain against the next harvest, a field, a period of labour or the service of a family member. When debtors failed to repay, lenders acquired their land and could keep relatives in service. Kings sometimes cancelled selected debts after an accession or revolt so abandoned fields would be cultivated again and indebted villages would support the new reign. Other loans and labour obligations continued.

Imperial projects drew workers through several systems. Free specialists received wages or rations. Villages owed seasonal labour. Estate dependants worked as part of their tenure. Prisoners and enslaved people laboured under guard. These categories affected food, movement, family life and the likelihood of surviving a failed project.

## Deportation and resettlement

Arqeshite deportation moved households to increase labour, weaken resistance and populate strategic districts. Officers selected craftsmen, soldiers, administrators and politically important families. Other captives were assigned to estates or sold.

Deportees travelled under guard with limited possessions. Families could be separated because different institutions requested different skills. Children died during poorly supplied movements. Survivors reached communities already containing people displaced by earlier wars.

Resettlement produced mixed neighbourhoods. A Qiryath engineer might work beside a Nemeru boatwright and a Vashari captive, and their children could inherit several languages and trades. Guards, estate managers and labour assignments still determined where deported families lived. Some maintained their religion and kinship through neighbourhood institutions; others joined local households or lost contact with relatives sent elsewhere.

Some deportees prospered. Palace employment, trade and state land could produce wealth. Their success might help poorer relatives or distance them from captive neighbours. Jirahar arguments over service in Arqesh grew from these unequal lives.

## Conquest and the successor households

The campaigns of Arezan Tiravan and Eryndor of Ortheia brought two conquering courts into the river country. Arezan's Vashari followers expected estates, commands and compensation for relatives taken by Arqesh. Eryndor's soldiers included Ortheian household troops, Compact levies, engineers, sailors and recruits from cities defeated during his western consolidation. Each army arrived with servants, traders, camp families and enslaved captives whose lives seldom appear in royal accounts.

The victors settled in different ways. Vashari officers took houses near road stations and married into canal families whose knowledge kept the fields productive. Ortheian veterans received plots near harbours, crossings and garrison towns. Nemeru boat crews claimed relief from former Ubar taxes, while Qahuri crown houses sought the return of fields lost during Merod II's intervention. These claims could fall upon the same orchard, workshop or village. Soldiers expelled some occupants; other families divided property through marriage, rent or service to the new government.

Eryndor's marriage to **Parena Tiravan** joined the royal households without joining their administrations. Parena received petitions from Vashari officers serving in the western camp and from Arqeshite families seeking protection against confiscation. Her attendants included highland women, Ortheian secretaries and river interpreters. After Eryndor died in 684 BR, the commanders who claimed to protect his heirs divided this household. Parena and her posthumous child were later imprisoned and killed by Demeran Kavos. Surviving attendants entered the courts of Arezan and Serevan of Delkos, carrying court practices, recipes, songs and hostile memories into both states.

The Wars of the Inheritance moved garrisons and households repeatedly between 684 and about 650 BR. A military family could receive land from one successor and lose it when another took the district. Ship crews followed Serevan to the island court at Delkos. Neresh of Qemaret established southern garrisons through marriages with local ruling houses. Arezan's Decree of Roads and Houses returned some deported communities while leaving people displaced by the recent conquest without a general remedy. The mixed river and coastal societies of the following centuries grew from these unequal settlements.

## Vashari household and estate life

Vashari society joined village cultivation, pasture and fortified great-house estates. Kin groups divided work between valley fields and seasonal herding. Wheat, barley, fruit, sheep, goats and horses sustained the core. Highland cooks used sour dairy, dried herbs, grilled meat and thick grain cakes suited to travel.

Great houses controlled land, armed followers and marriage alliances. Their authority depended upon tenant villages, herding partners and sanctuary relationships. Assemblies of household leaders judged grazing and compensation, though powerful rulers could dominate them.

Horse breeding brought wealth and status. It also demanded pasture, labour and winter fodder. Ruzhan households judged a ruler by whether his requisition left enough breeding stock for the following year. Arqeshite seizure of remounts helped turn several houses against the empire.

Metal travelled from Daryam's approaches through miners, charcoal burners, smelters and pack crews. Royal control of ore enriched allied families. Prisoners worked dangerous galleries. A mine praised for fine royal cups could also contain unburied workers whose households never received their wages.

## The imperial road

The Vasharan Dawn Road relied upon stations spaced according to water, fodder and terrain. Each station maintained animals, rooms, a repair yard and stores. Royal messengers held priority. Merchants and travellers paid for services or presented recognised warrants.

Stations encouraged settlement. Bakers, smiths, animal doctors, innkeepers and sex workers served passing traffic. Villages sold fodder and complained when armies requisitioned it. Robbers learned which officials carried tax silver. Governors used station reports to monitor movement.

Jirahar households became important as interpreters, carriers and keepers of parallel route memory. Their knowledge could save an army or let refugees avoid one. Vasharan rulers alternately rewarded and suspected them.

## Coastal cities

Tasar, Avesh and Ilyessa joined inland imperial systems to the sea. Their populations included older shore communities, Arqeshite and Vashari settlers, Jirahar families and Atherian-speaking merchants. Citizenship and political office differed by city.

Shipyards consumed timber, rope, pitch and metal. Grain arriving from the Ubar fed crews and urban households. Fishing, salt making and coastal gardens provided local food. Warehouses, taverns and shrines clustered near the quays.

Naval service divided society. Wealthy households financed ships and gained public honour. Citizens rowed or fought under civic obligations. Subject towns supplied crews. Paid sailors, freed people and enslaved rowers served beside them. Families ashore carried debt and bereavement after a lost fleet.

The wars created mixed loyalties. A merchant could prefer Vasharan roads and Atherian shipping. A city assembly could expel an eastern governor and later discover that its Atherian liberators required tribute and hostages. Tasar's two liberation festivals preserve both experiences.

## Marriage, status and conversion

Marriage connected roads, estates and governments. Road houses used it to secure hospitality across circuits. Arqeshite kings married royal women into subject houses. Vashari nobles exchanged children through hostage marriage and fosterage. Coastal merchants married across language communities.

Women exercised real authority through property, work, religious patronage and household alliances. Those opportunities varied by status. Enslaved women could be bought for sex or household labour. Elite marriages could also involve coercion where family survival depended upon consent to a ruler's demand.

Jirahar conversion law admitted outsiders through public instruction, declared obligations and reception before recognised members of a community. Practice remained contested. A convert accepted in one tower might be rejected by an old Qiryath house, and mixed children became particularly vulnerable during restoration disputes. The Yaelith–Neram household forced Second Qiryath to recognise families received through this public process when a restoration faction later tried to expel them as foreigners.

## Religion in daily practice

Jirahar households followed the religious tradition later called the **Path**. They preserved teachings carried from the Glass, observed appointed rest, recited sacred history, buried their dead and brought offerings to recognised sanctuaries. Qiryath became the principal centre of sacrifice and pilgrimage, while road houses and distant towers continued religious life far beyond the city.

Arqeshite cities maintained temple households tied to neighbourhood, craft and river. Offerings supported personnel and redistributed food. Divination and royal astrology shaped public decisions. The imperial image cult added compulsory acknowledgement above local practice.

Vashari households kept hearth fires and made seasonal visits to local sanctuaries. Teachings of Right Order praised truthful speech, cultivation and defence of the created world. Royal ideology turned those teachings towards obedience. Daryam critics preserved the distinction at considerable personal cost.

People also made bargains outside formal institutions. A spring might be governed through a local court relationship, and a sailor might promise part of a catch to a shore power. An Idol Mask could exploit an image already used in household or royal worship. Springs, shore powers and Masks remained separate actors even when worshippers used similar language for water, fire or ancestors.

## Death and burial

Jirahar burial societies sought prompt, named burial even under hostile governments. Recovering bodies after execution could bring imprisonment or mutilation. Those who buried a person undertook to preserve the name, notify distant kin and settle duties left unfinished by the death. The grave itself gave them no automatic title to the surrounding land.

Arqeshite practices varied by city and status. Household tombs, city cemeteries and temple rites existed beside royal monuments. Kings denied burial to selected rebels as a display of power. Deportees sometimes sent bones home generations later.

Vashari communities debated how a corpse could be returned without polluting a household fire, cultivated earth or drinking water. Highland exposure, stone chambers and burial at a distance from settlement all appear in regional evidence, with different sanctuary teachers defending each practice. Royal tombs followed the customs of particular dynasties and reveal little about the burial of ordinary households.

## What survives into later history

By 350 BR, the region contained visible layers of all these societies. Qiryath remained a sacred city within an extensive diaspora. Vasharan officials maintained Arqeshite canals and used Arqeshite water terms. The royal roads connected provinces whose inhabitants kept their own languages and customs. Coastal cities remembered several rulers and continued trading with their neighbours on both sides of the imperial frontier.

Later Atherian roads followed these older grades, and their provinces reused established water districts, stations and port facilities. The populations governed through those institutions included households whose histories had begun under several ancient governments. Much later, eastern Eclessian communities inherited the same canals, loan words and mixed neighbourhoods.
