# Jirahar and the Eastern Empires — Comparative Deepening and Canon Review

## Purpose

This review brings three bodies of research into the existing northern and eastern chronology:

- Israelite and Jewish development from patriarchal tradition through judges, monarchy, exile, return and foreign empire;
- Romani, Irish Traveller and other itinerant histories of migration, work, persecution and dispersed community;
- Iranian and Persian histories of plateau coalitions, restoration, imperial government, the Greek wars and heroic memory.

Each body supplies a different part of the history. The scriptural sequence gives the main sacred and political development of the Jirahar. Traveller histories explain the practical life of communities dispersed among host societies. Persian history develops the power which succeeds Arqesh and then meets the Atherian leagues. Their origins remain separate throughout.

The resulting history begins after the Prophet's death and ends with the Atherian incorporation of the east. It preserves all current dates as controlling points. The new periods, social developments and conflicts remain proposals until a canon pass.

## Controlling World Egg history

The following facts already govern the comparison.

1. The Great Prophet dies around c. 2165 BR, after the long Glass refuge. No human successor inherits his complete commission.
2. Southern war-settlement leagues become proto-Atherian between c. 2130 and 2050 BR.
3. The Near-Glass camp continuum divides gradually between c. 1950 and 1750 BR. Proto-Jirahar institutions favour portable courts, route memory and translocal custody. Proto-Ahl-e-Ahd institutions favour defended wells, territorial oath coalitions and enforceable restitution.
4. Jirahar communities build towers and establish the First Qiryath during the long period c. 1400–800 BR.
5. Arqesh destroys the First Qiryath in 742 BR and deports part of its population.
6. Arqesh falls around 690 BR. Return and diaspora continue together, and the Second Qiryath is rebuilt around c. 660 BR.
7. Atheria later absorbs the eastern riverlands. The completed dates and circumstances require development within the approach to Thalion around 350 BR.
8. The Second Qiryath is destroyed under Atherian rule in 70 AR. That later history belongs to the next chronological package.

The Jirahar are principally Ark-derived. They encounter and incorporate Orphaned neighbours without becoming an Orphaned people. The Tarkhan remain separate Orphaned societies. Arqesh is a mortal river empire whose culture precedes its final imperial dynasty.

## Proposed historical architecture

### I. The road households, c. 2165–1750 BR

After the Prophet's death, his people possess several real offices and no universal human leader. Provisioners control stores; judges settle disputes; archivists copy teaching and household names; captains defend routes; elders arrange marriages and care for dependants. Factions attempt to raise these responsibilities into succession. None succeeds.

The people leaving the Glass encounter inhabited country. Orphaned well communities, herders, ruined estate populations and small river settlements possess their own laws and memories of the Giant collapse. Near-Glass households negotiate access to water and burial ground. They purchase, receive and sometimes seize land. A successful judge can protect a mixed camp; a strong captain can turn emergency command into hereditary authority.

The earliest Jirahar-shaped institutions arise from distance. Households separated by drought continue to recognise judgements, marriages and burials made elsewhere. A travelling bench carries copies of important decisions. Route singers remember the order of wells and the families responsible for maintaining them. A grave can establish responsibility towards the dead without establishing political ownership of the surrounding country.

The northern fission grows from the accumulated consequences of these practices. Communities closer to defensible water and Tarkhan pressure favour walls, armed protector houses and territorial restitution. Those whose families occupy several routes fear that fixed authority will exclude distant kin. Marriage crosses the developing division for generations. One sibling may join a defended well while another remains with a travelling court.

This period draws selectively upon patriarchal stories. Wells, marriage, herds, fosterage and burial become the substance of history. Household guilt also belongs here. A road ancestor may sell a younger relative into a foreign caravan, expel a servant and child, or massacre a host settlement after sexual violence. Later sacred descent cannot make those acts righteous.

### II. The Bronze Roads and their breaking, c. 1750–1325 BR

The proto-Jirahar enter a wider exchange system linking Qahuri river courts, eastern canal cities, Gateway ports, Tarkhan carriers and Atherian maritime households. Bronze, salt, cloth, grain, timber, animals, medicines and captives move across the same routes. Certain Jirahar families become interpreters, drovers, repairers, caravan physicians and negotiators. Others cultivate land beside permanent shelters.

Route service produces the first great occupational houses. Their names may refer to copper pans, horse medicine, funeral washing, river sounding or night navigation. Each house contains varied work; the name records the service through which it first acquired a wide reputation. Marriages establish branches in several towns. A child can grow up speaking a local vernacular, a family road cant and the formal language of sacred recitation.

The Breaking of the Bronze Roads between c. 1450 and 1325 BR destroys the exchange system unevenly. Consecutive harvest failures, war among palace houses, royal seizure of transport and movements of refugees and armed sailors make long journeys dangerous. A few Jirahar houses profit by carrying food at extreme prices. Others lose animals, relatives and host communities. Some abandon distant dependants to preserve their core households.

Towers acquire new importance as granaries, workshops and shelters. Settled families become numerous around them. Road companies shorten their circuits and carry news between districts no longer joined by royal administration. The event fixes a lasting tension: towers can sustain travelling people, while wealthy towers can also use emergency stores to command them.

### III. The age of judges and first towers, c. 1400–1050 BR

Jirahar memory arranges local defenders, travelling judges, midwives, singers and caravan captains into an age of deliverance. They never form one company. Some live generations apart. The tales recur because each settlement faces a recognisable danger: a toll fortress taking children as security, a raiding household poisoning wells, a tower custodian selling grain during famine, or a foreign king demanding an image inside a sanctuary.

Several deliverers become guilty through victory. One executes surrendered raiders after promising safe passage. Another makes a vow which costs the life of a beloved child. A coalition answering the rape and murder of a traveller destroys the offender's settlement, kills families who had no part in the assault and seizes women to preserve a defeated remnant. The remembered age therefore contains real courage and a descent into retaliatory horror.

Judges derive authority from learning, reputation, emergency consent and the willingness of other communities to enforce a decision. Military captains repeatedly seek to keep their power. A few establish protector houses. Their followers guard roads and recover captives; they also collect tolls, take hostages and fight over marriages. The demand for stable protection grows from dangers people have experienced, rather than from an abstract argument over monarchy.

Travelling livelihoods become more structured. Tower councils license market yards and burial grounds. Foreign towns pay companies to maintain pumps, animals and wagons, then expel them when guilds complain. Families create guarded speech during periods of slave-taking. The cant protects routes and children while allowing powerful elders to conceal internal abuse.

### IV. First Qiryath and the protector houses, c. 1050–800 BR

Qiryath grows where routes, water and burial converge. Its early buildings include stores, workshops, guest courts, teaching rooms and a sanctuary. Pilgrims arrive because the city holds objects and readings associated with the Prophet's road. Permanent inhabitants include craftsmen, gardeners, scholars, porters and families serving travellers. Qiryath becomes urban without ceasing to depend upon people who live elsewhere.

The city never gains uncontested sovereignty over every Jirahar. Some towers accept its calendar and teachers while rejecting its officers. Road houses fear that a sacred centre will turn custody into taxation. Protector families compete to defend the city and control access to it. One may acquire a Davidic remembered arc: an overlooked singer or animal keeper enters military service, gathers debtors and refugees, protects Qiryath and later abuses the authority won in war.

The strongest version of this history gives the protector real achievements and personal crimes. He defeats a predatory river prince, feeds the city during siege and welcomes skilled exiles. He also takes a subordinate's wife or husband through coercive office, arranges the loyal spouse's death, and allows a commander to kill surrendered rivals. His repentance, if genuine, leaves the dead and the damaged household in history.

Successive protector houses and councils build civic institutions. Food levies, public repair, military service and sanctuary patronage create durable offices. Prophetic teachers confront rulers over land seizure, debt and foreign images. Some are killed. Their later Lives should preserve the concrete grievance which brought the confrontation rather than turning them into voices of generalised dissent.

### V. Arqeshite ascendancy and the first destruction, c. 800–690 BR

Arqesh develops from an old eastern river city into a deportation empire. Canal wealth, organised infantry, siegecraft, cavalry, subject troops and royal terror support its expansion. The kings present transferred peoples, mutilated rebels and ruined walls as proof that resistance is useless. They also repair waterways, protect chosen roads and employ scholars from conquered societies.

Qiryath matters to Arqesh because its roads move goods and information beyond provincial control. The empire first demands tribute and hostages. A later king requires installation of the royal image within the sacred precinct. Jirahar factions divide. Merchants fear losing their families and property; militant households believe compromise has already become apostasy; villagers outside the wall expect to bear the cost of either decision.

The siege of 742 BR kills inhabitants, destroys the upper tower and leads to deportation. Soldiers seize people according to skill and status. Some families are divided between imperial workshops, agricultural settlements and private owners. Executed defenders are denied burial. Survivors hide bodies, bribe guards and carry partial names into exile.

Arqesh resettles loyal populations in the conquered district and appoints local collaborators. Jirahar remain around the ruins, flee along several roads or continue in older diaspora settlements. The first dispersal therefore produces no clean division between exiles and natives. A child born to an Arqeshite soldier and a Jirahar survivor may inherit claims from both.

Life in the empire acquires its own depth. Jirahar engineers, musicians, animal doctors, scribes and traders serve royal cities. Some become wealthy. Burial societies and household teachers preserve worship. Officials alternate between protection, taxation, forced labour and seizure. An imperial census used to organise grain later becomes a deportation list after revolt.

### VI. The fall of Arqesh and the High-Road Empire, c. 690–520 BR

Arqesh falls through several converging failures. Dynastic murder divides the army. Provincial governors withhold revenue. Neglected canals damage harvests. Tarkhan and upland coalitions disrupt remounts and supply. Subject cities rebel. Atherian leagues attack western holdings and later claim the whole victory.

The leading successor should come from an older upland society east of Arqesh. For research purposes, this review calls its dominion the **High-Road Empire**. The name is provisional. Its royal house controls cultivated valleys, mountain passes, horse country and a sanctuary tradition centred upon truth, oath and tended light. It gathers former enemies rather than conquering the entire region alone.

The first High-Road king enters the Arqeshite capital after defeating the last royal armies and attracting provincial defections. He retains canal administrators and kills commanders who continue resistance. His government restores several sanctuaries, permits displaced peoples to return and confirms local rulers whose loyalty makes the provinces governable. Roads and staging posts bind the resulting empire.

The restoration of Qiryath around c. 660 BR belongs to this policy. The king supplies timber, funds and safe conduct. He expects road cooperation, stable taxes and a community able to counter former Arqeshite houses. Jirahar tradition can recognise providence in the decree without making him one of its prophets.

Return creates conflict. Families who remained near the ruin possess fields and houses abandoned generations earlier. Returned priestly and protector houses claim ancestral title. Some exiles remain in prosperous eastern communities. Mixed marriages become a test of belonging. A severe restoration faction repudiates spouses and children; its opponents accuse it of rebuilding a holy city through another expulsion.

The High-Road Empire remains imperial. Provinces owe grain, money, soldiers, horses and labour. Rebellions bring siege and execution. Royal rhetoric describes the king's order as truth and opponents as servants of the Lie. Local schools debate whether such language expresses an insight into created order or turns political success into idolatry.

### VII. Atherian leagues and the eastern wars, c. 620–350 BR

Atherian and eastern power meet first through coastal and river cities whose households already trade with both. A western province of the High-Road Empire contains mixed ports and Atherian-speaking settlements. Its governor manipulates local succession, and a revolt appeals to several Atherian leagues. The leagues send ships and burn an imperial centre before quarrelling over command.

The eastern king suppresses the revolt and demands submission from its foreign supporters. His first punitive expedition fails in unfamiliar coastal conditions. A successor launches a larger invasion through allied ports, subject fleets and overland supply. It wins several battles and destroys settlements, but naval loss and resistance among the Atherian leagues make permanent occupation too expensive.

Atherian victory produces a maritime confederacy which begins as defence and develops into an empire of tribute. Allies who attempt to leave lose ships, walls or hostages. The High-Road kings then influence Atherian politics through money, exiled claimants and agreements with rival cities. Eastern support helps one Atherian coalition defeat another. The border shifts without either civilisation disappearing.

Jirahar communities occupy every side of these wars. Qiryath lies under eastern authority; western towers trade with Atherian cities; road companies carry supplies; and diaspora households serve as interpreters and physicians. Atherian commanders suspect them of eastern loyalty. Eastern governors suspect them of sharing information through religious routes. Some Jirahar exploit both governments, while others are punished for decisions made by distant kin.

The final republican crisis preceding Thalion brings Atherian armies deep into the east. One commander may use an eastern succession dispute to establish clients and seize wealth. The High-Road royal house survives in diminished form or falls during the campaigns leading to unification. The choice requires coordination with the approved Thalion architecture and the queen's country.

By 350 BR, Atherian rule inherits roads, provinces, eastern great houses, Jirahar charters and a developed imperial religion. The conquest changes government; it does not empty the east or end its history.

## The Jirahar as a dispersed civilisation

### Four ways of life before Atheria

The later Four Ways should acquire identifiable early histories.

**Tower communities** grow from stores, refuges and schools. They support gardens, workshops, burial grounds and regional courts. Their citizens include people who rarely travel. Towers can protect road families and exploit them.

**Road Companies** develop from caravan, repair and adjudication circuits. Some own animals and wagons; others travel as hired specialists within foreign caravans. Routes change with war, technology and host policy.

**Sea Companies** arise around eastern and southern ports. They carry pilgrims, grain, passengers and small cargoes, and their law develops through shipboard danger and divided jurisdictions. They should not be projected unchanged into the first Near-Glass generations.

**Turning Companies** join watersheds, road yards and river craft. Their importance grows under the High-Road and Atherian road systems. Some become wealthy brokers whose sedentary agents live across several cities.

These ways overlap. A tower household may finance a road company and send a child to a sea branch. A poor road family may settle for three generations before war renews its mobility. Identity survives the change.

### Occupations and economic change

Jirahar occupations should vary by era and region. Early households herd, carry, mend, judge and preserve routes. Bronze-age specialists work metal, animals, funerals and long-distance trade. Arqeshite exile places engineers, musicians, scribes and physicians in imperial cities. High-Road government recruits relay keepers, interpreters and fiscal agents. Atherian rule opens law, military service and maritime commerce while challenging older guilds.

Every occupation needs an economic setting. Travelling smiths prosper where villages cannot support a permanent workshop. Cheap imperial foundries later ruin them. Animal doctors gain influence during cavalry expansion. Sea pilots lose work when a new harbour bypasses their coast. A legal family becomes powerful because several governments recognise its translations.

This history creates achievement and pleasure as well as hardship. Weddings join branches after years apart. Musicians reshape foreign tunes. Children learn three languages from customers and grandparents. A repaired cooking vessel may travel through a family longer than a royal charter. Fairs provide courtship, racing, food, jokes and news.

### Kinship, marriage and conversion

Dispersal makes kinship political. Marriages connect routes, secure hospitality and carry property between towers. Senior households can turn those functions into coercion. A daughter may be married to settle a debt; a son may be forbidden to marry a convert whose skill and devotion are unquestioned; a widow may use her birth kin against an abusive husband's company.

Endogamy can preserve law under hostile rule. It can also harden into status pride. Conversion therefore needs a history of actual disputes. Some communities welcome outsiders after war reduces their numbers. Others restrict entry during persecution. A wealthy convert family may buy influence. A child of mixed parentage may be accepted in one tower and rejected in another until later law resolves the case.

The comparison with Jewish and Traveller histories supplies pressure, not the ruling. Existing Jirahar canon governs descent and conversion. Historical articles should show how those rules acquired contested interpretations.

### Speech and memory

Jirahar language should preserve several layers:

1. a sacred register associated with the Scrolls and early Near-Glass judgement;
2. regional speech shaped by Tarkhan and Ahdari neighbours;
3. Arqeshite words for canal work, administration, instruments and city trades;
4. eastern imperial terms for roads, provinces and cavalry;
5. Atherian civic and legal vocabulary;
6. local cants used by particular occupational or escape networks.

No single cant represents the original language of the Prophet. Guarded speech develops in response to danger and trade. It may preserve jokes and courtship as readily as secrets. Foreign officials routinely mistake it for proof of conspiracy.

### Persecution and collaboration

Jirahar history needs named policies rather than an undifferentiated experience of hatred. Arqesh deports people for imperial control. The High-Road rulers restore Qiryath and later punish particular revolts. Atherian cities may grant quarters and citizenship while other provinces restrict guild membership or child movement. One ruler's settlement decree can become another ruler's seizure list.

Slavery must remain explicit where imposed. A Jirahar craft company may travel because a monastery, palace or private estate owns it and hires out its labour. Emancipation then leaves the household without animals or recognised stopping places. Descendants can remember freedom and dispossession in the same feast.

Jirahar individuals also collaborate. A tax farmer can enrich himself by identifying poorer families. An interpreter can save prisoners and help an army select deportees. A tower council may surrender a road company to preserve its charter. Persecution explains danger; it does not erase agency.

## The eastern successor civilisation

### Required regional ground

The High-Road Empire requires one developed eastern homeland before promotion. Its geography should include:

- an upland agricultural core connected to an older lowland civilisation;
- mountain roads linking riverlands and open country;
- horse-breeding districts with Tarkhan relationships;
- at least two royal centres used in different seasons;
- an older language and priestly tradition predating the royal house;
- subject cities inherited from Arqesh;
- a western maritime frontier through which the Atherian wars occur.

Its inhabitants need a name unrelated to modern Persia. The royal house may be ethnically particular while the imperial identity becomes political. Arqeshite, Qahuri, Tarkhan, highland and Jirahar subjects retain distinct histories.

### Religion and royal legitimacy

The older eastern religion can centre upon the Lord as source of created truth while containing regional errors, contested spirits and royal claims. It should not possess the complete covenant knowledge of the Prophet. A priestly reform may reject images, emphasise true speech and tend fire as a sign of creaturely order. Later kings adopt its language.

Several supernatural interpretations can coexist:

- one old highland sanctuary maintains a genuine but limited Menhir correspondence;
- royal splendour is mostly ceremony, reputation and inherited art;
- a dependent Pride Mask imitates the expected radiance around one conquering king;
- tended fire remains ordinary created matter;
- an epic bird helper is a court person, Patron visitor or literary figure in different accounts.

No resemblance decides identity. Each actual supernatural encounter requires its own continuity and historical evidence.

### Government and violence

The High-Road kings rule through great houses, appointed governors, local assemblies, temples, tax districts and military colonies. Their roads improve travel and enable extraction. Royal women manage estates and marriage alliances. Subject troops retain their own equipment and commanders.

Accommodation strengthens the empire. So does fear. A province that submits can retain its sanctuary and ruler. A rebellious city can be burned, its leaders mutilated and its families deported. Royal inscriptions call both decisions restoration of truth. Historical prose should identify the people who benefited and the people who paid.

## Proposed conflicts and legendary cycles

These are development leads rather than Garden roots or canon events.

### The Keeper Who Married the Return

A Jirahar widow who remained near ruined Qiryath marries an exile official sent home by the High-Road king. Her household controls the surviving cistern; his carries an ancestral temple claim. Their marriage makes rebuilding possible and enrages both constituencies. When a purity faction later expels foreign spouses, she refuses to let its judges classify the children of her own workers as strangers.

### The Three Fires of the Great King

Three provinces claim that one royal fire accompanied the first successor king. An upland sanctuary remembers a tended flame carried on a mule. Arqeshite art shows an imperial brazier. Jirahar tradition remembers only the lamp lit during the return decree. Later theologians mistakenly make the three objects one miraculous relic.

### The Horse Lord at the City Gate

A Tarkhan leader whose riders helped overthrow Arqesh arrives for the division of spoils. The new king offers pasture and a royal marriage but refuses him entry with an armed household. Their standoff ends through fosterage, not friendship. The child raised between court and open country later commands troops against relatives during revolt.

### The Champion and the Unrecognised Son

An eastern great-house champion fights an Atherian-allied young commander at a ford. Neither knows that the younger man was born during an earlier hostage marriage. Tokens identifying him arrive after the fatal wound. Both eastern and Atherian singers claim the father, while the mother's city remembers the story as proof that great houses consume their children.

### The Truth-Teller in the Lying King's Garden

A priest condemns a successful High-Road king for executing surrendered rebels after guaranteeing safe passage. The king preserves the priest's life and destroys his family estate. Later court histories cite the spared priest as evidence of tolerance; his descendants preserve the names of the labourers sold from the estate.

### The Two Liberations of the Western Port

A mixed coastal city expels its eastern governor with Atherian help. Years later, the Atherian league removes its fleet, takes hostages and increases tribute. The city's festival contains two processions called the Liberation. Each ends at a different wall scar, and families argue over which procession should go first.

### The White-Haired Fosterling

An upland lord exposes a white-haired infant from fear that an Idol touched the child. A great bird-shaped court person raises her and later returns her as a gifted physician and negotiator. She refuses the throne her father offers and becomes patron of a rival house. The actual identity and motives of the fosterer remain separate research questions.

### The Apron Raised at Arqesh

Workers forced to repair siege engines revolt during Arqesh's collapse. A smith raises a scorched work apron so allies can see the breach. Successor kings later place the apron in their coronation procession and omit the massacre of prisoners committed by the rebel army after victory.

## Collision and source-fidelity review

### Existing narrative collisions

- The Jirahar road-house material must not duplicate [[01 Canon Reference/History/80 Myths and Legendary Histories/G-04 - The Seven Who Stopped Seeking Home.md|Seven Who Took the Wrong Road]]. That company belongs to its own early Scattering tradition.
- A David-shaped Qiryath protector must remain separate from [[01 Canon Reference/History/80 Myths and Legendary Histories/G-05 - The Iron Ring before the Crown.md|the Crownless Company]] and any Atherian outlaw-founder.
- The Arqeshite foundling ruler remains in a predecessor city as previously recommended. He is not Cyrus, Sargon or the Caleran wolf-founder.
- The High-Road restoration king cannot absorb the Great Prophet's commission or become a manifested pre-Redeemer saint.
- The white-haired fosterling must not become another version of Caleran's wolf-raised siblings. Her adulthood, occupation, conflict and refusal produce a different tale.
- The unrecognised-son tragedy should attach to one eastern great house. It cannot become the origin of all Tarkhan–river hostility.
- The Two Liberations concerns an Atherian–eastern coastal city and remains separate from the Gateway Wars.

### Hardness retained

The history preserves the load-bearing violence of its sources:

- patriarchal household exploitation, slaveholding, sexual violence and kin massacre;
- Judges-era vows, rape, dismemberment, collective punishment and seizure of women;
- royal abuse, arranged killing, civil war and forced labour;
- Assyrian and Babylonian siege, mutilation, deportation and slavery;
- restoration disputes capable of breaking mixed families;
- imperial tribute, suppression, execution and destruction under Persian-shaped rule;
- maritime revolt, invasion, subject coercion and the conversion of defensive leagues into empires;
- Traveller histories of legal enslavement, expulsion, child removal, forced assimilation and genocide.

None of these acts becomes atmospheric cruelty. Each must belong to identifiable perpetrators, victims and consequences when promoted. No community inherits collective biological guilt.

## Canon implications requiring approval

### Strongly supported additions

1. Divide proto-Jirahar development into road-house, Bronze-road, judges and First-Qiryath phases.
2. Establish that First Qiryath grew from an inhabited network of towers, villages and routes.
3. Give Jirahar occupational and linguistic branches historical dates rather than treating them as timeless.
4. Expand the 742 BR destruction into siege, killing, deportation, resettlement and several kinds of survival.
5. Make the c. 660 BR return a partial, politically sponsored restoration involving disputes with people who remained.
6. Establish a separate post-Arqesh eastern empire through an upland coalition and inherited river government.
7. Give Atheria and the eastern empire a generational sequence of coastal revolt, invasion, naval limitation, league imperialism and diplomacy before Thalion.

### Additions requiring regional development

1. The name, territory and peoples of the eastern upland core.
2. The High-Road dynasty, its royal centres and succession.
3. The form and historical errors of its truth-and-light religion.
4. The precise western coast or river on which the Atherian wars occur.
5. The Jirahar protector houses and whether any ever claimed kingship.
6. The command through which the final Atherian republican armies entered the east.

### Material remaining exploratory

- all eight legendary leads;
- the provisional title **High-Road Empire**;
- every proposed ruler, champion and priest;
- any actual Menhir, Idol, Patron or saintly involvement;
- exact dates between the controlling chronological anchors;
- the final fate of the eastern royal house under Thalion.

## Recommendation

Adopt the seven-part Jirahar periodisation and the existence of a Persian-shaped successor to Arqesh as the working direction. Develop the successor homeland before naming its dynasty. This gives Qiryath's return a credible political setting and creates an eastern rival worthy of the Atherian leagues.

The first canon-development pass should cover the Road House Centuries through the fall of Arqesh. The second should cover the High-Road Empire, the return to Qiryath and the eastern wars. Traveller-derived social history should be written through both passes instead of appearing as a detached section about wandering customs.

## Later religious-history continuation

[[From Second Qiryath to the Peshara - Revolt Destruction and Jirahar Religious Formation]] continues this architecture beyond the period covered here. It preserves a settled Second-Qiryath society and an already extensive diaspora, then separates the pagan Atherian destruction of 70 AR from the converted empire's Vestian persecutions of 377–395. The Peshara develops gradually through emergency rulings, one early subject-ordered collection, several regional conversations and later diaspora redactions. It is not invented in one post-destruction council and does not immediately make its teachers rulers of every Jirahar community.
