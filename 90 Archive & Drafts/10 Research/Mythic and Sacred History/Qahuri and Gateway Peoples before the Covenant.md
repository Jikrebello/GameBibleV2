# Qahuri and Gateway Peoples before the Covenant

> [!note] Author-only development record
> The approved identities and belonging rules were promoted through [[Ancient Peoples before Atherian Unification - Canon Integration Review]]. The account retains its 350 BR boundary.

## PA-01 — The Qahuri

The Qahuri were the river people of the Sacred Basin. Their principal ancestry came from Orphaned communities which survived the collapse of nearby Ishkatari works and other Giant courts. Refugees, conquerors and immigrants entered the Basin over time, but no Glass migration founded its civilisation and no Giant royal blood defined its humanity.

**Qahuri** is a useful broad name and was increasingly used in foreign dealings, royal proclamations and travel by the mature crown periods. Early inhabitants more often identified themselves through a town, stretch of water, temple, occupation or household. A boat family of Tenaret and a quarry household near Sakhret could share river customs while possessing different political loyalties and speech.

Belonging arose through residence, acknowledged kin, work on water systems and participation in local worship. Marriage could bring a newcomer into a town network. Royal settlement moved soldiers and craftspeople between districts. Enslaved people worked fields, tombs and households without automatically acquiring the rights of a free Qahuri house. Manumission, marriage and acceptance by a neighbourhood or temple could make their descendants local members.

The Qahuri shared flood reckonings, river food, linen, reed craft and a long argument over the proper relationship between crown, temple and canal community. These common practices did not produce one government. A foreign ruler could occupy several districts and still depend on Qahuri water workers, boat crews and local officers whose knowledge the army could not replace.

By 350 BR, Qahuri identity joined the Basin across three principal crowns, numerous towns and several languages or dialects. People could serve a Vasharan governor, belong to a Qemaret temple and call themselves Qahuri without accepting that the foreign ruler possessed the Twin-Reed crown.

**Identity ruling:** a principally Orphaned river people and civilisation. It is broader than a dynasty, temple or crown and distinct from every later confessional identity.

## PA-02 — The Twin-Reed regional identities

Tenaret, Qemaret and Sakhret gave the Basin its most durable regional identities. Their inhabitants were Qahuri, but centuries of royal competition made town and crown allegiance consequential. Foreign writers who called each faction a separate nation misunderstood a political division; royal poets who called them perfectly united concealed recurring war.

**Tenareti** identity centred on the middle river, old spillway communities and the prestige of the Senareth and Zaheret crowns. The town's households often described themselves through the water chain they maintained. Temple and royal service gave some lineages a standing recognised beyond their district.

**Qemareti** identity developed around the lower fan, grain shipping and access to Gateway roads. The city attracted military settlers, merchants and multilingual households. Under the Yasharet dynasty, opponents used *foreign* as a political insult against districts whose mixed families had already become Qemareti through residence, marriage and service.

**Sakhreti** identity joined the southern narrows to terraces, stone approaches, garrisons and pastoral connections. Military households possessed unusual influence because they guarded the road beyond the cultivated corridor. Their claims to represent an older or purer Basin belonged to restoration propaganda. Sakhret itself contained subject soldiers, mining families and immigrants.

The Twin-Reed coronation taught that the regions belonged under a shared order while retaining distinct rites. This was a political ideal rather than an ethnic formula. A person could change crown allegiance, be transferred as a hostage or marry across the division without ceasing to be Qahuri. Royal executions and confiscations made such choices dangerous.

By 350 BR, these identities remained alive. Tenaret possessed the strongest genealogical prestige, Qemaret controlled much of the lower shipping and Sakhret held military power in the south. None had absorbed the others.

**Identity ruling:** three regional and civic Qahuri identities, never three biological peoples. Their names should receive stable sections within a shared article rather than independent ancestry trees.

## PA-03 — The Gateway coastal people

The Gateway people developed where cultivated country, desert approaches and Thalassian shipping met. Their society joined older shore populations, proto-Ahdari migrants, maritime settlers, captives and freed households between approximately 1700 and 1200 BR. The joining created durable common institutions, marriage networks and ways of life. This makes the Gateway one of the ancient world's clear Braided populations.

No single ancestry dominated every town. Families around old wells preserved different stories from ship households. Cultivators understood seasonal channels and local soils; maritime arrivals brought distant partners and vessel designs; proto-Ahdari settlers contributed forms of guest protection, restitution and defended water. Enslavement and warfare forced other people into the society without their consent. Some later obtained freedom and local kin; others remained excluded from property and civic honour.

The familiar phrase **Gateway people** described a regional culture more readily than an ancient endonym used in every household. Residents ordinarily named Qarzeth, Esharat, Sarqen, Belqara, Hazreth, Tammur, Oresh or Hadrath. Shared speech developed through markets and shipping, with marked town accents and words from the desert roads. Religious practice joined ancestors, springs, shore guardians and travelling powers without producing one compulsory pantheon outside Qarzeth's civic institutions.

The Gateway Republic made the region politically visible but did not give its inhabitants equal rights. A subject farmer, allied pilot and Qarzethi citizen could all belong to the same coastal culture while standing differently before the law. Opposition to the Bronze-Faced Provider arose within this world, particularly among threatened families and towns resisting collection parties.

**Identity ruling:** an ancient Braided regional people containing several civic and local communities. The Gateway Republic was one government within that history, not the people itself.

## PA-04 — The Qarzethi

**Qarzethi** primarily described civic attachment to Qarzeth. The capital contained citizens, resident foreigners, freed families, subject labourers and enslaved inhabitants. Later accounts often call them all Qarzethi because they lived or died in the city. Ancient rights distinguished them sharply.

Recognised citizens could participate in the assembly and seek office according to property, sex, household and status rules that changed over time. Council Houses exercised disproportionate influence through ships, land, credit and religious patronage. A person from a dependent town might acquire citizenship through marriage, military distinction, manumission or a grant while relatives at home remained subjects.

Qarzeth's harbour and dense working districts produced a strong urban culture. Storekeepers, shipwrights, sailors, cultivators, interpreters and sanctuary workers shared festivals and markets. Citizens could take pride in civic argument and naval achievement while benefiting from land, tribute and enslaved labour taken elsewhere. The sacrificial establishment belonged to this political world. Priests organised the killings, officials collected children and patrons protected the rites. Qarzethi families also hid children, fled with testimony and helped destroy the institution.

The sack of approximately 520 BR ended the city as a functioning civic body. Atherian troops murdered, raped and enslaved inhabitants and salted the central districts. Qarzethi identity survived through refugees, freed captives, displaced Council families, artisans and children raised in other towns. It ceased to confer citizenship in a living republic.

By 350 BR, a person calling a grandparent Qarzethi made a claim about origin, loss or former standing. Some used the name to seek confiscated property. Others concealed it because their household had served the collections. No descendant inherited guilt or civic privilege automatically.

**Identity ruling:** a destroyed civic population with a cultural and diasporic afterlife. Qarzethi must not be used as a synonym for every Gateway person.

## PA-05 — The Esharati

Esharat was an inland carrying and agricultural town whose people connected Gateway harbours with cultivated country and the Basin roads. **Esharati** was a local identity grounded in residence, maternal and paternal kin, fields, store space, pack animals and participation in the town's markets and rites.

Esharat's inheritance customs gave several women greater control of land and commercial shares than Qarzeth normally recognised. These customs were neither universal equality nor an isolated curiosity. They reflected the importance of women who kept estates and carrying agreements while other relatives travelled. Enslaved women and poor dependants remained exposed to owners and creditors despite the authority exercised by elite matrons.

The town paid dues to Qarzeth while retaining influential elders. Its carriers profited from the republic and resented collections which removed food, children or animals. Sapha's rescue network and Hesara's household gave Esharat an honoured place in opposition to the child sacrifices. Other Esharati supplied the capital, purchased enslaved labour after the sack or used the town's reputation for rescue to improve their own standing.

Marriage to Prince Tazirun Zaheret joined one Esharati household to junior Qahuri kin. It did not convert the town into a Qahuri colony. Hadrima inherited both relationships, and her descendants used them at Sarqen.

By 350 BR, Esharat remained a cultivated town with its own elders and carrying families under the Sarqeni crown. Esharati described a living local people, not merely a branch of the Nerethan genealogy.

**Identity ruling:** a durable Gateway town population with distinctive inheritance and transport traditions. Its Qahuri connection belongs to particular houses.

## PA-06 — The spared-town communities

The **Spared Towns** were a political and memorial category created by Qarzeth's destruction. Esharat, Sarqen, Belqara and Hazreth survived the planned razing after accepting different terms. They had not previously formed one ethnic community, and their survival did not give them one ancestry.

The first league coordinated seed, refugees, ransom and defence. People called themselves by town. *Spared* could express gratitude for rescue, shame at accommodation, pride in resistance or anger at an Atherian description which made continued existence appear a gift from the conqueror. Families dispossessed by occupying soldiers heard the word differently from officials whose offices survived.

Refugees altered every town. Qarzethi artisans entered repair districts. Freed captives searched for kin. Atherian veterans occupied confiscated fields. Children rescued from the collection rooms grew up in households whose names and languages differed from those of their parents. Some inhabitants accepted newcomers through marriage or apprenticeship; others guarded food and property against people they blamed for the war.

The league remained a practical association rather than a new people. Its commemorations nevertheless created shared memory. The reading of rescued, killed, enslaved and missing people joined communities whose governments remained separate. This common experience prepared cooperation under Sarqen without erasing local identities.

**Identity ruling:** a post-war coalition and memorial community, not an ethnicity. Its member towns remain separately named.

## PA-07 — Sarqeni royal identity

**Sarqeni** acquired a wider political meaning as Sarqen became the principal harbour of the surviving country. The town already possessed repair beaches, pilots and local households. Delkosi officers, Qarzethi refugees, Esharati carriers, Atherian soldiers and other Gateway families joined it after 520 BR.

Nerethan's command and marriage to Hadrima created a ruling household dependent on several constituencies. Delkosi soldiers required land and pay. Esharat wanted its property protected. Sarqen's established harbour families expected office. Caleran demanded access and ships. Their children and dependants developed a court culture using Gateway harbour forms, Atherian military practice, selected Qahuri ceremony and Delkosi titles.

Royal subjecthood did not make this population one descent group. A Sarqeni could be a long-established harbour resident, a person admitted from a spared town, a veteran settler or a descendant of refugees. Local citizenship and property continued to vary. The crown controlled garrisons, foreign policy and much port revenue while towns retained water, market and worship institutions.

By Samyra's generation, **Sarqeni** could mean a person of the capital, a subject of the kingdom or a participant in its mixed court culture. Those meanings must be specified. The kingdom possessed enough shared history to command loyalty, yet Esharati, Hazrethi and other local identities remained active.

**Identity ruling:** a new royal and civic identity formed among an older Braided regional people. It did not create a new ethnicity by decree and did not restore Qarzeth.

## PA-08 — The Hadrathi border identity

Hadrath was an allied border kingdom within the wider Gateway world. Its people shared coastal trade and some religious practice with Qarzeth while retaining a separate ruling house, agricultural country and military interests. **Hadrathi** therefore described regional and political belonging rather than a subordinate class of Qarzethi.

The kingdom's position along overland approaches made animals, grain and mounted service important. Its rulers entered marriages with Gateway commanders and negotiated service rather than accepting every civic command from the capital. Qarzeth used the language of alliance when Hadrath complied and dependence when its ruler resisted.

Household membership followed local kin, land and patronage. Foreign soldiers and spouses could enter the ruling and military population. Captives and enslaved workers also entered Hadrath without equal status. The marriage through which an earlier Hadrathi connection strengthened the Ordan military household demonstrates elite alliance, not a merger of the two countries.

Hadrath's exact political course after the final Gateway War remains less fully fixed than that of the four spared towns. This dossier establishes only what existing history requires: a distinct border population, participation in Gateway culture and a relationship with Qarzeth that never erased its local crown.

**Identity ruling:** a Gateway-related border people and kingdom. Its fuller internal history requires a regional article, not absorption into Qarzethi or Sarqeni identity.

|---|---|---|
| Qahuri | membership in the Sacred Basin's broad river civilisation | allegiance to one crown or later religion |
| Tenareti, Qemareti or Sakhreti | regional and civic belonging within Qahur | a separate human ancestry |
| Gateway person | participation in the coast's Braided culture | Qarzethi citizenship |
