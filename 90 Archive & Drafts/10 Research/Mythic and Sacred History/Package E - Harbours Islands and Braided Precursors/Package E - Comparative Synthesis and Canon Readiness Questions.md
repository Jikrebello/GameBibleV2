# Package E — Comparative Synthesis and Canon-Readiness Questions

> [!warning] Stop point
> This dossier completes research. It proposes no names, maps, peoples, wars or chronology events. Package E development begins only after the author chooses among the questions below.

## Authorial decision register

### Decision 1 — Five island environments within contested waters

**Locked during author review:** Package E will use five recurring island environments rather than reducing the Inner Sea to one archipelago or creating a separate analogue for every researched Mediterranean island.

1. a large multi-polity island whose coasts, interior and overseas relationships do not form one automatic state;
2. a near-mainland harbour island dependent upon, and politically entangled with, the opposite shore;
3. a scattered archipelago of specialised islands whose separate resources and communities encourage exchange without requiring unity;
4. a sacred or protected market island whose political importance exceeds its food and water supply;
5. low shoal-and-lagoon islands where pilots, fisheries, tidal routes and difficult approaches matter more than monumental fortification.

These types describe environments and political problems, not five predetermined peoples. Several communities, languages and legal systems may occupy one island type, while related households may be dispersed across several.

The island belt will also overlap with the colonies, claimed waters, supply routes and dependent ports of neighbouring mainland civilisations. Some islands lie close enough to other powers' settlements that fishing grounds, tribute, sanctuary, pilotage, timber, grain and naval access generate recurrent aggression and war. Control should remain layered: an empire might hold one harbour, tax one dynasty or claim the coast while lacking authority over the island interior.

“Sea peoples” may be used as a convenient outsider label for changing raiding fleets, migrants and island coalitions, but it does not name one ethnic nation or permanent confederacy.

### Decision 2 — Island heartlands and an outer contested ring

**Locked in principle during author review:** the five environmental types do not all function as exposed colonial frontiers. Package E requires a genuine island heartland in which maritime peoples developed their own settlements, kinship, cults, institutions and historical memory over long periods. These islands are homelands rather than strategic objects created by mainland attention.

Beyond and between those heartlands lies an outer ring of more exposed islands and near-mainland positions. These are the principal zones described by the contested large-island model: older island communities, foreign settler ports, dependent mainland colonies, imperial garrisons and mixed harbour populations hold overlapping claims. Control of one harbour does not make an outside power sovereign over the entire island.

The distinction is relational rather than a clean map circle. A sacred market or specialised resource island can belong culturally to the heartland while remaining politically vulnerable; a distant low island can become a colonial station; one coast of a large island can be incorporated into a foreign sea system while its interior and other ports remain independent. The wandering city moves between the two zones rather than defining either one.

### Decision 3 — A family of related island peoples

**Locked during author review:** the core islands are inhabited by several closely related maritime peoples rather than one unified island nation or a collection of wholly unrelated populations. Their languages or dialects are partly mutually intelligible, and long histories of marriage, fosterage, seasonal work, pilgrimage and ship service make common cultural ancestry recognisable.

Political and civic identity remains local. People normally identify first by island, harbour, interior district, lineage or dependent community. Related speech and custom do not create one crown, assembly, citizenship or uniform law. Island peoples may fight one another, exclude one another from land or office and invite outside allies into local conflicts.

Foreign observers often flatten these distinctions into a collective “sea people” name, especially during migration or war. Island traditions recognise a broader family resemblance but disagree over which communities belong, which preserve the oldest practices and whether shared ancestry creates any present obligation.

### Decision 4 — Sanctuary centre and kinship web

**Locked during author review:** the related island peoples are connected both through a shared sanctuary-market island and through decentralised relationships among households. The sanctuary provides recognised ground for pilgrimage, seasonal exchange, ransom, oath witnessing and negotiation under protected conduct. It does not legislate for the islands or possess automatic political neutrality.

Fosterage, marriage, adoption, seasonal crewing and reciprocal refuge bind the wider island world between gatherings. These relationships often matter more than formal diplomacy. A household may have kin and obligations in several hostile ports, allowing messages, captives and goods to cross a conflict without requiring peace between governments.

The sanctuary makes broader belonging visible, while the kinship web makes it durable. Neither creates one citizenship or permanent league. Outside powers can patronise the sanctuary, manipulate its officers or exploit island family divisions without thereby acquiring legitimate rule over the cultural family.

### Decision 5 — The Elmaric peoples and the Elmarai heartland

**Locked during author review:** **Elmaric peoples** is the broad cultural and linguistic family-name used for the related peoples of the core and connected islands. It does not identify a unified state, citizenship or single descent group.

The name is derived from the already established Elmarai Isles. **Elmarai** remains the proper name for one important heartland archipelago and its local inhabitants, institutions and dialects. Other Elmaric peoples will receive their own island, harbour or regional names during development. They are related to the Elmarai rather than interchangeable with them.

“Elmaric” may have begun as a scholarly, diplomatic or mainland classification based upon the best-known archipelago before some island writers adopted it for the wider family. Ordinary people can still identify primarily by their own island and dispute whether distant communities are truly Elmaric.

### Decision 6 — Orphaned island roots and early southern arrivals

**Locked during author review:** the deepest Elmaric populations descend from several local Orphaned island communities that survived the Great Unbinding in place. Their recovery of cisterns, terraces, fisheries, small craft, seasonal routes and inter-island kinship supplied the principal substrate from which the Elmaric language family and cultural world developed.

This substrate did not remain isolated for long. Folk communities moving north from the southern lands established themselves along the southernmost Inner Sea coast and nearby islands. Their migration preceded later national borders and did not make every settler part of one organised Folk colonisation. Coastal kindreds, boat families, refugees and marriage groups entered different relationships with local islanders.

Proto-Glass and proto-Atherian voyagers also reached the region during the wider southern dispersal. Some households landed and chose not to continue with the expeditions that eventually fed other Atherian settlement zones. They built permanent harbour quarters, joined or displaced local communities and retained memories of the Glass without becoming subjects of a later Atherian city by anticipation.

The Elmaric world was therefore locally rooted and already braided in its formative centuries. Older island practices remained structurally important, while southern Folk and halted Glass-descended settlers contributed ancestry, ships, rites, language and political conflict. No single migration created all Elmaric peoples, and later Atherian writers could not legitimately claim the island heartland merely because some inhabitants shared Glass ancestry.

### Decision 7 — Absorption through settlement and coerced incorporation

**Locked in principle during author review:** most early Folk and halted Glass-descended settlers were absorbed into Elmaric communities over several generations. Their descendants became members of particular island peoples rather than preserving three permanent population blocs. Regional differences nevertheless remained visible: southern coasts and selected outer ports retained stronger Folk-Elmaric or Glass-Elmaric ancestry, institutions and origin traditions than the central heartland.

Raiding and piracy contributed to this absorption from an early period. Elmaric crews captured people from other islands and from coastal cities around the Inner Sea. Captives included men, women and children used for labour, ransom, exchange and household incorporation, but captured women were disproportionately retained as enslaved concubines, coerced wives and reproductive dependants.

Repeated captive-taking brought ancestry, languages, recipes, cult practices, craft knowledge and foreign kinship memories into Elmaric households. This was not benign cosmopolitan mixture. Sexual violence, inability to refuse marriage, separation from relatives and unequal status formed part of the demographic history. Captive women and their descendants nevertheless transmitted knowledge, formed alliances, resisted owners and reshaped the households that claimed to absorb them.

Later island traditions could describe these women as foreign brides, rescued strangers or honoured mothers while concealing the conditions under which many arrived. Conversely, mainland enemies could reduce every mixed Elmaric lineage to pirate descent and ignore negotiated migration, refuge, trade and voluntary marriage.

Until a more central programme is explicitly selected, the accurate authorial description is **coerced reproductive incorporation**, not eugenics. The latter term would require institutions deliberately selecting reproductive partners or controlling descendants for supposed inherited qualities.

### Decision 8 — A regional spectrum of reproductive coercion

**Locked during author review:** customary redistribution was the most widespread Elmaric response to captives retained for household incorporation. Captains surrendered a recognised portion of captive women to island, harbour or lineage authorities. Those authorities allocated them according to household losses, labour needs, political patronage, lineage claims and perceived demographic need. The system varied by island and period rather than operating through one pan-Elmaric office.

Some communities left retention almost wholly to captains and individual households. In those places “personal choice” belonged principally to citizen captors and buyers; it did not make an enslaved or coerced woman's participation voluntary. Household negotiations, ransom, escape, manumission and occasional genuine marriage created different individual outcomes within an unequal system.

A smaller number of islands developed more explicit eugenic institutions. Their officials recorded ancestry, assigned or prohibited unions and selected captives for supposed inherited qualities such as health, stature, diving endurance, magical sensitivity or prestigious foreign descent. These beliefs were culturally and politically consequential without becoming authorially validated biological science. Records could be fraudulent, categories unstable and desired traits produced as much by training, nutrition and status as ancestry.

The differences generated conflict within the Elmaric family. One island might condemn another's assigned unions while continuing household concubinage of its own; sanctuary officers might refuse to recognise a coerced marriage yet facilitate captive redistribution; women and descendants could seek refuge in islands whose law offered better status. No later shared identity should erase these variations or their victims.

### Decision 9 — Children incorporated through the citizen household

**Locked during author review:** the dominant Elmaric rule treated a child acknowledged by a citizen household as a member of that household and of its island people. Such children could inherit property, learn protected rites, enter crews and eventually possess civic standing even when their captive mothers remained enslaved, concubinal or legally dependent.

Acknowledgement was therefore a major instrument of power. A citizen parent or household could incorporate a useful or favoured child while refusing others, selling them, leaving them in dependent status or denying the relationship. Disputes over paternity, household consent and legitimate union allowed elders and courts to decide which descendants counted as kin.

Some islands followed harsher maternal-status rules, while others required sanctuary or civic adjudication before recognising a child of captive origin. These remained variations around a dominant assimilative pattern. Rapid incorporation broadened Elmaric ancestry and enabled later families to celebrate foreign foremothers while obscuring those women's lack of freedom.

The rule did not make Elmaric identity purely biological. Household recognition, upbringing, language, island law and participation in work produced belonging. It nevertheless allowed male citizens and powerful houses to convert coercive access to foreign women into legitimate descendants, property alliances and claims of demographic strength.

### Decision 10 — The three-part Elmaric homeland

**Locked during author review:** three of the five selected island environments form the principal Elmaric homeland.

- The scattered specialised archipelago contains several island peoples whose resources and seasonal needs sustain dense interdependence without political unity. The Elmarai Isles belong within this heartland.
- The sanctuary-market island provides recognised gathering ground for pilgrimage, oath witnessing, ransom, exchange and protected negotiation.
- The low shoal-and-lagoon islands support Elmaric pilot, fishing and tidal communities whose expertise differs from the open-water and rocky-island traditions of the main archipelago.

The large multi-polity island and near-mainland harbour islands belong principally to the outer contested ring. Elmaric households, colonies or old quarters may exist there, but neither environment is automatically an Elmaric possession. Older non-Elmaric populations, mainland settlers and imperial garrisons can hold parts of the same island.

This produces an identifiable homeland without a bounded national territory. Cultural centrality follows kinship, sanctuary access and recognised routes rather than a line enclosing every Elmaric settlement.

### Decision 11 — Isolated Elmaric peoples of the large outer island

**Locked during author review:** the older populations of the large multi-polity island are not a newly invented unrelated ethnicity. They descend from local Orphaned survivors of the Giant collapse and belong within the wider Elmaric cultural-linguistic family.

The island's size and varied terrain made its development different from that of the central archipelago. Coastal ports, inland valleys, upland strongholds and distant ends of the island recovered with limited contact and formed distinct dialects, cults, property customs and political identities. Some communities participated regularly in the sanctuary and kinship networks; others treated “Elmaric” as a distant scholarly label or a claim used by coastal rivals.

Elmaric relationship therefore does not imply cultural sameness or ownership by the heartland. Archipelago captains could enslave, raid or colonise people whom later genealogists classified as kin. Mainland powers could ally with one island polity, occupy another port and describe the whole island as divided barbarians despite its related populations.

Foreign settlement creates an additional layer rather than beginning the island's history. Elmaric coastal colonies, proto-Atherian enclaves, Folk-linked communities and later imperial positions enter an island already divided among locally rooted Elmaric peoples.

### Decision 12 — Mixed near-mainland island communities from the earliest recovery

**Locked during author review:** the near-mainland harbour islands were neither culturally pure Elmaric possessions nor empty sites founded by later colonial states. Local island survivors and communities on the opposite shore resumed contact early after the Giant collapse through fishing, refuge, marriage, seasonal cultivation, exchange and violence.

Their societies developed across the channel. A household might hold a boat landing on the island, fields or relatives on the mainland and ritual obligations on both sides. Dialects, cults and legal customs consequently mixed before any expanding state imposed a colonial charter or garrison.

Later powers attempted to simplify this history. Mainland rulers called the islands natural dependencies of the facing coast; Elmaric leagues invoked sanctuary ties and island ancestry; settler elites treated their own fortified quarter as the beginning of civic life. None of those claims erased the earlier mixed communities.

These islands are therefore especially vulnerable to divided loyalty and civil conflict. Siege, blockade or imperial taxation can separate people from fields, wells, graves and kin visible across a narrow channel, while rival factions invite intervention from both island and mainland patrons.

### Decision 13 — Two connected colonial theatres

**Locked during author review:** the outer Elmaric world contains two connected but distinct theatres of colonial and naval competition.

In the Gateway-facing theatre, Elmaric communities and mixed island ports become entangled with the Gateway Republic, its Sarqeni successor, Atherian leagues and southern coastal peoples. The Gateway Wars, destruction of Qarzeth and creation of the queen's country alter patronage, refugee movement and ownership without converting every island conflict into an extension of Package A.

In the Delkosi–Vashari-facing theatre, island and near-mainland communities face Delkosi, Vashari and Atherian claims alongside the established politics of Tasar, Ilyessa, Avesh and the Arkenan–Saronikan cities. Garrisons, ship assessments, military settlers and competing protective leagues produce different pressures from those surrounding the Gateway.

Elmaric sanctuary, kinship and shipping networks carry news, captives, mercenaries, refugees and political arguments between the theatres. They do not create one coordinated Elmaric foreign policy. One island family can support an Atherian alliance in the Delkosi–Vashari conflict while relatives on Gateway-facing routes fight Atherian occupation, and both may appeal to the same sanctuary traditions.

### Decision 14 — Sermoran great-island zone and outer channel chains

**Locked during author review and corrected for map orientation:** the large multi-polity island and a neighbouring archipelago anchor the Sermoran and central-Sestaran maritime zone. Other outer routes are characterised more strongly by near-mainland islands, narrow channels and ports whose communities live across the water from competing coastal powers.

In present-day geography, Saronika remains a western-region power even though it occupies the eastern maritime edge of the Thalmyric Empire. Sermora lies farther east of Thalmyria, only slightly west of the Concord's centre on the Sestaran island zone, immediately west of the neighbouring archipelago, and west or north-west of most Eclessian territory lying below it. The older island history therefore underlies a later imperial and confessional fault line without making the ancient inhabitants proto-Thalmyrian or proto-Eclessian.

The Sermoran great-island zone can sustain several Elmaric polities, interior districts and foreign colonial ports. Its nearby archipelago supplies smaller harbours, pilot communities, refuges and contested stepping stones. This is the stronger setting for layered Gateway, Sarqeni and Atherian involvement and for the ancient populations that later contribute differently to Sestaran cities.

The outer channel chains connect the Elmaric world to Arkenan–Saronikan, Tasarian, Ilyessan and Aveshi routes. Delkosi, Vashari and Atherian powers can hold garrisons or client ports there while remaining dependent upon local pilots, opposite-shore food and divided island households.

### Decision 15 — Sermoran inheritance of the great-island zone

**Locked during author review and checked against the Sestaran canon register:** the great island and its neighbouring archipelago form the principal ancient substrate of modern Sermora. This does not make every ancient Elmaric community Sermoran by anticipation or place the other Sestaran cities upon the island.

Modern Sestara contains three important maritime members with different histories. Sermora is the old central-island naval republic whose fleets, pilotage and privateering dominate the Thalassian role. Orsena is the much smaller eastern free port, founded by Atheria as a customs and naval station and later specialised in neutral transshipment, repair, storage and prisoner exchange. Castara is the distant Boiling Sea member admitted only in 1332; its shipyards, exploration economy and Ash Road relationship with Tarkhan country belong to a much later geography.

Package E can therefore give Sermora the deepest Elmaric island inheritance. The eastern near-mainland communities can supply older populations and route knowledge around the later Orsenan site without contradicting the canon statement that Atheria founded Orsena as a city and station. Castara remains outside this ancient Inner Sea island complex.

### Decision 16 — Shared Elmaric ancestry, separate Sermoran and Saronikan futures

**Locked during author review:** modern Sermora and Saronika developed from different regional branches of the older Elmaric maritime world.

Sermora is the central Sestaran-side heir. Its great-island and archipelago communities eventually produced the canal capital, pilot villages, merchant houses and naval republic now belonging to the **Sestaran Concord**. It lies east of Thalmyria and west of the adjacent archipelago and most of Eclessia. Its mature Doge, Admiralty and ship-share oligarchy are later institutions, not the unchanged government of ancient Elmaric society.

Saronika is the Thalmyrian-side heir. Although it occupies the eastern maritime edge of the Thalmyric Empire, it remains westward in the broader regional geography. Naverion, Erenost, the Elmarai Isles and associated channel communities entered Atherian and later Thalmyric political histories through different conquests, naval settlements and royal institutions. Modern Saronika is a constituent maritime kingdom of the **Thalmyric Empire**, not a Concord member. Its queen, court, Admiralty and island councils supply the setting's closest Naples-like royal maritime counterpart to Venice-like Sermora.

The shared ancestry does not create a modern territorial claim or unified nationality. Atherian administration, religious change, occupation, Reconquest, city law and separate post-imperial state formation made Sermoran and Saronikan identities real and distinct. Older linguistic, ritual and kinship traces can nevertheless explain mutual familiarity, disputed sanctuary claims and the recurring belief that each society preserves the truer Elmaric inheritance.

Castara remains a later detached Boiling Sea member of the Concord and does not descend directly from this Inner Sea division. Orsena is a Concord port whose city was founded by Atheria; older mixed communities around its site may connect to eastern Elmaric routes without turning Orsena into an ancient Elmaric capital.

### Decision 17 — Recognisable Saronikan-side and Sermoran-side branches by 350 BR

**Locked during author review:** by the 350 BR boundary, the Elmaric communities ancestral to the later Saronikan and Sermoran zones form recognisable regional branches. Differences in route orientation, neighbouring languages, colonial pressure, ecology and political alliances have produced related dialect groups and contrasting customs.

The distinction remains a cultural gradient rather than two nations. Sanctuary rights, fosterage, marriage, ransom obligations and partially intelligible speech continue across it. Individual islands may resemble a community on the other side more closely than their immediate neighbours, and no regional council speaks for an entire branch.

Neither branch is yet modern Sermora or Saronika. The later republic and kingdom depend upon Atherian rule, religious change, occupation, Reconquest and post-imperial state formation. Ancient differences make those outcomes possible without predetermining them.

### Decision 18 — Ancient political tendencies reverse their modern outcomes

**Locked during author review:** by 350 BR, dynastic government is more characteristic of the Elmaric polities ancestral to the Sermoran zone. Great-island kings, harbour dynasties and competing courts use marriage, captive incorporation, fleet patronage and sanctuary claims to govern separate districts. Councils and captain coalitions exist but do not yet define the region as a republic.

The Saronikan-side Elmaric ports more often preserve civic councils, ship-owning associations and negotiated leagues. Their proximity to larger states also produces client councils, foreign garrisons and oligarchic collaboration; the pattern is not democratic equality. These institutions later lose or share power under Atherian administration, occupation governments, naval settlement and Thalmyric royal consolidation.

The later outcomes therefore reverse the ancient tendency. Sermora's merchant republic grows from the defeat, absorption or constitutional containment of old dynastic powers. Saronika's kingdom unifies and overlays ports whose older political traditions were frequently civic and locally autonomous.

In the present day, most inhabitants of Saronika identify as Thalmyric first or treat Saronikan identity as a Thalmyric regional identity. The older Elmaric-descended populations have largely been integrated through language, law, religion, military service, intermarriage and urban life. Residual island or pilot communities may preserve stronger Elmaric self-identification, but they do not constitute an untouched “pure” population or a majority nation awaiting restoration.

### Decision 19 — Displacement before empire, aggressive settlement during expansion, institutional absorption afterward

**Locked during author review:** Saronikan integration occurred in three broad phases.

Before Atherian imperial expansion, incoming Glass-descended and Atherian-speaking households produced slow, uneven demographic displacement. Merchant quarters, hired garrisons, military colonies and ship-owning families acquired harbour land, fields and civic influence. Some settlements remained negotiated and mixed; elsewhere debt, marriage pressure, enslavement and local war transferred property away from Elmaric households over generations.

During Atherian expansion and aggression, conquest and demographic replacement intensified. Garrisons seized strategic islands and port districts, veteran and naval settlements received confiscated land, resistant households were killed, expelled or enslaved and politically favoured newcomers gained access to water, walls and harbour office. This did not remove every Elmaric population, but it broke continuity in selected cities and reduced older councils to dependent or ceremonial roles.

Once imperial rule stabilised, gradual institutional absorption became more important than repeated replacement. Atherian law, service, language, schools, markets, military careers and intermarriage offered practical routes into the imperial order while penalising communities unable or unwilling to enter it. Elmaric ancestry persisted inside populations whose public identity became Atherian and later Thalmyric.

The later spread of the Redeemer's faith deepened this process through congregations, parish life, saints, burial, marriage law, education and shared sacred history. Conversion did not make conquest benevolent or eliminate local practice immediately. It supplied a durable common identity through which descendants of settlers and subordinated Elmarics could eventually understand themselves as one Thalmyric Saronikan population.

The transition from aggressive settlement to institutional integration crosses beyond the 350 BR stopping point. Package E may establish the land seizures, garrisons and population fractures visible by that boundary; later Atherian and Conversion histories control the completed assimilation.

### Decision 20 — Elmarai centre and dispersed Saronikan survivals

**Locked during author review:** the Elmarai Isles preserve the strongest recognisable Elmaric identity within modern Saronika. Local dialects, family histories, pilot institutions, island councils and selected ritual customs remain more visible there than in the kingdom's heavily integrated mainland-facing ports.

Smaller survivals remain dispersed through fishing villages, pilot houses and coastal communities elsewhere in Saronika. These populations do not form one organised minority, and many members identify simultaneously as Elmarai or locally Elmaric, Saronikan and Thalmyric. Practice, occupation and household memory may remain Elmaric even where everyday public language and political loyalty are Thalmyric.

The Elmarai centre is neither culturally frozen nor biologically pure. It experienced the same histories of migration, conquest, service, conversion and intermarriage in different proportions. Its significance lies in institutional and linguistic continuity, not immunity from history.

### Decision 21 — Elmaric majority and contested foundation in the Sermoran zone

**Locked during author review:** Elmaric peoples remain the most populous cultural and linguistic population in the Sermoran great-island and archipelago zone at 350 BR. Southern-descended, Glass-descended and other immigrant communities are important but do not demographically replace the island majority during this period.

Influence is less equal than population. Settler-descended shipowners, fortified harbour households, military patrons and foreign commercial partners can control valuable quays, grain stores or commands disproportionate to their numbers. Elmaric dynasties, pilots, cultivators, crews, captives and town populations likewise possess sharply unequal status among themselves.

This demographic reality later underlies Sermora's contested foundation. Older pilot and shore houses claim continuity from the Elmaric island communities that supplied the harbour, opened routes and defeated coercive fleets. Southern-descended Houses of the First Mooring emphasise the landing, stockade, ship base and fortified settlement from which they derive civic precedence. Both contributed to later Sermora, but neither can plausibly claim to have created an empty city-world.

Modern Elmaric identity in Sermora consequently remains politically contested rather than marginal. Popular island practice and old-house genealogy preserve it, while competing patrician narratives select different moments as the republic's true beginning.

### Decision 22 — The Harbour Founded Twice as a Sermoran predecessor

**Locked during author review:** the harbour remembered in [[G-58 - The Harbour Founded Twice]] is one major predecessor community later absorbed into the growing Sermoran capital. It is not the origin of the whole Elmaric world, every settlement on the great island or the complete Sermoran republic.

Its two foundations remain historically consequential. Settler-descended houses date the community from the damaged convoy's landing, seasonal quarter, stockade and seizure of the grain store, repair yard and shore. Older Elmaric shore houses and their allies date it from the later defeat of the punitive fleet by returned exiles, escaped captives, local fighters, settler defectors and pilots who knew the channels. Neither memory is wholly invented, but each chooses the event that best legitimises its descendants.

As the later capital incorporates this harbour, the dispute becomes a durable part of Sermoran civic identity. Rival calendars, launch observances, family precedence and restrictions on who may lead each commemoration preserve the double foundation inside the larger city. The tradition supplies one important strand of Sermora's ancestry without collapsing the republic's many other Elmaric towns, islands, migrations and institutions into a single foundation legend.

### Decision 23 — Grain dependence and the politics of supply

**Locked during author review:** dependable grain is the Elmaric heartland's principal strategic dependency. The core islands support fishing, orchards, vines, grazing and specialised produce, but their soils and available acreage cannot reliably feed the dense harbour and island population in every season. Grain-producing outer islands and opposite-shore lowlands therefore become essential parts of the maritime system without necessarily belonging politically to the core peoples.

This dependence supplies direct causes for convoy organisation, granary law, fortified storehouses, negotiated foreign quarters, seasonal debt and colonial aggression. Control of a modest harbour can determine whether grain reaches several larger islands. Crop failure, blockade or the loss of a protected channel can turn commercial competition into famine, regime crisis, raiding or war.

Ship timber remains an important secondary pressure. Elmaric communities possess sailors, pilots, yards and maritime skill, but larger hulls, masts, pitch and replacement oars often depend upon mainland forests or wooded outer islands. Grain determines immediate survival; timber determines whether the fleets that protect that survival can be maintained.

### Decision 24 — The sanctuary's grain and convoy court

**Locked during author review:** the shared Elmaric sanctuary-market develops a trusted court for grain measures, supply agreements and convoy disputes. Its officials certify weights and cargo records, witness treaties, arbitrate losses or damaged shipments and proclaim safe conduct during famine. This practical jurisdiction grows from the heartland's dependence on imported grain and the need for rival harbours to trust transactions across political boundaries.

The sanctuary's authority depends on recognition, custom, reputation and the willingness of participants to honour its rulings. It cannot tax all Elmaric islands, seize their stores, appoint their captains or command a common fleet. Rulers, harbour houses and captains may evade or contest its judgments, especially in war. The court links separate polities without creating a federal state or making the sanctuary politically neutral.

### Decision 25 — A mixed grain-supply system

**Locked during author review:** by 350 BR the core Elmaric islands secure grain through a regional mix of treaty trade and contested farming colonies, with the balance differing from island to island and changing over time. Some harbour communities depend chiefly on purchases and supply agreements with neighbouring coastal powers; others maintain fortified fields, farm settlements or protected grain ports on outer islands and opposite shores. A single Elmaric grain empire or uniform colonial policy does not exist.

The mixture creates both cooperation and conflict. Treaty partners can withhold grain or demand political concessions, while colonial holdings bring Elmaric claimants into disputes with prior inhabitants, neighbouring states and rival Elmaric harbours. Grain routes therefore connect the sanctuary court's commercial work to the politics of land, garrisons, convoy protection and famine.

### Decision 26 — Mixed labour and tenure in outer grain holdings

**Locked during author review:** no single class farms every Elmaric-held outer grain territory. Some pre-existing local households continue cultivating under treaties, rent or tribute; some Elmaric settler households farm land acquired by grant or seizure; and some estates, fortified holdings and harvest operations exploit enslaved captives. The balance varies by island, harbour patron, date and the violence of its occupation.

These categories can coexist within one district and need not be equally secure. A nominal treaty may conceal coercion; a settler family may become a debtor; a captive may be absorbed into a household without becoming free. Grain production is therefore a political and labour history, not a neutral resource label. This decision does not establish a uniform plantation system, a single ethnic caste or a biological hierarchy.

### Decision 27 — A plural Elmaric sanctuary

**Locked during author review:** by 350 BR the shared sanctuary-market is a sacred precinct used by several Elmaric traditions rather than a temple of one universally agreed patron. Its common obligations concern witnessed oaths, protected passage, hospitality, ransom and the handling of grain and convoy agreements. Custodians may maintain distinct shrines, ritual forms and accounts of the precinct's origin while recognising one another's right to meet and transact there.

Thalassor's followers may be present and influential, but the sanctuary does not make Thalassor the sole source of Elmaric sacred law or identify every older island power with him. Ancestral and household observances remain local. Shared practice permits cooperation without requiring theological uniformity, and disputes over which rites deserve space or precedence can still carry political weight.

### Decision 28 — Independent sanctuary island under pressure

**Locked during author review:** by 350 BR the sanctuary island governs itself through its resident community and custodial institutions. Its harbour rules, sacred precinct and grain-and-convoy court are not offices appointed by one neighbouring dynasty or a standing council of all Elmaric polities. This limited independence is one reason rivals can meet there without conceding jurisdiction to an opponent.

Independence does not mean equal power or immunity from intervention. The sanctuary needs imported grain and safe access by sea; larger harbours can influence its custodians through supply contracts, convoy protection, donations, debt, diplomatic pressure or threats to the approaches. Its autonomy is maintained through negotiated privileges, reputation and the costs other islands would incur by openly violating its safe conduct—not through a common Elmaric state or an unbeatable sacred fleet.

### Decision 29 — Captive refuge, ransom and unequal hearings

**Locked during author review:** an escaped captive who reaches the sanctuary gains protection from immediate violence or forcible seizure within the recognised precinct, but arrival does not ordinarily annul an owner's claim or make the person free. Custodians normally arrange return, exchange or ransom under the sanctuary's safe-conduct rules.

A formal hearing becomes more likely when a captive is considered valuable enough to attract a substantial ransom, a competing claimant, political leverage, specialised skills or a powerful sponsor. Such a hearing may examine ownership, captivity terms, debt or a possible route to release, but it is not an equal entitlement for every captive and does not promise freedom. This hierarchy exposes the sanctuary's complicity in the wider captive economy even as its rules prevent some immediate abuses. The measure of a captive's market or political value is a feature of the society's injustice, not an authorial measure of human worth.

### Decision 30 — Raiding as a maritime livelihood

**Locked during author review:** for some Elmaric crews, seaborne raiding is an established occupation rather than an exceptional lapse from ordinary trade or a status created by a ruler's warrant. Specialist captains and crews know how to find targets, cross difficult waters, land quickly, handle captives and cargo and secure buyers or ransom brokers. The same people may also trade, escort ships, serve in wars or pilot legitimate convoys as opportunity and patronage change. Not every Elmaric sailor is a raider, and raiding remains dangerous work with victims and coercive markets, not a romantic cultural essence.

Claims of lawful reprisal, breached debt, prior attack, harbour authorisation or enemy status coexist and are contested. A sponsoring city or house may honour a raid as defence or war while a victim calls it piracy; an allied court may later disown crews once their violence becomes inconvenient. These claims affect protection and punishment, but the professional raiding culture predates and exceeds any single legal category. This is a functional comparison with Viking-age raiding, not a claim that Elmaric institutions or chronology replicate the historical Norse world.

### Decision 31 — Independent share crews as the raiding baseline

**Locked during author review:** independent, captain-led share crews are the most characteristic Elmaric raiding organisation by 350 BR. Captains assemble vessels and specialist sailors for a venture, with agreed shares of proceeds and obligations for provisions, losses and captives. Crew membership, investment and destinations can change between voyages; this makes raiding a transferable skill and livelihood rather than a permanent state office.

Dynastic warbands and merchant-financed crews also exist. Island rulers may supply ships or retain experienced captains during a war, while harbour houses may finance raids, buy captured goods or keep deniable distance from a venture. These alternatives do not replace the independent share-crew baseline. Their coexistence allows the same professional sailors to pass through commerce, escort, mercenary service, licensed warfare and unlicensed seizure without making those activities legally identical.

### Decision 32 — Divided Elmaric service in the Gateway Wars

**Locked during author review:** the Gateway Wars, approximately 650–520 BR, divide Elmaric captains, ports and island households. Some supply Caleran with ships, pilots, escorts, repair work or fighting crews; others contract with the Gateway Republic or its allies, and some independent ventures use the wartime disruption without durable allegiance. There is no united Elmaric decision for either side and no single regional fleet.

The division can place related captains or crews on opposing sides, creating disputes over ransom, captured cargo, debts and betrayal that outlast the war. Contracted service does not make every sailor a professional raider, nor does shared ancestry settle political loyalty. This research decision supplements the established account of Arkenan and Saronikan contributions to Caleran's maritime war effort without making all those communities Elmaric or rewriting their existing relationships.

### Decision 33 — Selective postwar amnesty and suppression

**Locked during author review:** after Caleran's victory in the Gateway Wars and the fall of Qarzeth in 520 BR, its authorities treat Elmaric crews that served opponents selectively in ports and waters where the republic has effective leverage. Captains with useful pilotage, strong patrons, ships or negotiable loyalties may receive amnesty, contracts or a recognised place in the new order. Less protected crews can be outlawed, pursued or labelled pirates for similar wartime actions.

This is not a universal Caleran decree applied to every Elmaric island. Local rulers, sanctuary intermediaries and rival states can shelter or employ people whom Caleran condemns. The uneven policy leaves former auxiliaries seeking new employers and makes anti-piracy simultaneously a protection for commerce and a means of deciding which captains may use force under recognised authority.

### Decision 34 — Caleran outer footholds, independent Elmaric heartland

**Locked during author review:** by 350 BR Caleran directly holds selected outer ports, strategic islands or comparable maritime footholds in the Elmaric-connected seas. These positions give the republic practical leverage over grain routes, naval repair, customs and the pursuit of crews it labels pirates. Its influence also travels through client relationships and contracts beyond the places it governs.

The core Elmaric heartland, including the principal Sermoran-side island and archipelago communities, remains politically independent. Shared ancestry does not make Caleran-held Elmaric populations the subjects of a single Elmaric government, and one occupied outer harbour does not imply conquest of the neighbouring islands. Later Atherian imperial campaigns and institutional absorption must remain distinguishable from this pre-imperial pattern of scattered republican footholds.

### Decision 35 — Caleran's stronger Gateway-facing presence

**Locked during author review:** Caleran's direct maritime footholds are strongest on the Gateway-facing approaches by 350 BR, reflecting the Gateway Wars, the destruction of Qarzeth and the republic's subsequent position in Sarqen. It can hold selected outer ports or strategic islands and apply naval, customs and diplomatic pressure along these routes without annexing the Elmaric core.

In the Delkosi–Vashari-facing channels, Caleran's relationships are generally thinner: treaties, ship contracts, allied harbours and isolated stations compete with Delkosi, Vashari and other regional claims. This does not assign every eastern island to another power or forbid exceptional Caleran positions there. It establishes an asymmetry between two connected theatres while preserving their locally varied politics.

### Decision 36 — Rival blocs within a politically separate heartland

**Locked during author review:** by 350 BR several rival maritime leagues and dynastic blocs operate among the independent Elmaric heartland polities, but they do not gather every island into durable membership. Most cities, island kingdoms and harbour communities retain their own governments, laws and interests. Alliances can be limited to a convoy season, grain emergency, war or disputed channel and may shift as rulers, marriages and supply needs change.

The blocs matter because they can pool ships, protect a route, bargain with Caleran or contest influence over the sanctuary; they are not a single Elmaric state or a permanent federal navy. A dynastic house may dominate one coalition while a neighbouring city remains outside it or joins only for a particular campaign. This combines regional organisation with the persistent autonomy and rivalry of separate island polities.

### Decision 37 — Unequal raider access to the sanctuary harbour

**Locked during author review:** the sanctuary's stated rule allows raider-captains conditional entry for ransom, arbitration and regulated exchange. They may not launch attacks from its harbour or strike travellers under its recognised safe conduct. Entry into the sacred precinct can require disarmament and submission to custodial rules.

In practice, access depends strongly on a captain's clout. Influential raiders with ships, patrons, money, hostages or leverage over supply can bargain for wider harbour trading privileges and more favourable terms. Lesser crews may be admitted only to settle a claim or arrange ransom under tight restrictions. Custodians defend the boundary but cannot enforce it evenly against every armed visitor. The resulting inequity is part of the sanctuary's political history, not proof that raiding and sanctuary law are the same institution.

### Decision 38 — Vorthara's continuously inhabited refuge landscape

**Locked during author review:** the Black Spire and its approaches have had some form of habitation for as long as the known history of the place reaches. The settlement story does not begin with post-Unbinding resupply, an Atherian outpost or the later concentration of magical schools. During the Giant age, the Spire's remoteness and the Giant powers' consistent avoidance of it made the surrounding ridges a destination for escaped slaves and other people trying to live beyond imperial reach. Their refuges, paths, food plots and gathering places became an old inhabited landscape, even though the population, buildings and institutions changed over time.

Vorthara's reputation as a place for strangers and outcasts therefore has deep roots rather than being a recent branding or a single founding event. The earliest refuge communities need not have possessed the present city's wards, Truce, Shell industry or faction structure. Giant avoidance is a fact of the remembered historical pattern, but it does not identify the Black Spire's builders or original function. The question of how successive communities obtained food and materials remains important, but it is secondary to this older continuity of habitation.

### Decision 39 — Hidden Spire refuge expanding into ridge communities

**Locked during author review:** the earliest known fugitive habitation begins as one hidden settlement within accessible parts of, or immediately against, the Black Spire. The location offers concealment and a recognisable destination for escapees without establishing who made the structure, why it exists or how much of its interior was then accessible.

As arrivals accumulate, habitation spreads into scattered shelters and hamlets across the ridges. These communities share paths, water, warnings, exchange and stories of the original refuge, but do not become one continuously governed town. Expansion may already begin during the Giant age and continue through the Unbinding; exact dates and the degree of continuity at each site remain open. This sequence precedes the city's later ward powers, Spire Truce, Atherian overlay and Shell industries.

### Decision 40 — Giant-selective emanation at the Black Spire

**Accepted authorial direction, with mechanics still open:** the Black Spire has an uncanny, radiation-like magical emanation that ordinary humans can inhabit without the harm it causes Giant beings and people bearing Giant inheritance. This selective danger offers a concrete reason Giant powers avoided the place and why escaped human slaves could shelter there. "Radiation" is a descriptive analogy, not yet a claim about nuclear physics, a named energy system or the purpose for which the Spire was made.

The effect's radius, symptoms, strength, persistence into later ages and response to different kinds or degrees of Giant inheritance remain undecided. Its observed selectivity does not reveal the Spire's builders or original function, and it should not automatically be identified with the later Shell anchors or every form of Black Spire leakage. A Giant-blooded human remains human and retains a human soul under established cosmology, even if the emanation affects that person's body or inherited traits.

### Decision 41 — Persistent but diminished Spire effect; population scope open

**Locked during author review:** the Giant-selective emanation persists into the present, but it is weaker or more localised than in the Giant age, with its strongest effects close to the Black Spire. The ancient refuge explanation therefore need not imply that every modern Vortharan ward or approach is closed to everyone with distant Giant ancestry.

**Population question not yet locked:** the author envisages overt Giant-blooded people as extremely uncommon in the present and suspects that by 350 BR most such inheritance among ordinary people was weak, with stronger survivors hidden or elsewhere. Existing canon does not support treating Giant descent as wholly extinct: some Folk and Ättar lines inherited irregular bodily or magical traits from lesser Giants and giant-blooded refugees, rare true Giant births remained possible, and other remnant histories exist. None of these sources establishes a population count or requires a concentrated Giant-blooded people near Vorthara. The Spire's response to weak inheritance still needs a rule.

Vampires are a separate special case. Their human souls are held by an Ansharan Giant-derived blood lien; Pride strengthened the original system but did not create their human bloodlines as a simple Idol gift. Whether the Spire reacts to that lien is not settled by its effect on biological Giant descent and should not be assumed in this decision.

### Decision 42 — Graded response to active Giant inheritance

**Locked during author review:** the Black Spire's selective emanation produces a graded response rather than an all-or-nothing reaction to any genealogical trace. Genuine Giants are harmed severely by close approach. Humans with strongly expressed Giant-derived bodily or magical inheritance can suffer substantially, while people with faint expression may notice only discomfort near the Spire. Ordinary humans without active Giant inheritance are unaffected by this selective aspect.

Intensity follows the relevant inherited expression and exposure, not a precise ancestry percentage, political pedigree, moral standing or soul category. The emanation cannot be used as an infallible test of descent or to classify a human soul as partly Giant. Its diminished present-day range makes obvious incidents uncommon, especially if strongly expressed Giant inheritance is rare in the region. Vampire blood liens remain a separate unresolved case.

### Decision 43 — Vampire blood liens do not trigger the Spire effect

**Locked during author review:** vampires do not have a special reaction to the Black Spire's Giant-selective emanation merely because their condition rests on an Ansharan Giant-derived blood lien. That lien is a distinct predatory blood craft holding a human soul out of its ordinary route, not the same thing as active inherited Giant bodily or magical traits. The Spire is therefore neither a dependable vampire detector nor a new universal vampire weakness or soul-release mechanism.

An individual vampire could still be affected by an independently inherited Giant trait or by a separate hazard within Vorthara, but vampirism alone does not trigger this particular effect. This ruling preserves the existing Ansharan and Pride distinction without implying that vampires are ordinary living descendants of Giant dynasties.

### Decision 44 — Vorthara as an informal occult refuge town by 350 BR

**Locked during author review:** by 350 BR Vorthara is a substantial refuge town and pass market around the Black Spire, linked to its older ridge hamlets. It already has a reputation for strange and powerful magic. Warlocks, summoners, conjurers, necromancers and other capable or feared practitioners arrive from multiple distant civilisations, often because they seek to escape rulers, laws, rivals or social constraints elsewhere.

The concentration is largely personal and informal, not yet the **modern system** of named schools, guilds and rival institutional wards. Individual practitioners may attract apprentices, household servants, guards, patrons and clients, and they may feud or cooperate; no unified magical academy, city council or townwide faction constitution governs them. **Later clarification in Decision 56:** the Pale Hand is an early exception, already a leading organised death-working faction by 350 BR, without governing other magical professions or Vorthara itself. The reputation predates Atherian administration without implying that every outsider is a criminal or that every practitioner uses the same kind of magic. Decision 45 distinguishes the Spire's bounded anomalies from the stronger social attraction of refuge and accumulated expertise.

### Decision 45 — Bounded Spire anomalies and the stronger draw of refuge

**Locked during author review:** unusual and poorly understood effects of the Black Spire give some specialists real reasons to study or work nearby, but it is not a universal amplifier that simply makes every spell stronger. Different practitioners may seek different anomalies, materials, observations or experimental conditions without discovering the Spire's original purpose.

The larger and more durable attraction is social and political. Vorthara is remote, has long sheltered strangers and offers room to practice forms of magic constrained or condemned elsewhere. Its accumulated practitioners, assistants, clients and informal knowledge exchange make it a destination in its own right. Reputation, not a worldwide formal school network, carries news of the refuge between civilisations.

### Decision 46 — Nominal Atherian affiliation at the 350 BR boundary

**Accepted authorial direction, with treaty mechanics open:** as Thalion's imperial government emerges around 350 BR, it claims the land and routes around Vorthara and recognises the refuge town within its external sphere. Vorthara nominally affiliates with or joins the imperial order and sells magical and specialist services, while governing its inner settlement and practitioner households itself. The developing empire has reasons to secure Crown Gate Pass and access useful expertise, but little conventional farmland or easily collected tax revenue compensates for a costly assault on a remote town of formidable mages. Decisions 62–63 sharpen this into an initial market-and-surveillance policy with inner nonintervention, followed by an exclusive imperial services clause **later during expansion**, not at the 350 BR boundary.

This is a boundary and early-imperial development, not an earlier act by a fully formed empire. It fits the established later pattern in which Atheria builds roads, quarantine forts, laboratories and inspection systems around the Black Spire but never fully governs the inner city. The town's autonomy is practical and negotiated, not proof that the empire has no strategic interest in the pass or that Vorthara already possesses its **full modern array** of ward factions and the Spire Truce; the Pale Hand alone is now selected as an early named death-working faction (Decision 56).

### Decision 47 — Temporary compact with an echo in the Concord settlement

**Locked during author review:** when the emerging Atherian Empire seeks an arrangement around 350 BR, a temporary gathering of refuge households, pass keepers and influential practitioners speaks for Vorthara on that limited external matter. Its participants agree to terms concerning the surrounding roads, access and services, then disperse rather than becoming a permanent city council, hereditary magistracy or general government over every household.

The arrangement establishes a recognisable Vortharan political habit: people who refuse common domestic rule can still assemble for a bounded external bargain. That habit echoes Vorthara's much later accession to the Sestaran defensive compact in 909 AR, when it added its seal while rejecting domestic oversight. The two agreements are not one unchanged charter, and the early participants do not thereby become a city government or ancestral Wardens. An early Pale Hand representative may join the temporary bargaining without speaking for all Vortharan magi. The precedent is a method of limited cooperation, not an uninterrupted civic institution.

### Decision 48 — No extradition as the refuge's general rule

**Locked during author review:** Vorthara's arrangement with Atheria grants no general right to extradite a fugitive from the inner settlement. Giving imperial officers one recognised right to take an unwanted person would create a precedent for residents and factions to sell one another out, undermining the town's reason for being a place to disappear. The default is therefore refusal, not a standing procedure of case-by-case surrender.

An imperial officer can still enter or attempt an unauthorised seizure. The agreement does not make that person safe inside Vorthara: the target, neighbours or powerful practitioners may resist, and others may join for solidarity, fear of precedent or their own interests. Nor is rescue guaranteed to every fugitive; there is no citywide police or automatic common defence. Imperial authorities may decide that dangerous exiles are less harmful when they remain secluded at the Spire, while policing the roads and outside territory they claim. This practical tolerance coexists with occasional attempts, threats and failed raids without turning any of them into lawful extradition.

### Decision 49 — Anarchic refuge with limited shared custom

**Locked during author review:** by 350 BR Vorthara is anarchic in the literal political sense: it has no ruler, council, citywide magistracy or comprehensive law. The refusal to hand inhabitants over to outside powers is its strongest common expectation. Practical customs around shared water, shelter and usable paths also exist, but they arise from neighbourhood need and do not amount to a formal townwide code or the later Spire Truce.

Enforcement is decentralised and contingent. Refuge households, neighbours, armed followers or individual practitioners may join to resist an intrusion, defend access or retaliate after damage; others may remain indifferent or exploit the dispute. No one can confidently promise that a guest will be rescued or that a dangerous resident will be restrained. The town survives through repeated bargains, mutual deterrence, personal ties and the fact that destroying shared necessities harms rival occupants alike, not through a hidden government.

### Decision 50 — District-based freedom and captivity

**Locked during author review:** an outside owner's claim has no recognised force inside Vorthara, but arrival does not guarantee lasting freedom. A fugitive can become bound by a new pact, coerced patronage, debt, kidnapping or local enslavement; the absence of extradition is not a citywide ban on internal bondage. The consequences of entering a particular neighbourhood depend heavily on whose protection, labour demands and rules operate there.

This makes Vorthara sharply district-based. Some adjoining refuge clusters shelter fugitives and actively oppose slave-taking; others profit from captives or coerced service. They may fight, bargain, buy people out or retaliate across a street without either side speaking for the whole city. At 350 BR most such clusters are households, followings and neighbourhood practices rather than the **full set** of named modern factions; the Pale Hand's death-working quarter is the selected early exception. Later districts can preserve or reshape those conflicts without copying Kaer Maga's exact wards or Freemen.

The comparison is supported by Paizo's classic Kaer Maga material: the abolitionist Bottoms and the city's slave markets coexist under local rather than universal civic control. Vorthara borrows the structural tension of neighbouring incompatible jurisdictions, not Pathfinder's faction names or a claim that slavery is morally neutral.

### Decision 51 — Local food, imported grain and cultivated reagents

**Locked during author review:** the growing refuge town draws on both ridge hamlets, terraces, gardens and herds and necessary food imports through neighbouring valleys and pass routes. Local production sustains a baseline and provides resilience, but it cannot wholly feed the larger concentration of practitioners, clients, servants and craftspeople. The routes bringing grain remain a real vulnerability even where imperial commanders fear the mages within.

Ridge growers also cultivate valuable magical herbs and plants. These are medicinal, ritual or craft inputs and tradable specialist produce, not a substitute staple for the town's grain supply. Cultivation and processing give Vorthara a material reason to trade outward and can help pay for imported food. The present-day [[The Crafters’ Union and The Elixirium|Elixirium]] canon establishes apothecaries, distillers, medicines and reagents, but does not yet explicitly assign it herb gardens. Ancient growing and preparation can be a precursor practice without placing the later guild federation in 350 BR; its direct institutional descent and the properties of particular plants remain to be tested in the canon pass.

**Next review phase requested by the author:** after the remaining short Package E questions, compare the classic 1E Kaer Maga districts with Vorthara's existing factions and neighbourhoods. Build a ward-by-ward translation matrix separating portable district dynamics from Pathfinder-specific factions, then identify which Vortharan districts need development. Keep this author-only until the author chooses what to promote; preserve the Black Spire's unknown original function, Vorthara's lack of citywide government, its later Spire Truce and its existing faction controls.

### Decision 52 — Native and cultivated-imported magical plants

**Locked during author review:** Vorthara's valuable plant trade draws on both unusual species native to the Spire's mountain environs and rare species imported from other regions for careful local cultivation. Distinctive soils, exposures, water and horticultural skill can matter alongside particular bounded anomalies, but the Giant-selective emanation is not automatically the cause of every magical property. Different growers and apothecaries control different seeds, sites and preparation methods.

This gives the refuge town an export and a reason for distant specialists to exchange botanical knowledge, while preserving its dependence on grain and other ordinary food imports. The ancient growers are not yet identified with the present-day Elixirium; that possible institutional descent belongs to the Kaer Maga–Vorthara district and faction review and later canon decisions.

### Decision 53 — Vorthara's historical weirdness and ward trajectory

**Accepted authorial direction:** develop Vorthara's history from its oldest known refuge habitation through the 350 BR boundary while keeping its eventual present-day factional city in view. By 350 BR the city should already have a conspicuous neighbourhood of corpse-working and animated dead, and a distinguishable place where cults and ritual brokers associated with Idols, Menhir and Egg White patrons gather. Its strangeness should be as memorable and spatially legible as the Kaer Maga comparison, but its causes, metaphysics and urban form must arise from World Egg conditions rather than copied Pathfinder districts.

**At this point in review**, the precise emergence and boundaries of those neighbourhoods, which cults are overt or hidden, the reason corpse-work first concentrated, any names, the dating of specific rites and methods, and direct institutional descent to modern factions remained open. Decisions 54–56 now establish Qahuri roots and an organised Pale Hand by 350 BR while leaving many dates and ward boundaries open. [[Vorthara before 350 BR - Refuge Cults and the Dead]] supplies the staged working model; [[Kaer Maga Districts and Vorthara Ward-Faction Translation Review]] remains the comparative control. The Spire Truce, four Shell anchors and Astral Conclave must not be silently backdated to 350 BR.

### Decision 54 — Qahuri roots of Vorthara's undead quarter

**Locked during author review:** ancient **Qahur**, the human Egyptian-coded civilisation of the Sacred Basin north of the inner-sea region, supplies the historical source of Vorthara's necromantic tradition. The Vortharan corpse-working quarter grows from Qahuri mortuary and necromantic practitioners who move into the refuge town and adapt their crafts there. It does **not** originate primarily in Vorthara's earliest fugitive burial customs or as a wholly local answer to freight demand. Older Vortharan funerary practice and dangerous pass work still shape reception and later use.

Qahuri tomb estates, embalmers and the minority White Measure provide **distinct** possible inheritances. Qahur is not Ishkatar; the White Measure's successful founders are soul-bound human mummies with Egg White estates, not the same as most Vortharan animated bodies, which contain no routed person. At this decision the arrival pattern, institutional lineages and relationship to the present Pale Hand remained open; Decision 55 below selects **several waves** while leaving their exact dates and houses undecided. [[Vorthara before 350 BR - Refuge Cults and the Dead]] develops the Qahuri diaspora model.

### Decision 55 — Several Qahuri waves and a wider death-working refuge

**Locked during author review:** Qahuri influence reaches Vorthara in several waves, not one founding migration. Small early mortuary households establish the core; upheavals including Qemaret's fall and the Single Radiance closures and aftermath can send later practitioners, dependants and rivals. By 350 BR the conspicuous undead quarter remains **Qahuri-rooted** but includes smaller traditions brought by particular Folk druids, other Orphaned peoples and individual exiles from further afield. A few ancient vampires may also find cover in Vorthara. These groups do not become one race, doctrine or standard animation technique; Decision 56 brings many practitioners into the Pale Hand's practical organisation without erasing their differences or all independent operators.

**Causal qualification:** many societies distrust corpse-working, predatory soul-binding and death magic, and some punish practitioners or force them out. But persecution is not universal or the only motive. Qahuri courts maintained honoured and wealthy mortuary estates, some Folk death and burial specialists served accepted local duties, and others travelled for clients, forbidden study, patronage or freedom from oversight. Before 350 BR such pressure comes from local rulers, households, temples, patrons and rival practitioners—not the later Redeemer's Church. Vorthara's no-extradition norm and ward-by-ward rules attract all of these. A Folk druid's Menhir relationship is not automatically necromancy; Morvalis governs bodily ending and grave stillness, not soul passage, and may oppose animation. An Ansharan vampire retains its own human soul by a Giant-derived blood lien, is not a soulless zombie or Qahuri mummy, and is not affected by the Spire merely for being a vampire. Exact arrivals, rites, vampire identities and social alignments remain open.

### Decision 56 — Pale Hand leadership and open death-magic recruitment by 350 BR

**Locked during author review:** the Pale Hand is already the **leading faction within Vorthara's death-working quarter by 350 BR**. It grows from a mostly Qahuri-led mortuary organisation formed across the several migration waves, then becomes sufficiently integrated and pragmatic to recruit skilled death-magic practitioners regardless of origin. Its effective offer is shelter from outside restrictions, access to clients and equipment, and paid work for anyone who can demonstrate a useful craft. Members and partners collaborate in different combinations according to the job: embalming, funerary custody, residue study, animation, counter-animation, body transport, medical preparation, dangerous-site work or patron negotiation. Qahuri roots remain visible in techniques and lineage, not as a permanent ethnic membership rule.

The Pale Hand's leadership comes from accumulated workshops, contracts, guarded stores, apprenticeships, equipment, clients and the ability to organise labour; it is **not** a townwide government, monopoly on every corpse, common law or charter over all other magi. Independent practitioners and opposed enclaves remain possible. A Folk druid may be a paid consultant who stops animation rather than performing it; a vampire may be a hidden patron, client or individual contributor, not a vampire caste ruling the quarter. The faction can contain cooperation, exploitation and disagreement without making soul-binding ordinary or morally neutral. This specific early organised faction qualifies Decision 44's general picture of informal magic and gives the present Pale Hand a plausible long institutional ancestry, while the date of its first name, exact founding, membership rules and later changes remain for development.

### Decision 57 — Public prohibition and concealed soul-binding

**Locked during author review:** by 350 BR the Pale Hand's **public working rule forbids binding a human soul** against the proper death-route. Its visible business is corpse preparation, funerary work, controlled animation of bodies without routed persons, dangerous labour, residue study and other paid death-craft. A concealed inner circle, protected clients or both nevertheless pursue soul-binding beneath the faction's shelter. The ban is a rule of the faction's public face, **not** a Vortharan citywide law. Discovery could split members, alienate clients and set neighbouring refuge households against it.

This is not an acceptable contractual exception: consent to a White Measure-style self-binding does not make the act good, and binding another person's soul compounds the wrong. Ordinary members need not know or approve. The secret gives the present Pale Hand's public services and hidden soul-route predation a possible historical line, but the first secret cell, its technique, patrons, victims, degree of leadership knowledge and continuity into the present remain open. Do not backdate the present black medium or every later practice simply because the moral double structure already exists.

### Decision 58 — Adjoining but distinct cult and death-working quarters

**Locked during author review:** by 350 BR Vorthara's plural cult courts and the Pale Hand-led death-working quarter are **recognisable, separate neighbourhoods that adjoin one another**. They share a disputed working edge rather than one blended ritual district or distant, unrelated locations. Qahuri Egg White patron rooms, funerary clients, contract witnesses, lodging and brokers have reasons to gather near the meeting streets. Menhir-associated courts and Idol-devotional houses have their own spaces and allegiances within the wider cult quarter; neither is simply a subdivision of the Pale Hand.

The boundary is social and practical, not necessarily a wall, fixed survey line or city ordinance. Practitioners cross it to hire, trade, spy, recruit, protect clients or dispute a body or bargain. The Hand can dominate death-work without governing the cult quarter, and cult houses may need its services while distrusting its public ban and hidden conduct. This gives Vorthara two distinct kinds of strangeness with an inevitable interface. The exact street layout, neighbourhood names and sequence by which the cult houses accumulated remain undecided.

### Decision 59 — Layered microcult quarter and competing outside agents

**Locked during author review:** the cult quarter formed gradually. Scattered rites and gathering places among the old refuge households gave later residents familiar places to meet; growing pass traffic, paid witnesses, lodging and professional brokers then concentrated ritual houses into a recognisable quarter by 350 BR. It has no single founding temple, revealed pantheon, cult parliament or discovered purpose of the Black Spire.

The quarter is a crowded nursery of **microcults**. Powers large and small seek human agents, intermediaries or witnesses because mortal access matters within the Material world. Human groups gather around purported demons, loyal or fallen angels, Idols and their Masks, Menhir and lesser courtiers, Egg White patrons, infernal offices, and other specific presences. Some contacts are genuine; others are exaggerated, misidentified, counterfeit, inherited after the original contact ended, or entirely human inventions. **A cult does not need to grant spells** to recruit, endure, collect money, command loyalty or change events. Influence may be a bargain, dream, threat, trade relationship, social network, ritual skill, image or merely a credible story.

These labels are **not** interchangeable cosmological species. “Demon” is vernacular until the individual power is identified; a devil mantle, fallen angel, Depth being, Idol Mask and Abyssal wound have different continuities. Loyal angels reject worship, cannot be purchased or routinely summoned, and act only under bounded commission; an angel cult may be sincere but mistaken, a counterfeit, or a community remembering a genuine encounter without owning it. Menhir courtiers bargain by their laws without moral judgement; Idol Masks knowingly feed vice; Egg White patrons trade through their particular anchors and agents. The cult quarter's plurality concerns **human institutions and claims**, not a neutral market in equally valid gods. Exact cults, names, numbers, visibility and rivalries remain for development.

### Decision 60 — Openly advertised dangerous cults by 350 BR

**Locked during author review:** most of the cult quarter is **open about whom it claims to serve**. Houses dedicated to Idol Masks, infernal or “demonic” powers, Menhir courts, Egg White patrons and claimed angelic visitations may display signs, receive clients and recruit publicly. The quarter is not a harmless frontage concealing all serious rites behind ordinary trades. Its visibility is one of the reasons Vorthara is notorious and attractive to people expelled or constrained elsewhere.

Open identification does **not** require a cult to disclose its inner rites, victims, true bargain terms or even the real identity of the being answering it. A human house may sincerely name a loyal angel but be mistaken; a true angel still rejects worship and cannot be hired. A cult may openly use its local Mask name without understanding the full Idol Throne behind it. Some groups remain secret, and outsiders may attempt suppression, but secrecy is the exception rather than the neighbourhood's dominant public form. The ability to advertise follows Vorthara's decentralised protection and lack of citywide religious law, not a civic endorsement of the cults' claims or acts. Decision 61 below establishes the rival deterrence that helps smaller houses persist.

### Decision 61 — Rival deterrence among the microcults

**Locked during author review:** by 350 BR no cult synod, shared quarter law, neutral police or permanent protection company governs the microcults. Houses defend their own thresholds, maintain armed and social allies, and retaliate when a neighbour seizes a client, shrine, lease or ritual site. A would-be hegemon risks bringing otherwise incompatible houses into a temporary coalition. The resulting balance is unstable and unequal: a tiny group with no patrons may still be crushed, while a larger house may win several lanes before provoking resistance.

This is **not** a cult version of the later Spire Truce. Deals for passage, witnesses, funerary work or protection last only while those involved can enforce them. Menhir courtiers, Idol Masks, infernal agents, Egg White patrons and claimed angelic houses do not acquire one moral or metaphysical standing because their human clients sometimes cooperate. The Pale Hand can sell services or intervene at the shared edge, but it does not rule the entire cult quarter. Decision 62 sets the Atherian external posture without making these cults orderly or harmless.

### Decision 62 — Atherian market cover, perimeter surveillance and exclusive services

**Locked during author review:** Atheria's official account describes Vorthara as a **small, legitimate trading market** whose business is above board. That is an imperial policy and public characterisation, **not** an objective description of the sizeable refuge town or proof that the openly advertised cults, coercive bargains and concealed soul-binding are harmless. Imperial authorities regard the *surrounding region* as needing surveillance: approaches, roads, pass traffic and outward movement are their concern, while inner streets and rival households remain Vorthara's own affair.

The initial agreement establishes **nonintervention in the city's internal governance and practices**. As the Atherians expand their empire, a **later exclusive contract for Vortharan services in support of imperial ambitions** is added to that relationship (Decision 63). The empire becomes the sole external hirer of the services **enrolled under that later clause**, rather than merely one preferred customer. This does not turn every Vortharan into an imperial employee or give imperial officers a domestic police or extradition power. Decisions 64–65 fix the mixed assembly, signatory and perimeter-enforcement mechanism and provider-specific schedules of covered services. The official market story can coexist with private imperial knowledge of alarming cults and occasional unauthorised interventions; neither cancels the agreed nonintervention policy.

### Decision 63 — Exclusive hire is a later expansion-era addition

**Locked during author review:** the agreement around the **350 BR boundary** recognises Vorthara's internal autonomy and Atheria's surveillance of the surrounding approaches; it does **not** yet grant Atheria exclusive hire of Vortharan services. That clause comes **later, as imperial expansion increases Atheria's need for specialists**, and is added to the existing external relationship rather than replacing the nonintervention guarantee. The exact later year, occasion and negotiating parties remain open. This is a forward trajectory beyond the present pre-350 BR research boundary, not a new event to insert before it.

### Decision 64 — Signed providers and enforcement outside the city

**Locked during author review (B + C):** a later temporary Vortharan gathering can accept the **framework** for exclusive imperial hiring, but cannot bind every resident as if it were a city government. **Named factions, households and individual practitioners sign their own service commitments.** Atheria is the exclusive external hirer of the services those signatories have enrolled. An independent provider who never signs remains legally outside that contract and may take other work; their mere residence in Vorthara does not make their labour imperial property. Thus this is a substantial contracted monopoly, **not** a literal monopoly over all Vortharan magic or every independent practitioner.

Imperial officers watch the roads, passes and outward hiring routes for breaches by **signatories**, using the external access Atheria controls. They have no agreed right to enter a ward and police its members, compel a non-signatory to join, or extradite someone for refusing the bargain. Disguised contracts, proxy clients and providers who depart by other routes create real enforcement problems. Decision 66 establishes the incentives to sign; sanctions for breach and the later date of the clause remain to be determined. None is an event before 350 BR.

### Decision 65 — Provider-specific exclusive-service schedules

**Locked during author review:** each later imperial contract **names the services reserved for Atheria**, negotiated with that particular faction, household or practitioner. The arrangement is neither a blanket claim over every kind of work a signatory can do nor limited by default to a single empire-wide category such as military magic. A signatory can take other commissions outside its listed services, and a non-signatory remains free even to offer comparable skills. Exact schedules may differ sharply between a death-working faction, a conjurer, a healer and a transport specialist; **no particular service list is fixed yet**. The named schedule makes a breach identifiable at the watched routes, but ambiguous jobs and concealed clients still invite dispute. This is a later expansion-era contract design, not a pre-350 BR institution.

### Decision 66 — Mixed rewards and protection for contracted magic

**Locked during author review:** Vortharan providers sign for a **negotiated combination** of reliable imperial payment and valuable access—food and material supply routes, safe passage, customers or other privileges suited to the particular provider. A further major inducement is **imperial protection while carrying out official or contracted work**: the practitioner can perform the craft and magic Atheria hired them to use without being punished merely because local authorities or ordinary prohibitions condemn that practice. This protection makes the empire's demand for otherwise suspect expertise credible, rather than leaving the provider exposed after doing its bidding.

The protection attaches to the **authorised commission and its listed practices**, not automatically to every private act by a signatory or to every resident of Vorthara. Decision 67 fixes its priority over ordinary local prohibitions; proof of authorisation and the consequences of exceeding a commission remain open. It is not an Atherian right to police Vorthara's inner wards, and it belongs to the **later expansion-era contract**, not the initial 350 BR bargain.

### Decision 67 — Imperial commission overrides local prohibitions

**Locked during author review:** within Atherian jurisdiction, a valid imperial commission **overrides local bans on the craft or magic it authorises**. Provincial or municipal officials cannot arrest, prosecute or punish a Vortharan signatory **merely for carrying out that authorised work**, even where the same practice would ordinarily be forbidden. This is stronger than a promise to review a local arrest afterward: the practitioner works under imperial protection from the outset. It is part of the later expansion-era contract, not the initial 350 BR agreement.

The commission is not blanket personal immunity. Private work, acts beyond the authorised service, and disputes over whether an action was actually commissioned remain distinct; Decision 68 assigns **commission-scope disputes** to a separate imperial tribunal, while remedies for established breaches remain open. Nor does this grant Atherian officers a corresponding right to police Vorthara's inner wards. The sharp conflict with local authorities is a consequence of the imperial hierarchy, not evidence that those authorities welcome the arrangement.

### Decision 68 — Separate imperial tribunal for disputed commissions

**Locked during author review:** when an official, harmed person or practitioner disputes whether a contested act fell within a Vortharan signatory's imperial commission, a **separate imperial tribunal** hears the matter. It is not simply the officer who ordered the work deciding whether their own order was exceeded. **Local witnesses and complainants can give evidence**, even though local authorities cannot arrest or punish a practitioner merely for carrying out the authorised craft. The tribunal determines the commission's scope under imperial authority; the precise procedure, available remedies and consequences for proven excess remain open.

This tribunal concerns **later Atherian contract work beyond the 350 BR boundary**. It is not a court over Vorthara's inner wards, a general right to extradite residents, or proof that all local grievances receive a fair outcome. Its existence creates a channel for conflict while leaving room for political pressure, biased evidence and disagreement about what an imperial commission permitted.

### Decision 69 — A city inside and around the Black Spire

**Locked during author review (A + B):** by 350 BR Vorthara has a **substantial inhabited interior within accessible parts of the Black Spire** *and* dense connected neighbourhoods built **against and around its lower exterior, foot and ascent**. Neither part is merely a token annex to the other. The earliest hidden refuge in or against the Spire expands into inhabited interior rooms and adjoining exterior construction while older ridge shelters and hamlets continue to grow. Repeated use of the same thresholds, water, freight routes, clients and markets joins formerly separate clusters into **one settlement in practice**, without creating one government or uniform ward law.

This is not a claim that the whole Spire is open, safe, built as a city, or understood by its inhabitants. Its deep or upper extent, original function and makers remain unknown. The inner–outer division is a historical and spatial fact; the **proportion of population in each, the particular entrances, vertical routes, interior ward boundaries and exact sequence of expansion** remain for later decisions. [[Vorthara before 350 BR - Refuge Cults and the Dead]] develops the provisional growth sequence. The modern Spire-Annex, Collegium and four Shell anchors are not automatically present by 350 BR.

### Decision 70 — Graduated passage from public market to guarded depths

**Locked during author review (A + C + B by location):** Vorthara's interior–exterior connections are **not governed by one access rule**. The approaches most used by strangers, traders and ordinary clients have **open, commonly used passages** with private rooms and premises opening off them. Other, less public specialist neighbourhoods retain a few shared main routes, while households and practitioners control side passages, workshops and chambers. Farther into more dangerous or secretive occupied areas, powerful practitioners, gangs and other local groups may guard entrances, demand permission or payment, and defend deeper thresholds by force or bargain.

The pattern grew from use: travellers and food carriers keep commercially valuable routes open; specialist work needs bounded space; dangerous groups seek concealment and control. An **open route is not a city-owned route**, a guarded threshold is not proof of jurisdiction over the whole Spire, and none implies a 350 BR council, common police, formal ward map or the later Spire Truce. “Deeper” describes some inhabited and contested accesses, **not** a claim that the Spire's unknown full interior has been explored. The precise passage network, gates and social rights of way remain open for mapping.

## 1. What the research changes

The initial image of Package E as “Mediterranean port cultures” is too broad to guide history. The research supports a sharper model:

1. **Old coastal and island communities already possess histories.** Proto-Atherian and Gateway settlers enter inhabited harbours, farm districts, fishing grounds, sacred places and legal relationships.
2. **Ports are systems, not façades.** Their institutions grow from grain, water, timber, pilotage, storage, labour, law and defence.
3. **An island is not a polity by default.** One island may contain rival ports, upland communities, settler fields, imperial forts and sacred neutral ground.
4. **Braiding is unequal and slow.** Settlement, marriage, manumission, military grants, enslavement, flight and shared danger all contribute; none guarantees harmony.
5. **Maritime violence is economically embedded.** Piracy requires buyers and patrons. Anti-piracy can expand state power. Captivity and ransom connect respectable ports to raiders.
6. **The later merchant republics are afterlives, not origins.** Ancient harbour committees and convoy practices may be remote institutional ancestors, but Venice, Genoa and Ragusa belong to different conditions.

## 2. Comparative structures with the highest Package E value

### A. The multi-port island

Crete, Lesbos, Rhodes before synoecism and large western islands show that one island can sustain rival civic centres. Package E can use an island whose inhabitants share routes and sanctuaries but not a government. War or imperial pressure may create a federation, common harbour court or new capital without erasing old identities.

### B. The near-mainland island

Salamis, Arwad, Tyre, Motya, Sant’Antioco and the Dalmatian islands show that the decisive hinterland may lie across a channel. Mainland orchards, quarries, markets and roads feed the island; island walls and fleets protect the mainland-facing port. Sovereignty is therefore relational.

### C. The water-poor sacred market

Delos, Samothrace and small central islands show how holiness, exemptions and trusted arbitration can draw a population larger than local ecology supports. Grain and cistern management become sacred politics.

### D. The resource island

Cypriot copper, Melian or Liparian obsidian, Parian marble, Elban iron and Sardinian metals show that extraction is a chain of labour and transport. The strongest history follows mines, forests, fuel, roads, brokers and exhausted landscapes—not a single fabulous commodity.

### E. The settler port among older communities

Sicily, Sardinia, Ibiza, Hvar, Vis, Korčula and Cyprus show multiple outcomes: violent land seizure, negotiated enclave, mixed sanctuary, bilingual administration, continued upland autonomy and eventual imperial classification. Package E’s core braided formation should choose different outcomes in different ports.

### F. The pilot archipelago

The Kvarner and Tunisian islands show that shallow-water, channel and storm knowledge can outweigh fleet size. Pilot houses can be service guilds, hostage groups, hereditary officials or politically divided brokers.

### G. The refuge/destination city

Kaer Maga and Vorthara show a city attracting forbidden trades and displaced people while depending on supply choke points. Ancient historical parallels add sanctuary ports, mountain passes and imperial border markets. Vorthara’s distinct institutions must remain controlling.

### H. The raider–naval continuum

Ancient, Adriatic and early-modern cases show captains moving among commerce, escort, mercenary service, licensed seizure and piracy. A peace treaty can criminalise crews without removing their skills, debts or patrons.

### I. The drowned predecessor and successor port

[[Atlantis Azlant Absalom and the Drowned-City Problem]] finds room for an Atlantis-like emotional register only by separating it from Nythalum's established domain. The strongest bounded structure is an older inner-sea port or small polity lost through local seismic, hydraulic and political causes, followed by a later cosmopolitan city built on adjacent high ground, uplift, harbour fill, causeways or exposed upper ruins. Refugees, neighbours, salvagers and later citizens inherit incompatible claims. The successor may say that one founder “raised” the city, while material history records generations of communal rebuilding. This carries Atlantis below and an Absalom-like civic centre above without copying crystal power, mortal apotheosis, a second global catastrophe or another lunar empire.

## 3. Structures that should not be combined automatically

- A Delos-like sacred free port and a Tortuga-like raider base solve different trust problems.
- A Tyre-like island capital and a Venice-like lagoon republic belong to different institutional ages.
- A Nuragic-like tower landscape does not imply Kaer Maga/Vorthara-style unknown architecture.
- Rhodian maritime law, Ragusan diplomacy and Genoese finance should not be compressed into one “merchant republic culture.”
- Balearic mercenary reputation should not define all households or all islands.
- Phoenician, Greek, Punic, Illyrian and Roman labels cannot become one-to-one World Egg peoples.

## 4. Canon compatibility audit

### Package A — Qahur and the Gateway

Package A controls the Gateway Republic, its wars with Caleran, the payment revolt, child sacrifice, the destruction of Qarzeth and the spared towns. Package E may show island suppliers, sailors, captives, mercenaries or refugee destinations only after specific integration. It cannot invent a second Punic-War sequence, transfer Qarzeth’s role to an island or make the Gateway the source of all maritime civilisation.

**Safe research use:** ask which existing ports bought Gateway goods, sheltered enemies or absorbed displaced households.

### Package B — Qiryath and eastern empires

Package B controls Arqesh, Vashar, the Crown of Roads and Dawns and the eastern wars. Cyprus/Levant analogies demonstrate tributary city-kingdoms and multilingual imperial negotiation, but cannot create an unrecorded maritime empire on Vashar’s flank.

**Safe research use:** compare how an island dynasty pays tribute, supplies ships and retains local courts.

### Package C — Vathra and northern ground

Package C controls the Great Unyoking, proto-Ahdari and Tarkhan development and the absence of anachronistic horse mastery. Package E should not create maritime settlers who explain inland peoples’ origins or import later nomadic institutions into port history.

**Safe research use:** trace grain, livestock, salt and metal exchange through named intermediaries rather than ethnic shortcuts.

### Package D — southern cold world

Package D controls Folk, Ättar, Grobi and Menhir relations. Package E’s sailors, raiders or islanders do not become Ättar because they use keels; island sacred sites do not become Menhirs through resemblance. Existing Atherian contact chronology remains fixed.

**Safe research use:** ask when Package D maritime routes meet Package E ports and which side controls ships, pilots or cargo.

### Existing Arkenan–Saronikan history

The existing cities and geography are prior constraints. Research must attach to established coasts rather than redraw them around Greek or Adriatic maps. Older settlements require continuity, abandonment or destruction evidence before replacement.

### Vorthara

The Black Spire is pre-Atherian and of unknown original function. Atheria added an outer administrative and infrastructural layer without fully governing the wards. The Spire Truce, Wardens, four Shell anchors, Astral Conclave and metaphysical distinctions remain fixed. Package E research can clarify regional supply and migration but cannot identify the Spire’s builders or purpose.

### The 350 BR boundary

Merchant republics, Hospitaller Malta, Barbary regencies, Venice’s mature convoy state and Tortuga are afterlife comparisons only. Package E may establish modest ancient antecedents—harbour magistrates, merchant associations, public granaries, prize disputes—without importing their mature successors.

## 5. Unsuitable or misleading borrowings

Reject the following unless later evidence independently establishes them:

- one ancient pan-island ethnic nation;
- a peaceful trade civilisation whose wealth is detached from coercion;
- universal sacred matriarchy inferred from figurines;
- archaeological style as proof of population replacement;
- every unexplained monument built by Giants;
- one city constitution copied from Rhodes, Carthage, Venice or Kaer Maga;
- pirates living outside markets and political protection;
- island ecology producing fixed national character;
- modern criminal, tourist or national stereotypes projected backward;
- later folklore presented as Bronze Age survival;
- a single decisive battle instantly creating or destroying a commercial system.
- a second oceanic crystal super-civilisation beside Nythalum;
- a lost mother race used to explain all later human cultures;
- mortal apotheosis, divinity awarded by ordeal or a city raised by an otherwise ordinary founder's god-act;
- a submerged city treated as an empty dungeon when descendants or present inhabitants retain claims.

## 6. Authorial questions for Package E development

### Geography and scale

1. How many established islands and island clusters matter to the known Arkenan–Saronikan routes by 350 BR?
2. Which are large multi-polity islands, and which are small specialised stations?
3. Which port’s essential farmland or timber lies across a strait under another community’s control?
4. Where are the dangerous seasonal gaps, shoals, lee shores and water-poor crossings?

### Old populations and newcomers

5. Which communities survived the Unbinding on the islands, and what material institutions did they retain?
6. Which proto-Atherian or Gateway settlers arrived as refugees, conquerors, hired crews, merchants or sanctioned colonists?
7. Where was land negotiated, where seized, and where did newcomers remain a harbour minority?
8. Which older populations retained upland, fishing or sacred jurisdiction?

### Government and legal status

9. Does any island possess a shared council by 350 BR, or only common sanctuaries and wartime leagues?
10. Who counts as a citizen, resident foreigner, client, freed person or enslaved person in each major port?
11. Which disputes fall under harbour law, household law, settler charter or imperial decree?
12. What narrow public office emerged first: water keeper, harbourmaster, grain commissioner, pilot warden or convoy captain?

### Economy and labour

13. What staple feeds each city, and who controls the granaries?
14. What export is valuable only because of mines, forests, kilns, fisheries or skilled processing elsewhere?
15. Who builds ships, who owns them, who rows or sails them, and who may leave?
16. Where are captives sold, ransomed or absorbed into households?
17. Which port’s prosperity collapses when a route, resource or patron changes?

### War and piracy

18. Which crews served the Gateway, Caleran or Atherian wars, and under what contracts?
19. Which peace rendered former auxiliaries pirates?
20. Which respectable city buys plunder while publicly condemning raiders?
21. Is anti-piracy policy protecting ordinary sailors, extending a monopoly, or both?

### Cult and memory

22. Which harbour sanctuary mediates oaths or safe conduct without becoming politically neutral?
23. Which imported cult was translated into local terms, and what metaphysical distinction prevents false identity?
24. Which later foundation myth hides land seizure, mixed household formation or a failed settlement?
25. Which folklore is demonstrably late but still useful as a later cultural memory?

### Vorthara

26. Which Package E routes fed the known pre-imperial settlement around the Black Spire?
27. What did Atherian quarantine and road systems change in existing regional exchange?
28. Which later Vortharan ward identities are braided descendants of distinct coastal and inland migrations?

### Drowned predecessor and successor city

29. Is the desired object a dead city, a small living remnant or a later successor metropolis built over and beside the dead?
30. Did submergence occur during the Unbinding, or through a later human-era sequence of earthquake, ground failure, damaged water works and political abandonment?
31. Does the predecessor have no Nythalum connection, a bounded client/trade relationship, or only a later false identification with Nythalum?
32. What regional institution—not crystals, lunar travel or machine immortality—made the predecessor unusually important?
33. Which established Arkenan–Saronikan city could inherit this layered past without erasing its current canon or distorting the Thalassian map?
34. Who controls salvage, burial, access and interpretation in the drowned districts, and whose labour or displacement does the official “raising” legend conceal?

## 7. Recommended review sequence

The author can now review without deciding the whole package at once:

1. choose three to five **geographic island types**;
2. choose two or three **settlement relationships** between old communities and newcomers;
3. choose the **port institutions** already present by 350 BR;
4. decide how slavery, ransom and mercenary service connect to Package A wars;
5. decide what Vorthara can know about its regional past without explaining the Black Spire;
6. decide whether the drowned-predecessor/successor-port model belongs in an existing city, a bounded new site or nowhere;
7. only then commission a named Package E development history.

## 8. Final research ruling

Package E is ready for authorial selection, not canon promotion. The corpus provides enough comparative breadth to avoid a single Greek, Phoenician, Venetian, pirate or Pathfinder template. No canonical file, map, people, event or chronology has been created by this research pass.
