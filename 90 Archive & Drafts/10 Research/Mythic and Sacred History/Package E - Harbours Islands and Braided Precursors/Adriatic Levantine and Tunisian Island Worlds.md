# Adriatic, Levantine and Tunisian Island Worlds

> [!warning] Comparative research only
> The modern Croatian, Syrian, Lebanese, Tunisian and Albanian labels locate the cases. They do not provide ancient ethnic identities.

## Regional frame

The eastern Adriatic is a longitudinal island corridor: karst ridges, narrow channels, sheltered bays and scarce fields make sailing and local topography inseparable. The Levantine cases are dense harbour islands facing mainland cities. The Tunisian cases are low, dry islands whose fisheries, orchards, shoals and African connections challenge the stereotype that strategic islands must be rocky fortresses.

## 23. Hvar–Vis

**Source facts.** Hvar and Vis contain sheltered bays, karst uplands and limited but valuable fields. Prehistoric hillforts and burial landscapes show local communities before Greek foundations. Pharos on Hvar was founded in the early fourth century BCE and its chora preserves a striking field division; Issa on Vis developed as a Greek polis and established or influenced mainland settlements. The relationship between settlers and local communities included fighting, land division, exchange and cultural mixture rather than simple replacement.

Vis’s outer position and harbour supported shipping; Hvar’s Stari Grad plain supported agriculture. Greek and local ceramic traditions, coinage and inscriptions trace networks but do not assign every household an ethnic identity. Roman power later absorbed both. Mercenary, raiding and “Illyrian piracy” labels in Greek and Roman texts require care because imperial enemies described maritime violence selectively.

**Cult, myth and afterlife.** Palagruža’s Diomedes sanctuary and navigators’ dedications show sailors inscribing dangerous routes into sacred geography. Later Venetian island culture and modern maritime folklore are afterlives, not ancient continuity.

**Portable structures.** A paired island system: outer naval port and inner agricultural colony; surveyed settler fields beside surviving hillfort communities; sanctuaries marking open-water risk.

**Do not borrow.** Do not make field grids proof of peaceful colonisation or use “Illyrian pirate” as a stable ethnic profession.

**Package E application.** Useful for a sea-lane partnership in which one harbour supplies crews and another grain, while local upland communities contest both.

**Canon collisions.** Must not introduce Croatian or Greek identities into the World Egg. Any route must fit established Arkenan–Saronikan geography.

**Sources.** University of Michigan Kelsey Museum, “[Archaeological Fieldwork in the Adriatic Islands](https://lsa.umich.edu/kelsey/publications/newsletters/fall-2003/archaeological-fieldwork-in-the-adriatic-islands--2003.html)”; Branko Kirigin and collaborators’ Adriatic Island Project publications; [UNESCO Stari Grad Plain](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1240).

## 24. Korčula–Brač

**Source facts.** Korčula and Brač are long Dalmatian islands with caves, hillforts, terraces, bays and difficult internal ground. Vela Spila on Korčula preserves deep prehistoric occupation and evidence of Adriatic mobility. Literary and epigraphic evidence records Greek settlement claims, including Lumbarda’s famous decree, but the location and success of some supposed colonies remain disputed. Indigenous communities persisted and adopted selected Hellenistic goods and practices. Brač’s stone, grazing and position near the mainland made it a different economic landscape; ongoing archaeology shows imported Greek and Italian goods by the early first millennium BCE.

Household farming, herding, fishing, quarrying and seafaring underlay exchange. A harbour settlement did not command every hillfort. Roman estates, quarries and towns later reorganised labour and land.

**Cult, myth and afterlife.** Foundation decrees are political instruments defining settlers and land; they do not preserve the voices of those displaced. Medieval stonework and Venetian-era island identities are later afterlives.

**Portable structures.** Colonist land allotment recorded publicly; several indigenous strongholds surviving around a claimed colony; quarry communities supplying prestigious mainland building.

**Do not borrow.** Do not infer total Hellenisation from pottery or treat a foundation inscription as a neutral account of ownership.

**Package E application.** A strong model for treaty colonies whose parchment or stone title is clearer than their effective territorial control.

**Canon collisions.** Land division cannot silently displace established old island survivors; dispossession and negotiated survival must appear if used.

**Sources.** Kelsey Museum’s [Adriatic fieldwork overview](https://lsa.umich.edu/kelsey/publications/newsletters/fall-2003/archaeological-fieldwork-in-the-adriatic-islands--2003.html); University of Colorado, [Brač Island Project](https://www.colorado.edu/classics/brac); Slobodan Čače et al., *Corpus of Greek Graffiti from Dalmatia* ([BMCR review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2023/2023.11.33/)).

## 25. Cres–Lošinj–Krk–Rab

**Source facts.** The Kvarner islands sit between Istria, the northern Adriatic and Dalmatia. Karst, bora winds, sheep pasture, timber, narrow channels and uneven fresh water shaped settlement. Caves and open sites preserve prehistoric occupation and long-distance interaction. Iron Age hillforts participated in Liburnian and northern Adriatic networks; Roman conquest produced towns, estates, roads and veteran or colonial arrangements without erasing all local practice.

Ancient literary labels such as Liburnian and Roman accounts of raiding are external categories as well as historical evidence. Fast local craft, pilotage and channel knowledge could serve trade, naval service or raiding depending on patron and context. Rab and Krk held better agricultural and urban possibilities than some smaller neighbours.

**Cult, myth and afterlife.** Archaeological burial and sanctuary evidence is stronger than recoverable named local mythology. The medieval communes, Glagolitic tradition and Venetian/Habsburg frontiers are important later histories but cannot be projected backward.

**Portable structures.** Channel pilots and small craft as political resources; island hierarchy based on water and cultivable land; raiding labels imposed by expanding states; closely spaced islands retaining separate civic centres.

**Do not borrow.** Do not create a single “Liburnian people” with one government or romanticise bora-hardened pirates.

**Package E application.** Useful for an archipelago where practical navigation knowledge gives small communities bargaining power over armies and grain fleets.

**Canon collisions.** No automatic Ättar link follows from boats or raiding; Package D’s Ättar history remains distinct.

**Sources.** Stašo Forenbaher, “[Archaeological record of the Adriatic offshore islands](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-journal-of-archaeology/article/abs/archaeological-record-of-the-adriatic-offshore-islands-as-an-indicator-of-longdistance-interaction-in-prehistory/8679680A7309D83F818D49066DFBB108)”; Mitja Guštin and regional studies of Liburnian archaeology; University of Zagreb/Institute of Archaeology research on Kvarner cave and island settlement.

## 26. Arwad and ancient island Tyre

**Source facts.** Arwad is a very small walled island off the Syrian coast, facing mainland Antaradus/Tartus. Its natural harbour, defensive position and location between the Levant, Cyprus and Anatolia made it a city-state and naval participant under successive Assyrian, Babylonian, Persian, Hellenistic and Roman hegemonies. Water and food necessarily tied it to the mainland. Ancient Tyre likewise occupied an island close to shore, with harbours and a mainland agricultural dependency. Alexander’s 332 BCE siege created a causeway, permanently changing the coastline.

Phoenician cities shared language and practices but were separate polities. Tyrian settlement and commercial networks in the west did not form a modern nation-state or centrally controlled “colonial empire.” Kings, councils, temples, merchant households, ship crews and subject communities had different interests. Purple-dye production, timber acquisition, craft, tribute and long-distance trade depended on coerced as well as free labour.

**Cult, myth and afterlife.** Melqart’s Tyrian cult travelled and was translated by Greek and Punic communities without proving that every Heracles was one being. Biblical, Assyrian and Classical accounts are interested witnesses. Europa, Dido and Hiram belong to different literary-political traditions. Arwad’s Crusader/Templar last stand is a much later afterlife.

**Portable structures.** Dense island capital dependent on a mainland “opposite city”; kings surviving by tribute and fleet service under empires; merchant diasporas with shared cult but local political autonomy; siege physically converting an island into a peninsula.

**Do not borrow.** Do not construct a unitary Phoenician empire, assume mercantile peace or equate translated gods metaphysically.

**Package E application.** A prime model for an island port whose apparent autonomy rests on mainland orchards, cisterns, imperial bargains and a diaspora it cannot command directly.

**Canon collisions.** Must not duplicate Qarzeth or the Gateway Republic, identify World Egg powers through Melqart/Heracles comparison, or invent a coast-altering siege without synchronising geography.

**Sources.** [UNESCO Tyre dossier](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/299); [UNESCO Arwad dossier](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/1303); Glenn Markoe, *Phoenicians*; Metropolitan Museum, *Assyria to Iberia at the Dawn of the Classical Age* ([catalogue PDF](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Assyria_to_Iberia_at_the_Dawn_of_the_Classical_Age.pdf)).

## 27. Djerba–Kerkennah

**Source facts.** Djerba and the Kerkennah islands are low, dry and close to the Tunisian coast. Shallow seas, lagoons, fisheries, palms, olives and difficult water management produced economies unlike the rocky Aegean. Djerba’s ancient Meninx became a major Roman-period production and trading centre, especially associated with purple dye and imported marble. Earlier Punic and local North African histories are present but unevenly recovered. Kerkennah’s shoals and fish-trap traditions made intimate environmental knowledge essential; ancient Cercina appears in Roman political and exile narratives.

Their location encouraged coastwise exchange without guaranteeing urban centralisation. Roman roads, estates and ports tied island production to African cities. Low islands were defensible through shoals and pilotage rather than walls alone.

**Cult, myth and afterlife.** The Homeric “Lotus-eaters” were later located on Djerba, but literary geography is not identification. Djerba’s Jewish, Ibadi Muslim and later corsair histories are significant afterlives and should be dated rather than projected into antiquity.

**Portable structures.** Shoal-protected ports navigable only with local pilots; fisheries and dye works joining rural households to export; low islands serving exile, refuge and surveillance; religious minorities sustained by later legal histories.

**Do not borrow.** Do not turn the Lotus-eaters into ethnography or describe the islands as empty stepping-stones between “civilisations.”

**Package E application.** Useful for island communities whose greatest defence is environmental expertise and whose politics face the nearby mainland more than the open sea.

**Canon collisions.** Do not import later Jewish or Ibadi analogues before Package E establishes appropriate World Egg religious histories.

**Sources.** Stefan Ritter et al., Meninx research, including “[Foreign architects, artisans, and marble at Meninx](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/libyan-studies/article/looking-over-the-builders-work-foreign-architects-artisans-and-marble-at-meninx-djerba/A39678C5E07B76DAC0AB3ECA27F596CB)”; Elizabeth Fentress, Ali Drine and Renata Holod, *An Island Through Time: Jerba Studies*; David Stone, Lea Stirling and Ben Lazreg, work on Roman coastal Tunisia.

## Short comparative capsules

### Lastovo–Mljet

Remote southern Adriatic islands combined forests, anchorages, fields and seasonal connections. Greek pottery and later Roman villas show contact without evidence for large independent states; Mljet’s identification with Odysseus or St Paul is later competitive sacred geography. **Use:** islands whose remoteness is relative to wind and route. **Source:** Forenbaher, “[Adriatic offshore islands](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-journal-of-archaeology/article/abs/archaeological-record-of-the-adriatic-offshore-islands-as-an-indicator-of-longdistance-interaction-in-prehistory/8679680A7309D83F818D49066DFBB108)”; *Corpus of Greek Graffiti from Dalmatia*.

### Sazan–Zvërnec

Sazan commands the entrance to the Bay of Vlorë, while low Zvërnec lies in the Narta lagoon. Their main comparative value is paired control: exposed military lookout and sheltered lagoon sanctuary/landing. Ancient evidence is thinner than for Dalmatian islands, so claims should remain topographic and regional. **Use:** paired outer watch-island and inner refuge. **Source:** Albanian Institute of Archaeology surveys of the Bay of Vlorë and Pierre Cabanes, *Les Illyriens de Bardylis à Genthios*.

### Lebanon’s Palm Islands

The small islands off Tripoli offered anchorages and later archaeological traces but never matched Tyre or Arwad as urban island states. They are useful precisely as minor route features whose occupation fluctuated. **Use:** avoid promoting every mapped island into a kingdom; some are seasonal stations, quarries or hazards. **Source:** Lebanese Directorate General of Antiquities and UNESCO Man and the Biosphere, [Palm Islands reserve](https://en.unesco.org/biosphere/arab-states/palm-islands).

## Regional conclusions

The Adriatic cases show **corridor sovereignty**: control can mean pilots, signals, anchorages and agricultural landing rights rather than a continuous territorial border. Greek foundations and Roman towns sit within older island landscapes that remain politically active.

The Levantine cases expose the cost of **dense insularity**. Arwad and Tyre could defend a small urban surface only by binding themselves to mainland food, water and imperial bargains. Their diaspora relationships were powerful but never identical to direct rule.

The Tunisian cases provide the corrective of **low-island power**. Shoals, fisheries, lagoons and route knowledge can matter as much as cliffs and fortresses. Package E should include at least one port culture whose expertise lies in shallow water, tidal passage and food production rather than heroic open-sea sailing.
