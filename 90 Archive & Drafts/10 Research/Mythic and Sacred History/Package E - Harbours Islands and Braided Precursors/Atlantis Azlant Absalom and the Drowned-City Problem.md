# Atlantis, Azlant, Absalom and the Drowned-City Problem

> [!warning] Research boundary
> This is an author-only comparison. It creates no World Egg city, civilisation, catastrophe, map, chronology or surviving people. Its purpose is to determine what a drowned inner-sea history could add **without duplicating Nythalum** or importing another setting's metaphysics.

## Authorial decision register

### Decision 1 — Core form

**Locked during author review:** the inner-sea Atlantis concept is a **drowned predecessor city or small kingdom followed by a later living successor port built above and beside its ruins**.

The predecessor is not a second Nythalum and the site is not an intact hidden empire waiting to return. A small, bounded remnant below the water remains possible, but most of the predecessor population died, escaped or entered other communities. The successor city's inhabitants, institutions and mixed population require their own history rather than inheriting automatic identity with the drowned polity.

### Decision 2 — Historical period and cause

**Locked during author review:** the predecessor was a mixed Giant–human maritime polity of the late Age of Chains. It had genuine commercial and political connections with Nythalum but did not possess, reproduce or secretly participate in Nythalum's lunar programme.

The city drowned amid the wider Great Unbinding through a local causal sequence involving seismic damage, failing harbour or water-control works, neglected maintenance and civil conflict. Nythalum's launch did not directly sink it. The two disasters belong to the same collapsing world without becoming one event.

### Decision 3 — Colony, rising power and disputed sisterhood

**Locked in principle during author review:** the predecessor began as a Nythalum-founded colonial harbour within the wider Moon Harbours of Caelene network. It did not begin as Nythalum's peer. Distance, accumulated wealth, control of inner-sea routes and the growth of a locally rooted mixed population gradually gave its ruling houses the means to resist metropolitan direction.

In its later period, the colonial government asserted autonomy and styled the polity as Nythalum's sister, successor or improved continuation. Nythalum continued to describe it as a dependent harbour, rebellious client or provincial creation. “Sister civilisation” is therefore a partisan constitutional claim rather than an objective statement of equal antiquity or capacity.

This uses the productive Azlant–Thassilon relationship—an offshoot becoming an independent rising power—without copying Xin, Runelords, a sevenfold imperial order or Azlanti human primacy. The polity never develops a rival lunar programme.

### Decision 4 — Conquest, settlement and unequal mixture

**Locked during author review:** the colony joined conquest to demographic settlement. Nythalum's Giant-blooded colonists and the human populations they brought with them did not arrive upon empty land. They defeated the existing human inhabitants, destroyed or subordinated their principal institutions and took control of the best harbours, fields and water systems.

The colonial population became numerically and politically dominant in the core districts. The earlier inhabitants were nevertheless not exterminated or wholly replaced. Survivors persisted through enslavement, dependent villages, flight to marginal islands and uplands, coerced incorporation and mixed households. Their ancestry and practices remained strongest outside the colonial core and survived inside it in work, food, local knowledge, family life and contested sacred places.

The comparison to the peopling and state formation of the Japanese archipelago applies only at the level of **successive migration, expansion, admixture and uneven regional survival**. Ancient-DNA research supports substantial mixture among Jōmon-related inhabitants and later continental migrants, with regional differences rather than a simple one-time replacement ([Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-54052-0); [Journal of Human Genetics](https://www.nature.com/articles/jhg2016110)). The World Egg polity remains its own conquest history and does not map those populations onto fantasy species.

### Decision 5 — Living marine infrastructure

**Locked during author review:** the colony's rival civilisational technology was biological, marine and ecologically distributed. Nythalum pursued crystal memory, mechanical calculation, bodily continuity and physical lunar travel. Its colonial offshoot instead combined appropriated native reef husbandry with Giant fleshcraft to turn engineered marine ecosystems into infrastructure.

Networks of altered coral, molluscs, sponges, kelp, siphonophore-like colonies and leviathan-scale reef organisms grew nacreous buildings, harbour walls, desalinating membranes, bioluminescent signals, organic ships, buoyancy organs and tide-processing ganglia. The city was not merely built upon this system: much of the city was a cultivated living body-complex. Its distributed biological processing could model currents and weather without storing personalities, transferring minds or becoming a second Nythalum computation system.

The rulers' central moral error was to treat bred life as morally empty infrastructure. Individual organisms could feel pain, fear and conditioning even where the network possessed no single unified person. Colonial ideology credited Giant bloodcraft while erasing the earlier human cultivation knowledge and the continuing labour required to feed, prune and restrain the system.

The same technology caused the fall. During the Unbinding, seismic damage, biological disruption, rebellion and contradictory dynastic control signals drove the living foundations into defensive closure and attempted deep migration. Some structures sealed or digested occupied chambers; others shed dead shell or detached. The contracting foundation destabilised saturated ground and pulled much of the capital into deeper water. Surviving organisms continue to maintain, consume and regrow portions of the ruin without reproducing its intended plan.

### Decision 6 — The dolphin escape and transformed survivors

**Locked during author review:** a limited number of inhabitants escaped through emergency biological transformation. Living chambers continuously reshaped their existing bodies into cetacean, amphibious or otherwise pelagic forms. This was bodily alteration rather than memory transfer: an Image-bearing human remained the same human soul, while an actual Giant retained the same Giant continuity if the living transformation remained uninterrupted.

The ruling order initially reserved viable transformations for selected dynastic households and valuable specialists. Workers and rebels seized some facilities during the collapse. Many candidates drowned, died in malformed transitions or survived unable to return to land. Only a minority escaped successfully. Their later communities, offspring, degeneration and possible relationship to one local Deep Envoy population remain open for a dedicated bestiary and continuity review; they do not explain every aquatic people.

Later tradition compressed this unequal event into the claim that the drowned city's people became dolphins and followed their living city into the deep. That motif is not part of Plato's Atlantis story. Its securely ancient real-world comparator is the transformation of the Tyrrhenian pirates in the *Homeric Hymn to Dionysus* ([Harvard Center for Hellenic Studies](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/primary-source/homeric-hymn-to-dionysus-sb/)). In the World Egg tradition, transformation can signify rescue, punishment or the ruling houses' attempt to disguise rationed survival as a collective miracle.

### Decision 7 — Dynastic confederation and the attempted monopoly

**Locked during author review:** the mature colony was governed by a confederation of colonial dynastic houses. Each house maintained hereditary control grafts, specialist households and custodial rights over a different part of the living infrastructure: reef foundations, ship organisms, fresh-water membranes, defensive fauna, communication ganglia, transformation chambers or other bounded systems. Safe operation depended upon cooperation among houses and the human experts and labour communities beneath them.

One senior dynasty held a first-among-equals office. As the colony's wealth and independence grew, that house attempted to combine the other control lineages within its own ruling body and convert a negotiated confederation into a centralised hereditary empire. Rival houses resisted; technicians, imported human communities and descendants of the conquered inhabitants pursued their own survival and political claims.

The resulting civil conflict divided physical control of the city-organism. During the Unbinding, its systems received incompatible commands to remain anchored, close the passage, attack rebels, preserve selected districts, transform chosen refugees and begin deep migration. The fall was therefore simultaneously constitutional, colonial, biological and material.

### Decision 8 — Responsibility for the drowning

**Locked during author review:** Unbinding-era earthquakes, biological disruption and broken external supply made catastrophe possible, but the senior dynasty made the city's drowning effectively inevitable. Its rulers knew that the foundations were injured and that incompatible commands were propagating through the living network. They nevertheless overrode confederate safeguards and ordered a forced deep migration intended to preserve the palace, selected specialists and controlled transformation nurseries.

The human revolt did not cause the city's fall. Labour communities, native-descended households and their allies seized control sites because the senior dynasty was abandoning most inhabitants while reserving viable escape bodies for its dependants. Rebel actions worsened some local failures, as did resistance by rival houses, but the decisive culpable choice was the dynasty's attempt to force a wounded and suffering biological system beyond its limits.

Later religious traditions may interpret the converging disaster as judgement. The historical account retains distinct layers of cause: the wider Unbinding, local material vulnerability, colonial exploitation, civil conflict and a knowing final override.

### Decision 9 — Continuous but broken occupation

**Locked during author review:** the site was never wholly abandoned and later rediscovered as empty country. Survivors established camps, fishing settlements and refuge quarters on the remaining high ground soon after the drowning. They included native-descended communities, imported human workers, escaped slaves, lesser Giant-blooded households, ordinary refugees and people subsequently drawn into the passage by trade.

These communities retained practical knowledge of tides, dangerous organisms and a limited number of safe routes into the ruins. They did not retain command of the complete living system or recreate the drowned constitution. Over generations, salvage camps became permanent wharves, shrines, workshops and neighbourhoods. Later migrants joined, displaced, married and bargained with existing communities rather than “discovering” and civilising an empty site.

The successor metropolis therefore possesses genuine demographic and material continuity with the drowned city, but it is not the same civilisation restored. Its mixed population and institutions require a post-catastrophe history of their own.

### Decision 10 — The wandering reefback city and the Thalassorian refoundation

**Locked in principle during author review:** the major successor was not founded by an Absalom-like engineer-lawgiver. Between roughly 1000 and 900 BR, a powerful ritual specialist from one of the coastal Folk traditions reached the survivor settlements. Later accounts call this person a druid and follower of [[01 Canon Reference/Cosmology/30 Menhir/20 Third Circle/Thalassor.md|Thalassor]], but neither title implies a universal Folk priesthood or an ordained representative of the Menhir.

The druid did not resurrect the dead or restore the predecessor civilisation. They learned to negotiate with the wounded marine infrastructure, the local tide courts and a colossal [[01 Canon Reference/Creatures/10 Material and Residue/10 Predators and Megafauna/Material Sea Leviathans.md#Reefbacks|reefback]] whose migration passed through the ruins. Through a bounded Thalassorian bargain, surviving living structures were grafted into a new settlement across the reefback's shell without pinning or enslaving the animal. The city cleaned, fed and protected its bearer; the reefback carried the city along a recurring but variable migration. Thalassor governed the currents, concealment and price of the passage rather than possessing or personally steering the creature.

The result was a moving harbour rather than an entire island floating free. Its temple, ritual courts, oldest living buildings and central refuge rode upon the reefback. Organic vessels, barges, rafts and detachable neighbourhoods gathered around it when conditions allowed and dispersed before dives, storms or war. Outsiders consequently remembered a vanishing island-city borne upon a world-turtle. Its actual movement, seasonal absences and Thalassor-granted concealment explain why territorial empires could raid or bargain with it for centuries without permanently occupying it.

The renewed settlement first served fishers, salvagers, drowned-city descendants and coastal refugees. It then became a sanctuary, free port and market for exiles, smugglers, mercenaries and sea raiders. Its long "pirate age" was not one unchanging pirate nation: custodial circles, harbour assemblies, merchant houses, captains' leagues and outside clients successively controlled parts of its economy. Thalassor did not approve piracy or excuse its violence. Safe currents and sanctuary made raiding possible, while buyers, political sponsors, ransom markets and rival ports made it profitable.

The city also became a principal meeting ground between surface peoples and local [[01 Canon Reference/Creatures/20 Faewild and Menhir/40 Mythic Fauna and Convergences/Aerials and Deep Envoys.md#Deep Envoys|Deep Envoys]]. These were never one fishfolk nation. They included distinct trench households, wreck-field communities, transformed descendants of the drowned colony and other pressure-adapted peoples with incompatible interests. Surface names such as fishmen, mermen and Deep Ones blurred those differences.

Child-taking belonged to particular bargains and communities, not to every aquatic person. Children might be promised as the price of safe passage, seized in retaliation for broken coastal covenants, taken as wards or hostages by a tide court, or recovered from drowning only after bodily alteration into forms the sea could sustain. Some returned amphibious and estranged; some entered deep households; some were exploited or killed. Later horror tales collapsed these histories into claims that all Deep Envoys stole children for breeding. That prejudice must not erase the real crimes of individual courts, raiders or surface families who knowingly paid living children as a maritime price.

The exact price accepted by the founding druid, the reefback's degree of conditioning, the city's migratory circuit and the means by which Istran Melvo's fleet finally trapped or crippled it remain open. The best chronological fit for its destruction is the existing defeat of maritime opposition in 355 BR, followed by massacre, enslavement and dispersal rather than the extinction of every community connected to the city.

### Decision 11 — The price of motion and the lives owed to the sea

**Locked during author review:** the founding bargain combined two prices. First, the revived city could never anchor permanently, enclose a portion of the sea as its possession or convert a temporary refuge into an immovable territorial capital. Its deepest predecessor ruins remained surrendered to the sea, while the new city was required to travel with its reefback bearer and accept recurrent departures, flooding and changed landfall. The settlement could form relations with shores but could not own the current that brought it there.

Second, the sea retained a claim upon people of the wandering city. The druid knowingly accepted that saving the survivor population did not mean every person would be restored safely or remain upon the surface. At prescribed conjunctions or after particular maritime rescues, tide courts could demand a living return. Children became the principal objects of this claim because households and rulers treated future generations as obligations they could promise more readily than themselves.

Thalassor did not command a universal schedule of child sacrifice and did not declare children morally guilty. The foundational covenant created a dangerous category of debt; later surface governments, custodians and Deep Envoy communities interpreted and exploited it. Some children were surrendered outright. Some were taken as wards, hostages or apprentices in deep households. Some drowning children were saved only by transformation and thereafter counted by the tide courts as returned. Some seizures were ordinary kidnapping or predation falsely defended as ancient law.

This history makes the founder morally compromised rather than secretly innocent. The bargain preserved thousands of lives and made the wandering city possible, but it authorised adults to bind children who could not consent. Later traditions therefore divide between reverence for the druid as saviour, condemnation of the druid as the first child-seller, and claims that corrupt rulers expanded a narrower emergency debt into hereditary tribute.

### Decision 12 — A natural reefback made into living infrastructure

**Locked during author review:** the bearer of the wandering city combined a natural origin with ancient biological alteration. It was born as an extraordinary but material reefback, not created ex nihilo, incarnated by Thalassor or endowed with a hidden humanoid soul. The predecessor civilisation captured or acquired it while it was much smaller and spent generations using selective breeding, controlled feeding, hormones, grafts and Giant fleshcraft to prolong its growth and turn its shell into a mobile harbour platform.

The old regime cut channels through accumulated shell, cultivated buoyant reef tissue, attached command organs and conditioned the animal's migrations. This work did not make the reefback a city-sized person capable of consenting to a constitution. It remained an animal capable of pain, fear, learned routes and attachment. Treating those responses as machinery repeated the predecessor's wider moral error toward its living infrastructure.

The reefback survived the drowning damaged, partially overgrown and carrying fragments of the old biological systems. The Folk druid did not build it or simply command it through divine favour. The druid removed or quieted the worst control grafts, healed infected shellworks and negotiated currents in which animal, surviving infrastructure and refugee settlement could coexist. The resulting relationship was a managed symbiosis rather than freedom in any perfect sense: the city fed and defended the reefback and adapted to its migrations, while still depending upon inherited alterations that could never be wholly removed without killing it.

Later civic myth divided this history into cleaner stories. In one, Thalassor raised an island-turtle specifically to carry the faithful. In another, the druid tamed an ancient sea monster. In a third, the reefback willingly chose the city and judges every passenger. None is literally correct. Its natural life, coerced alteration and later reciprocal dependence all remain materially visible.

### Decision 13 — A Morlach founder

**Locked during author review:** the druid came from a Morlach sea-kindred rather than a Dovren harbour tradition or Belathi learned house. Their formative knowledge was that of seasonal sailing, exposed coasts, island fosterage, mobile households and survival across dangerous landfalls. This background made a travelling community borne by an animal migration culturally imaginable before any pact made it materially possible.

The founder remains one exceptional Morlach ritual specialist, not evidence that all Morlach served Thalassor or possessed comparable powers. Later residents of the wandering city could magnify the founder into the representative of an entire people, while Morlach traditions preserved competing accounts of a difficult traveller whose covenant carried both refugees and an inherited human debt.

### Decision 14 — The tide-borne vision and deliberate quest

**Locked during author review:** the Morlach druid was neither invited by the survivor settlements nor accidentally shipwrecked among them. He received a recurring vision through his existing devotion to Thalassor and deliberately sought the drowned site. The experience conveyed a real distant condition through tide-carried knowledge, but it was not a plain spoken command, a promise of moral righteousness or proof that Thalassor had selected a human prophet.

The vision arrived through sea-water, dreams timed to the tide and impressions carried in stranded marine life. It showed enough of the wounded reefback, drowned living structures and people enduring among the ruins for the druid to recognise signs and pursue a route. It did not explain the predecessor's complete history, the bargain's eventual cost or what political order should follow. He interpreted the experience as a summons and assembled the voyage by his own judgement.

Survivors therefore retained agency. The druid found an inhabited and disputed place whose communities could accept, resist or reshape his proposed covenant. Later founder traditions simplified negotiation and opposition into the claim that a chosen man arrived, awakened the sacred bearer and created the city alone. More critical accounts argue that he followed a distress signal, appropriated survivor knowledge and then attached his name to collective labour.

### Decision 15 — A vision of the city to come

**Locked during author review:** the vision's dominant image was not the reefback's pain or the immediate suffering of the ruin communities. The druid saw a magnificent inhabited island moving across the sea: towers and living reefs upon an immense shell, ships gathering around it, strange surface and deep peoples sharing its harbours, and landbound powers unable to hold it.

The vision was a possible configuration revealed through tide-carried knowledge, not a guaranteed future supplied with instructions. It concealed or rendered ambiguous the coercion required to rework the reefback, the lives owed under the bargain, the violence of the later pirate economy and the city's eventual destruction. The druid nevertheless treated the image as a future he was meant to bring about.

Later supporters cited the city's real emergence as proof that every choice made in pursuit of the vision had been authorised by Thalassor. Opponents answered that the druid saw an outcome, mistook possibility for command and accepted intolerable prices to force reality into the image. The vision can therefore remain genuine without absolving its interpreter.

### Decision 16 — From ambitious saviour to founding fanatic

**Locked during author review:** the druid began as a sincere visionary whose compassion was inseparable from personal ambition. He wanted to preserve displaced communities and create a refuge no land empire could surround, but he also believed the vision identified him as the necessary founder of a great new city. Early successes, accurate signs and the reefback's survival reinforced that conviction.

As negotiation, ecological limits and political opposition prevented the settlement from matching the vision, confidence hardened into fanaticism. The druid increasingly treated disagreement as failure of courage or understanding, regarded present suffering as the price of an already revealed future and accepted terms he would once have rejected. He did not begin with a plan to sell children or perpetuate ancient coercion, but he knowingly accepted the human claim and continued using alterations that could not be removed from the reefback without killing it.

The city was nevertheless not made by his will alone. Survivor expertise, Deep Envoy negotiation, Morlach crews and the labour of many households realised the project and sometimes constrained him. This leaves later memory with a credible dispute: the revered founder truly saved a population and created the wandering refuge, while the condemned fanatic also established the precedents by which future rulers subordinated living beings and unborn generations to civic survival.

### Decision 17 — The founder as the first living return

**Locked during author review:** the druid's rule ended when the sea claimed him as the first living payment under the covenant. Once the reefback city had achieved the essential form shown in his vision, a tide conjunction carried him from the surface into a local deep court. No publicly verifiable body returned, and the event did not supply an unambiguous moral verdict.

The departure may have been knowingly accepted, compelled by terms he had ceased to control or staged by the founder as the final proof of his revelation. Thalassor did not pronounce sentence or identify the act as reward, punishment or apotheosis. The sea merely enforced the changed condition and living return to which the druid had agreed.

Later custodians treated his surrender as the precedent that legitimised subsequent human payments: the founder had paid first, so every generation supposedly owed the same courage. Critics replied that a grown man choosing his own risk could not authorise rulers to surrender children. Deep households preserved incompatible accounts of his arrival, death or alteration, while surface cults variously remembered him as drowned martyr, sleeping navigator, first merman or prophet awaiting the city's final return.

### Decision 18 — No canonical answer to the founder's fate

**Locked during author review:** the founder's fate after entering the tide court remains permanently unresolved at the authorial level. No surviving witness, accessible archive, immortal founder or uniquely reliable Deep Envoy account establishes whether he drowned, was transformed, lived for a time below or suffered some other changed condition.

Contradictory traditions are all permitted to preserve evidence without any becoming a disguised answer. Surface custodians may teach his willing death; transformed communities may claim him as the first of their kind; hostile accounts may describe imprisonment or consumption; sailors may expect his return whenever the reefback completes a rare route. Encounters, relics and biological similarities can complicate the question but must not resolve it.

### Decision 19 — Founder dynasty, covenant council and captains' ascendancy

**Locked during author review:** government after the founder developed through three overlapping constitutional phases rather than beginning as a pirate republic.

First, the druid's household and principal disciples formed a sacred ruling house. They claimed hereditary custody of his vision, the right to interpret the covenant and privileged knowledge of the reefback's route. Their authority joined kinship, ritual access and control of the first living structures; it was not proof that the founder had actually appointed a bloodline. This regime regularised the living returns owed to the sea and converted an exceptional founding price into a governing institution.

Second, the sacred house lost its monopoly and authority passed into a covenant council. Survivor districts, Morlach ship-kindreds, infrastructure custodians, trading households and recognised Deep Envoy partners acquired seats or negotiated voices. The council separated route keeping, harbour judgement, provisioning and covenant interpretation so that no single household could again claim the founder's complete authority. Old dynastic descendants remained prestigious and sometimes regained executive office, but no longer ruled by uncontested sacred inheritance.

Third, ship captains accumulated political power because every migration, defence, evacuation and detached neighbourhood depended upon their crews. The council gradually developed into, or was overshadowed by, a captains' assembly. Admission rested upon command, contribution and armed following more than ancestry. Merchant houses, ritual custodians and deep partners remained influential, but captains increasingly chose war, sanctuary, raiding agreements and the city's temporary anchorages.

These were not neat replacements. Dynastic claims survived inside the council; council offices survived during captains' rule; some captains married founder-descended households or sought tide-court recognition. By the late pirate age, every faction could claim one authentic piece of the city's constitutional past while disagreeing about which institution was sovereign.

### Decision 20 — The hunger revolt and sacrifice of the sacred house

**Locked during author review:** the sacred dynasty lost power after a disastrous fishing and raiding season produced acute hunger. Failed catches deprived the moving population of its ordinary subsistence while unsuccessful or intercepted raids brought back neither food nor saleable cargo. Stores became political weapons, dependent vessels began to leave and the ruling household could no longer turn ritual prestige into reliable provisioning.

The dynasty answered the crisis through the institutions it had built. It protected its own stores, insisted that proper payment would restore favourable currents and prepared further living returns from less powerful households. The revolt began among hungry residents, crews and families threatened with surrender rather than as an abstract constitutional reform. Participants turned the dynasty's claim against it: if the ruling blood uniquely inherited the founder's covenant, then its members bore the first responsibility for satisfying the sea.

The rebels seized the sacred household and cast its available members into the sea as the season's collective sacrifice. This was a mass political killing expressed through covenant law, not proof that Thalassor demanded or rewarded their deaths. Some later traditions claimed that fish returned immediately; others recorded that hunger continued and relief came only when allied ships shared stores or the reefback reached better water.

The revolt destroyed the dynasty's monopoly but did not erase every collateral descendant, disciple or ritual text. The emergency coalition that allocated surviving food and stabilised the fleet became the basis of the covenant council. Founder-descended claimants could later return to influence, but none could plausibly restore the old household without confronting the memory that sacred rulers might themselves be selected as the city's lawful payment.

### Decision 21 — The covenant's violence exported onto outsiders

**Locked during author review:** the covenant council did not abolish the living tribute. It protected recognised citizens and member households by redirecting ordinary payments onto people without civic standing: captured enemies, enslaved foreigners, condemned criminals and people taken in maritime raids. Children among captured coastal populations were not categorically exempt.

The reform was celebrated internally as the end of dynastic predation because rulers could no longer seize a citizen household's child merely by invoking the founder. In material terms, however, it transformed an internal sacred levy into an external demand for captives. Raiding became part of the city's ritual provisioning as well as its food and commercial economy. Captains who returned with prisoners could claim that they had preserved citizens' families, while coastal communities experienced the wandering refuge as a child-taking slaver city.

Not every captive was sent below. Tide courts demanded different forms of living return; city authorities selected among prisoners for deep wardship, coerced transformation, exchange, labour, ransom or death. Corrupt judges could condemn politically inconvenient residents and strip their civic protection before surrendering them. Deep Envoys disagreed among themselves over whether such substitutions satisfied the original covenant, and predatory groups used the custom to disguise ordinary kidnapping and consumption.

Thalassor did not declare foreigners less valuable than citizens or morally endorse slavery. The city created that hierarchy to move the bargain's cost away from its own recognised members. This decision becomes one of the central causes of the long pirate age, regional hatred of the moving city and the willingness of later enemies to portray its destruction as liberation.

### Decision 22 — Citizenship through ownership of the moving fleet

**Locked during author review:** mature civic protection rested upon ownership of a recognised share in a vessel, mobile neighbourhood or indispensable fleet installation. A citizen was literally a stakeholder in the material system that allowed the wandering city to migrate, provision itself and fight. Residence, free birth, ritual participation or years of labour did not by themselves grant equal standing.

Shares carried votes, access to protected stores, standing before the harbour courts and exemption from ordinary selection as living tribute. Large holders divided interests among several ships and dominated the captains' assembly; small holders might possess only a fractional family interest tied to one boat or workshop. Shares could be inherited, pledged, lost through debt, confiscated after conviction or concentrated through marriage and purchase.

This system strengthened the transition from covenant council to maritime oligarchy. Captains and merchant houses could describe government as the collective rule of those who kept the city afloat, while sailors, labourers, dependants, resident foreigners and enslaved people performed much of that work without owning the legal instruments that protected them. Deep Envoy partners generally held treaty standing through their communities or courts rather than ordinary surface citizenship.

The distinction also made civic death materially precise. A hostile judgement could cancel or seize a person's share, stripping protection before enslavement, expulsion or surrender to the sea. Conversely, access to even a small lawful share offered one possible route by which an outsider might cease to be treated as cargo—if the existing proprietors permitted it.

### Decision 23 — Citizenship by exceptional grant

**Locked during author review:** an enslaved captive, resident foreigner or ordinary crew member could not acquire civic protection merely by serving for a fixed term or accumulating enough money to purchase a share on an open market. The captains' assembly could grant a lawful vessel share for exceptional service to the city.

Such grants produced celebrated freed pilots, defenders, negotiators and raiding captains whose careers supported the republic's claim that courage could overcome birth. They also made advancement dependent upon patrons, public acclaim and assembly judgement. Existing proprietors controlled which services counted as exceptional and could demand years of dangerous labour without ever recognising a claim.

A granted share was real property and conveyed genuine protection rather than honorary status. Its rarity made each elevation politically charged: sponsors gained a loyal voter, old houses lost relative influence and excluded populations acquired a possible champion. The same institution could reward resistance to invasion, successful ransom negotiation or profitable violence against outsiders, giving civic heroism no consistently moral content.

### Decision 24 — Licensed sea war and deniable piracy

**Locked during author review:** the mature captains' assembly regulated most major raiding through limited warrants. A warrant named permissible targets, waters, duration, division of prizes, treatment of captives and the share owed to city institutions. It might enforce a claimed debt, retaliate for seizure, serve a foreign patron or authorise attacks during a recognised conflict. The city described this activity as lawful sea war, reprisal or contracted protection rather than piracy.

Warrants did not make the violence impartial or humane. Wealthy interests shaped target lists, evidence of injury could be purchased and broad terms allowed captains to attack neutral or ambiguously affiliated shipping. Raided communities had no reason to recognise the legal distinctions of a mobile city that sold force and carried away their people.

Unlicensed piracy persisted alongside the formal system. Some captains exceeded their warrant, concealed prizes, forged authorisations or returned to seek retroactive approval after a profitable voyage. The assembly alternately punished, disowned or protected them according to political value. Foreign states likewise hired the city's vessels when convenient and denounced the same crews as pirates when alliances changed.

This arrangement joined merchant-republic law to a Tortuga-like maritime refuge without treating the city as a unified sovereign pirate nation. It also allowed raiding, privateering, smuggling, naval service and slave-taking to remain analytically distinct even when the same captain practised several during one career.

### Decision 25 — Paid opposition to Thalion and defence of maritime independence

**Locked during author review:** the wandering city entered the Atherian succession struggle through a paid alliance with interests opposed to Thalion, while also acting from strategic self-preservation. Velsar-aligned or associated coastal sponsors purchased convoy protection, raiding warrants, ships and experienced crews. The contract promised immediate profit, access to friendly stores and recognition of the city's maritime privileges.

The captains' assembly also understood that a consolidated Atherian power would eventually threaten its independence. Thalion's commanders were building dependable fleets, securing grain and timber, standardising obligations and subordinating ports that had prospered amid divided authority. A mobile market that sold naval violence, sheltered contraband and recognised only its own warrants could coexist with competing states more easily than with one expanding imperial order.

The decision was therefore neither ideological republican solidarity nor mindless piracy. Shareholders expected a profitable limited intervention that would preserve a divided coast. They underestimated Thalion's ability to sustain naval operations and Istran Melvo's capacity to attack the provisioning relationships upon which the supposedly elusive city depended.

Republican fugitives and other enemies of Thalion later reached the wandering city under older sanctuary customs, further raising the political cost of compromise. Their presence mattered, but it did not cause the original intervention. Later Atherian histories could consequently portray the campaign as the suppression of pirates sheltering traitors, while the city understood it as a contracted war for continued maritime autonomy.

### Decision 26 — The first sea-fire and the blackening of the pearl city

**Locked during author review:** Istran Melvo's victory in 355 BR included the first documented operational use of an Atherian equivalent to Greek fire. The compound could spread across water and adhere to wet timber, shell, rigging and living reef. It was projected or cast from specially prepared vessels at close range rather than functioning as effortless long-distance magic. Manufacture, storage and deployment were dangerous, expensive and initially limited.

The weapon was uniquely catastrophic against the wandering city. Its pale nacre, cultivated oils, dried surface growth, organic membranes, air passages and closely gathered flotilla carried flame through structures designed to remain wet and resist ordinary fire. Burning liquid entered channels and enclosed habitats; smoke filled living chambers; heated shell cracked; defensive organisms panicked or released toxins; people who entered the water found burning material moving across it.

The attack transformed the city's defining appearance. What sailors had remembered as a pearl-white moving island became an ebony-black ruin of charred nacre, soot-filled coral and burned shell. Later descriptions of the Black City, Black Reefback or an island darkened by judgement descend from this material event rather than a curse. Surviving pale layers beneath fractures preserve the earlier colour.

The burning killed a large portion of the resident population and destroyed or scattered much of the attached fleet. Death came through flame, smoke, collapsing structures, drowning, trampling, pressure failure and subsequent exposure rather than instantaneous magical annihilation. Atherian troops could still enslave captives, seize records and recover specialists after the assault; other survivors escaped in detached vessels or entered deep water.

This was not Thalassor punishing the city, nor did seawater make the flame supernatural or unstoppable. The victory required intelligence, specialised crews, favourable conditions and a means of approaching or confining the mobile target. The compound's later history, custody and influence on Atherian naval power require separate canon decisions. The precise operation that placed Melvo's fire-bearing vessels within striking distance remains open.

### Decision 27 — The prize ship inside the wandering city

**Locked in principle during author review:** Melvo placed the sea-fire inside striking distance through the World Egg's equivalent of the Trojan Horse stratagem. He did not present a literal wooden animal. His fleet staged a withdrawal or defeat and left behind an apparently valuable vessel that the wandering city's captains claimed as lawful prize or accepted as the vehicle of supposed defectors.

The prize was built or refitted with concealed compartments below its ordinary cargo spaces. Fire specialists, assault troops, signalling equipment and protected containers of the new incendiary remained hidden while inspectors found enough genuine cargo and convincing damage to support the deception. The city's own institutions completed the infiltration: rival captains disputed ownership of the prize, harbour officers brought it into the protected flotilla and salvagers moved it close to valuable living structures before its full inspection was complete.

Once secured inside the harbour formation, the hidden force emerged under favourable night, wind or tide conditions. It attacked local watch crews, opened or damaged access structures, ignited prepared targets from within and signalled Melvo's fleet to return. External fire vessels then struck a city already burning around its central anchorage. The operation joined deception, the target's prize economy and an unfamiliar weapon; neither the ruse nor sea-fire alone would have guaranteed success.

Later epic tradition transformed the vessel into increasingly symbolic forms: a hollow sea-beast, an ivory ship offered in surrender, a temple-barge or a treasure that consumed those who coveted it. The historical core remains a disguised prize vessel and concealed assault force. This becomes the setting's foundational analogue to Troy's horse without requiring Troy or reproducing its image literally.

### Decision 28 — The apparent Atherian flagship as irresistible prize

**Locked during author review:** the disguised vessel appeared to be a magnificent Atherian command ship abandoned or captured during Melvo's staged withdrawal. Visible fittings, command insignia, weapons, records and a genuine portion of military treasure made it valuable as both property and political trophy. Possession would enrich its claimant and publicly demonstrate that the wandering city had humiliated the emerging Atherian fleet.

Competition among shareholder-captains defeated caution. Rival claimants pressed to bring the vessel into the central harbour formation where its cargo could be inventoried under assembly authority and its command emblems displayed. Inspectors verified enough authentic material and plausible battle damage to quiet objections, while the concealed force remained behind false bulkheads and protected ballast spaces.

The stratagem therefore exploited the mature republic's institutions rather than a generic inability to recognise danger. Prize law, shareholder rivalry, public accounting and the political need to exhibit victory all encouraged the city to move the ship closer to its most valuable structures. Later moral accounts reduced this chain to greed; the historical explanation retains Melvo's preparation, institutional incentives and a deception designed for this specific enemy.

### Decision 29 — The Black Reef and the uncertain surviving bearer

**Locked during author review:** the reefback neither died neatly beneath the city nor escaped carrying the entire metropolis intact. Fire, panic and structural failure separated the animal from most of the accumulated urban mass. During a violent dive, roll or flight into deeper water, cultivated shell layers, attached harbour works, living buildings and much of the surrounding flotilla tore away from the bearer.

The severed city mass grounded in shallower water. Burned nacre, collapsed organic chambers, hulls, anchors and rapidly colonising reef growth formed a fixed black ruin and navigational hazard. Currents continued to rearrange its channels and scatter wreckage, while surviving biological systems made some apparently dead structures dangerous. The resulting reef remained a feared landmark, salvage ground and grave for generations.

The natural core of the reefback escaped alive but catastrophically injured. It retained its own shell and some inseparable ancient grafts rather than a functioning city. Its extreme longevity and predecessor alterations make survival beyond an ordinary animal lifetime possible, but no accessible account establishes when or where it finally died.

Subsequent sailors reported a scarred black island moving beneath storms, an immense shell surfacing near the ruined reef or a dark creature overturning ships in bad weather. These reports do not have one hidden solution. Some early sightings may concern the maimed bearer; others concern ordinary reefbacks, floating wreckage, deliberate wrecking, navigation error or storms interpreted through the famous catastrophe. Even genuine encounters do not prove vengeance or humanlike intent: a frightened, injured animal could destroy ships without becoming the conscious enemy imagined by folklore.

The historical tradition can therefore support a Loch Ness–like afterlife without preserving an intact pirate city or secretly immortal civic population. The fixed Black Reef is materially real; the Black Bearer is an unresolved cluster of sightings, losses and stories.

### Decision 30 — Destruction, Atherian captivity and divided survivals

**Locked during author review:** death and Atherian capture accounted for most of the wandering city's resident population after the 355 BR assault. Sea-fire, smoke, structural collapse, the reefback's violent escape and the breakup of the flotilla killed many before organised surrender was possible. Melvo's forces then captured people from disabled vessels, grounded ruins and surrounding water.

Captivity was not one uniform fate. Atherian officers separated navigators, shipwrights, biological custodians, translators, shareholders, political fugitives and ordinary labourers according to perceived value and danger. Specialists could be compelled into naval service or dockyard work; prominent enemies could be interrogated, executed or displayed; others entered slavery, forced resettlement, ransom exchange or dependent service. This selective survival allowed Atheria to appropriate skills without preserving the defeated city's institutions intact.

A smaller maritime diaspora escaped aboard detached vessels that had not been drawn into the central anchorage or cut free during the burning. These groups reached several ports rather than founding one successor city. They carried family histories, fragments of prize law, ritual objects and competing accusations of betrayal, but no single assembly possessed recognised continuity with the destroyed republic.

A second small survival moved below the surface. Deep Envoy allies, transformed descendants and functioning biological chambers carried or remade some refugees for life under pressure. Their survival did not preserve an underwater version of the city. They entered distinct deep households and local ecologies, where surface civic rank and vessel shares had uncertain or no legal force.

By the 350 BR boundary, the mobile polity had ceased to exist. Its people persisted only as captives, dispersed maritime households, members of deep communities and uncertain stragglers. The Black Reef, confiscated knowledge, regional family memories and stories of the Black Bearer became its principal historical afterlives.

### Decision 31 — Sea-fire as an Atherian state secret

**Locked during author review:** Atheria retained the water-burning incendiary after its first documented operational use against the wandering city. The formula, preparation sites, trained crews and delivery equipment became restricted military knowledge under central protection. Possessing one ingredient list did not suffice to reproduce the weapon safely; refining, proportioning, storage, pressure equipment and coordinated shipboard drill were all necessary.

The weapon remained rare. Production was expensive, accidents could destroy the carrying vessel and weather or handling errors could make deployment suicidal. Only a small number of specialised ships and crews could use it effectively. Ordinary rams, missiles, boarding, blockade and logistics therefore remained central to naval war rather than becoming obsolete after one innovation.

Thalion's regime could present the Black Reef victory as proof that disciplined imperial organisation defeated predatory maritime disorder. In practice, the state also benefited from captured specialists, records and materials taken after the assault. Later Atherian accounts minimised those appropriations and attributed invention, stratagem and victory wholly to Melvo's command and imperial purpose.

The technology's later spread, loss or refinement lies beyond the 350 BR stopping point and requires future-period review. At this boundary it is an emerging Atherian monopoly, not a common commodity available to every navy or adventurer.

### Decision 32 — Central circuit and exceptional long treks

**Locked during author review:** the reefback's ordinary range was a central Inner Sea island circuit rather than the whole sea. Recurring feeding grounds, water temperature, breeding or resting behaviour and established provisioning relationships drew the moving city through a broad but recognisable sequence of archipelagos. Ports could anticipate an approximate season of arrival without controlling the exact day or anchorage.

At irregular longer intervals, the bearer undertook major treks beyond that circuit. Some carried it east into Arkenan–Saronikan and later Atherian waters; others crossed a much greater portion of the Inner Sea. Ecological conditions, accumulated ancient conditioning, injury, current changes and human attempts to redirect the route could all contribute. No single civic authority possessed complete control over when a trek began or where it ended.

These exceptional voyages made the city internationally famous without placing it inside every maritime history. Communities on the central circuit knew it as a recurring political and commercial presence. More distant peoples might encounter it only once in several generations and remember the appearance as omen, invasion, market, pilgrimage or legendary island. Later storytellers often combined separate treks into claims that it visited every coast each year.

The distinction also constrained power. The city could raid, contract and trade widely during a trek, but its long-term food, repairs, information and alliances remained concentrated within the central circuit. Enemies unable to catch the bearer directly could attack those predictable relationships.

### Decision 33 — Destruction during an eastern trek

**Locked during author review:** the 355 BR assault occurred in eastern waters during an exceptional trek into the Atherian sphere. The city had moved east to fulfil its paid alliance, operate against Thalion's supply system and use friendly coastal provisioning relationships. It was therefore campaigning beyond its most secure central circuit when Melvo executed the prize-ship deception.

The Black Reef formed where the burned city mass grounded after separating from the fleeing reefback. Its position on or near an important eastern route made it a continuing navigational hazard, salvage site, grave and visible monument to Atherian victory. Imperial narratives could point to the ruin as proof that Thalion had made the sea safe, while survivors and hostile traditions treated it as evidence of treachery and mass destruction.

The original drowned predecessor site remained within the central island world. The predecessor ruin and the Black Reef are therefore two different places: one is the ancient colonial capital lost during the Unbinding, while the other is the burned body of its mobile successor left in 355 BR. Later stories sometimes conflate them into a city that drowned twice or a black island that returns to its own grave.

### Decision 34 — From Thalassorian theocracy to commercial unbelief

**Locked during author review:** the place of Thalassor changed across the city's three constitutional ages.

During the founder and sacred-dynasty period, the city was effectively a Thalassorian theocracy. The founder's vision, the reefback covenant and the city's survival authorised the ruling household. Public rites ordered migration, living returns and relations with tide courts. Inhabitants could address Thalassor as a god or divine patron even though the authorial cosmology retains Thalassor as an amoral Menhir sovereignty rather than a moral deity.

Under the covenant council, Thalassorian practice became the city's shared public religion and maritime law without excluding other cults. Different households, migrants and crews retained their own gods, ancestors and rites, while the covenant governed matters that affected the whole moving settlement: routes, refuge, flooding, deep diplomacy and the price owed to the sea. Ritual custodians remained influential but had to negotiate with civic and commercial offices.

During the mature captains' oligarchy, much of the ruling class treated the founder cult as antiquated symbolism or useful sailor superstition. Covenant terms survived as precedent, contract and harbour procedure. The assembly continued to exploit safe-current rites, Deep Envoy agreements and captive substitution while dismissing religious objections that limited profit. Traditional custodians lost authority except during danger, when captains abruptly demanded their expertise.

The final society was therefore not simply secular. It selectively disbelieved: shareholders rejected the old religion when it challenged their decisions but relied upon its inherited protections and imposed its costs upon outsiders. After the burning, devotees could interpret the catastrophe as the consequence of abandoned reverence, while the historical account attributes defeat to alliance, logistics, institutional rivalry, deception and sea-fire rather than Thalassor's judgement.

## Canon-uplift synthesis

The selected history now has the following bounded form:

1. A Nythalum-founded Giant–human colonial harbour subjugated earlier human inhabitants and developed living marine infrastructure rather than lunar or crystal-memory technology.
2. Dynastic civil conflict and a culpable forced deep-migration order caused most of the capital to drown during the Great Unbinding. A minority survived through flight, continued shoreline occupation and limited bodily transformation.
3. The original drowned site remained inhabited around its edges but never restored its old polity. Its living systems, transformed descendants and local Deep Envoy relationships endured in fragmented form.
4. Around 1000–900 BR, a male Morlach ritual specialist received a tide-borne vision of a magnificent moving island-city and deliberately sought the ruins. Ambition and compassion initially coexisted; successful interpretation later hardened into fanaticism.
5. He healed and reorganised the surviving biological systems around a naturally born reefback that the predecessor regime had enlarged and modified into living infrastructure. The resulting city was a mobile harbour and flotilla, not a literal free-floating geological island.
6. The Thalassorian covenant required perpetual movement and a living human return. The founder became the first payment, but his fate below remains permanently unresolved. His successors later converted the debt into institutional child-taking.
7. Government passed from a sacred founder dynasty to a covenant council after famine rebels cast the ruling house into the sea, and then toward a shareholder captains' oligarchy. Citizenship and protection depended upon a legally recognised vessel or infrastructure share, with exceptional elevation controlled by the assembly.
8. The council protected citizens by substituting captives, enslaved foreigners and condemned people for the covenant's living returns. Licensed raiding supplied cargo, ransom, labour and human tribute. This made the moving refuge a merchant republic to insiders and a pirate-slaver city to many neighbouring coasts.
9. The city normally travelled a central island circuit but undertook exceptional long treks. In 355 BR it entered eastern waters under paid contract to Thalion's enemies and to defend its own maritime independence.
10. Istran Melvo staged a withdrawal and left an apparent Atherian command ship as irresistible prize. Hidden assault and fire crews opened the attack from inside. The first operational Atherian sea-fire burned the pearl-white living city black.
11. Most inhabitants died or entered Atherian captivity. Smaller flotilla and deep-water diasporas survived without preserving the republic. Atheria retained sea-fire as a rare state secret and appropriated selected maritime and biological expertise.
12. The burned urban mass grounded in eastern waters as the Black Reef. The maimed reefback escaped, but later Black Bearer sightings mix possible survival with storms, other animals, wrecking and folklore. The original central drowned city and the eastern Black Reef remain separate sites frequently conflated in legend.

### Remaining work before promotion

- establish separate names for the predecessor polity, moving successor and Black Reef tradition;
- place the original drowned site, ordinary central circuit and eastern Black Reef against existing mapped geography;
- choose a narrower foundation date within the current 1000–900 BR range;
- identify which existing chronology, Atherian, Thalassorian, creature and regional overview pages require additions;
- audit language around Menhir agency, human continuity, Deep Envoy diversity and the material reefback classification;
- decide how much of this history is public knowledge, restricted scholarship or contested legend at the website's default date;
- preserve 350 BR as the stopping boundary and defer the later history of sea-fire, captive descendants and Black Bearer sightings.

No name, map placement or canon-file promotion is created by this synthesis.

## 1. The useful question

The weak question is, “Where can the World Egg put another Atlantis?” Nythalum already occupies most of that surface description: an oceanic island empire, monumental advanced infrastructure, crystal systems, a catastrophic sinking and dangerous survivals.

The stronger question is:

> Can the Thalassian inner sea hold a drowned **predecessor city or small maritime kingdom** whose destruction created refugees, disputed inheritances, dangerous ruins and the foundations of a later cosmopolitan port?

That formulation separates four elements which modern Atlantis stories often collapse:

1. the literary use of a lost empire as a moral and political counter-example;
2. the physical processes by which real coastal settlements become submerged;
3. the adventure grammar of a living or explorable lost civilisation;
4. the civic afterlife in which later peoples build above, around or against the drowned past.

The fourth element is where Absalom becomes useful. Pathfinder's Absalom is not a sunken city: it is a later metropolis founded on land raised from the sea around the Starstone. Its value here is therefore not “another Atlantis,” but the image of a **successor city whose centrality depends upon a dangerous object and a spectacular claim about what came from beneath the water**.

## 2. Evidence and translation controls

The dossier distinguishes:

- **Source fact:** supported by a primary text, archaeology, official production account or controlling published setting source.
- **Scholarly dispute:** a live argument for which the evidence does not permit one secure reconstruction.
- **Design reading:** an interpretation of how a story produces its effect.
- **World Egg translation:** a possible use constrained by existing canon; never a historical claim and not itself canon.

Three controls are essential.

First, Plato's Atlantis is not treated as a report whose coordinates need to be found. The story belongs to *Timaeus* and *Critias* and is designed to put an idealised ancient Athens into action against an imperial antagonist. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plato-timaeus/) identifies the Atlantis war as Critias's promised demonstration of the ideal society in motion. Christopher Gill's study of Atlantis as “designer history” likewise places it within fourth-century political and philosophical argument ([Journal of Hellenic Studies](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-hellenic-studies/article/abs/designer-history-platos-atlantis-story-and-fourthcentury-ideology/828B7BB540EB710BF9F9D6E3840F2456)).

Second, resemblance is not descent. Thera, Helike, Pavlopetri and Thonis-Heracleion show that ancient Mediterranean settlements could be destroyed, buried or submerged. None thereby becomes Plato's Atlantis. The existing [[Aegean and Cypriot Island Worlds#6. Thera|Thera profile]] already rejects the archaeological shortcut from Akrotiri to Atlantis.

Third, “lost mother civilisation” stories require exceptional care. They easily turn exchange into diffusion from one superior race, erase local invention and provide pseudo-historical ancestry claims. A World Egg drowned city should produce **regional debts, refugees and contested claims**, not explain all architecture, language, magic or civilisation.

## 3. Plato's Atlantis: imperial mirror before lost-world adventure

### Source fact

Plato locates Atlantis beyond the Pillars of Heracles and describes a rich island power ruled through related kings. Its capital is engineered through concentric land and water rings; its wealth and naval reach support an expansion which threatens the Mediterranean world. Ancient Athens resists it. Earthquakes and inundation then remove both armies and sink Atlantis, leaving dangerous shoals and mud.

The story's narrative weight lies before the sinking. Atlantis begins under ordered laws and apportioned rule, then becomes acquisitive and morally disordered as its better inheritance is diluted. The catastrophe closes a political contrast: enormous resources and technical mastery do not make an imperial society just. The [Oxford Classical Dictionary's Atlantis overview](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548548173) treats Plato as the earliest and controlling source; the elaborate identification of Atlantis with a recoverable Bronze Age site is a later project, not something archaeology has demonstrated.

### Portable structures

- **Constitution before catastrophe.** The lost polity is memorable because it had offices, districts, tribute and internal limits which could fail—not because it was generically advanced.
- **Water as planned infrastructure.** Rings, canals, bridges, docks, springs and irrigated hinterland make the capital a worked landscape rather than a palace floating in blue light.
- **Maritime wealth as imperial capacity.** Ships, tribute, metals, food and subject territories connect splendour to coercion.
- **The rival archive.** Several societies remember the same war differently; later writers use the lost enemy to argue about their own constitution.
- **Moral decline expressed through institutions.** The important change is not “people became decadent,” but that restraint, shared office and obligation ceased to bind rulers with great coercive means.

### Unsuitable borrowings

- a single lost civilisation as the source of all later peoples;
- concentric rings copied as the mandatory city plan;
- a destruction whose only cause is vague divine anger;
- “advanced” as a substitute for labour, food, law and political conflict;
- Atlantis as an archaeological certainty or Thera under another name.

## 4. Real submerged settlements: four different causal grammars

These cases matter because “the sea took the city” is not one event type.

### Helike: disaster, memory and opportunistic neighbours

The Achaian polis of Helike was destroyed in 373 BCE by earthquake and inundation. Scientific and archaeological work has complicated the popular image of a single enormous tsunami: seismic landslides may have dammed waterways whose later release produced the decisive flood. Sediment buried the city, while its territory was partly reoccupied. Ancient fascination with visible or remembered ruins preserved the disaster as a moralised wonder. A recent review also notes that neighbouring communities divided Helike's territory, so political predation helped make temporary catastrophe into permanent civic extinction ([Journal of Hellenic Studies](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-hellenic-studies/article/environment-sustainability-and-hellenic-studies/0374FE4A2EA03269E349735EEB26287B); [Radiocarbon study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/radiocarbon/article/locating-archaeological-horizons-with-14c-sediment-dating-the-case-of-the-lost-city-of-helike/3B5238EC241A47487761E4ED41184F3B)).

**Portable structure:** a city can die because relief fails, archives scatter, neighbours absorb fields and cult rights, and survivors cannot reassemble sovereignty. The natural event starts the process; political choices finish it.

### Pavlopetri: ordinary life preserved beneath shallow water

Pavlopetri in Laconia preserves streets, building foundations, courtyards and burials in shallow water. The settlement was occupied across more than two millennia before the end of its Bronze Age life. Its importance lies precisely in its ordinariness: it was a functioning community with houses and circulation, not merely a temple or royal machine. The University of Nottingham's project used digital survey and underwater excavation to recover its plan; UNESCO highlights both its survival and its exposure to anchoring, looting, pollution and construction ([Nottingham research project](https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/csps/research/research-projects.aspx); [UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/pavlopetri-named-2016-world-monuments-watch-site)).

**Portable structure:** a drowned city should preserve thresholds, graves, workshops, lanes and property arguments. Ruins are evidence of households, not a neutral dungeon prepared for adventurers.

### Thonis-Heracleion: a port landscape, not one frozen catastrophe

Thonis-Heracleion stood within the changing land-and-water system of the Nile delta. Excavation has recovered sanctuaries, canals, port basins, decrees, weights, statues and more than seventy ancient vessels. The ships entered the archaeological record through several processes: wrecking, ritual deposition, abandonment and structural reuse. Oxford's research stresses both the city's economic-religious life and the decisions inhabitants made within a changing harbour landscape ([Oxford Centre for Maritime Archaeology publications](https://ocma.web.ox.ac.uk/publications-1); [ship depositional study](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A85dd9538-16c6-4669-b0e6-b0b0d8f305dc)).

**Portable structure:** a submerged port can preserve immigration control, tax decrees, multilingual cult, obsolete ships, reused hulls and channels whose functions changed before the final submergence. “It sank” need not mean “everything stopped on one night.”

### Baiae: slow submergence and a living shoreline

The Roman coastal complexes at Baiae were gradually submerged through bradyseism, the vertical movement of land in the volcanic Phlegraean Fields. Villas, baths, mosaics, fishponds and port structures now sit below water while related architecture remains on land. The Italian archaeological park explicitly contrasts this gradual process with sudden destruction at Pompeii ([Italian Ministry of Culture](https://cultura.gov.it/luogo/parco-archeologico-dei-campi-flegrei-parco-archeologico-sommerso-di-baia); [Submerged Park account](https://progettoamphitrite.cultura.gov.it/aree-marine-protette/parco-archeologico-sommerso-di-baia/)).

**Portable structure:** generations can watch waterfront streets become unusable, move shrines and warehouses uphill, quarrel over abandoned property and reuse a receding district. A “sunken city” may remain socially continuous with the living settlement above it.

### Combined historical lesson

The best grounded drowning sequence has more than a wave:

1. a vulnerable geology, delta or engineered water system;
2. warning events and unequal knowledge;
3. failure of quays, wells, grain stores and evacuation routes;
4. deaths, flight and salvage;
5. neighbouring seizure of land, cult rights or archives;
6. gradual conversion of homes into ruins and ruins into claims;
7. later moral stories which compress the process into one night.

That chain supplies cause and consequence without requiring a second world-ending meteor or a duplicate of the Great Unbinding.

## 5. Disney's *Atlantis: The Lost Empire*: the living ruin

### Source fact and design reading

The 2001 film combines a 1914 industrial expedition with a surviving Atlantean population. Milo Thatch's linguistic knowledge opens the route, but the expedition's commander turns discovery into extraction by taking the crystal power bound to Kida and the city's survival. Disney's own synopsis centres the crystal, the kidnapping and the threat to the living city ([D23 film entry](https://d23.com/a-to-z/atlantis-the-lost-empire-film/)).

The production deliberately pursued a graphic action-adventure language instead of a conventional musical. Mike Mignola's angular design, First World War poster contrasts, large mechanical vehicles and a constructed Atlantean script made the surface expedition and lost city visually legible as different systems ([Computer Graphics World](https://www.cgw.com/Publications/CGW/2001/Volume-24-Issue-7-July-2001-/Staying-Tooned.aspx); [D23 production retrospective](https://d23.com/producer-don-hahn-explores-the-20-year-legacy-of-atlantis-the-lost-empire/)).

Its most productive premise is not the crystal aesthetic. It is the contradiction of a people living among machines they can operate only partially and texts many can no longer read. Material inheritance, linguistic inheritance and political authority have separated. The city is simultaneously a home, a ruin, a sacred system and an extraction target.

### Portable structures

- **A living ruin.** Descendants inhabit the place; explorers do not get moral ownership by discovering it.
- **Knowledge distributed across parties.** Outsiders can read one text; inhabitants know routes, customs and consequences; craftspeople understand components; rulers possess incomplete archives.
- **Expedition fracture.** Linguists, pilots, labourers, financiers, soldiers and looters enter with different motives and can change sides when the real cost becomes clear.
- **Infrastructure as covenant.** The central system sustains water, shelter, defence or memory and cannot be removed without killing the community.
- **Imperial extraction as the real monster.** The betrayal follows contracts, private finance, armed hierarchy and a market for stolen power.
- **Ancient design with ordinary life.** Monumental forms matter only when joined to farming, repair, children, burial and governance.

### Borrowings to refuse

- another blue crystal energy economy beside Nythalum's crystal memory systems;
- a princess physically fused with the one city battery;
- a single magic object which performs power, archive, healing, longevity and defence;
- unchanged ancient people waiting for an outsider to restore their literacy;
- the scholar-adventurer as saviour of passive natives;
- Mignola-derived vehicles, leviathan guardian and glyph forms copied as an aesthetic package.

The correct inheritance is **ethical and institutional**: who may interpret, enter, repair, sell or remove the past?

## 6. Pathfinder's Azlant: ruin archipelago and dangerous inheritance

### Source fact

Classic Pathfinder presents Azlant as the first great human civilisation of Golarion, secretly cultivated or manipulated by alghollthus. Rebellion provokes Earthfall: the Aeon Star shatters and drowns the continent, helps begin the Age of Darkness and leaves islands, atolls, sea canyons and magically preserved ruins. Later societies copy Azlanti forms and claim descent; explorers pursue artefacts while Mordant Spire elves guard the remains. The classic controlling treatments are *The Inner Sea World Guide*, pp. 203 and 210–211, *Lost Kingdoms* and the *Ruins of Azlant* Adventure Path; the [PathfinderWiki article](https://pathfinderwiki.com/wiki/Azlant) is useful as an index into those sources, not as sole authority.

The *Ruins of Azlant* campaign's strongest contribution is smaller than the setting-wide cataclysm. It begins with a new colony whose first settlers have disappeared. Survival, food, labour, trust and investigation precede the deepest lore. Paizo's official description of [*The Lost Outpost*](https://paizo.com/products/btpy9qya?Pathfinder-Adventure-Path-121-The-Lost-Outpost=) emphasises an abandoned settlement, survivors and the hazards of establishing a home; later volumes carry the expedition into the shattered continent and underwater ruins.

### Portable structures

- **Ruin archipelago rather than one intact dome.** Peaks, atolls, drowned roads, inaccessible cliff ruins and dangerous channels distribute discovery across a sea.
- **The colonial outpost as moral problem.** A later settlement needs food, labour, legitimacy and relations with existing guardians and underwater peoples; “empty ruins” may not be empty country.
- **Preservation is failing.** Exploration accelerates because systems, seals and records are degrading, forcing choices about rescue, custody and plunder.
- **Legacy claims produce present politics.** Architecture, blood claims and recovered objects become tools of empire long after the original society is gone.
- **The destroyer may remain active.** Catastrophe is not necessarily closure; surviving makers, enemies or custodians can manipulate excavation.

### Borrowings to refuse

- an alien species engineering humanity or inventing civilisation;
- another planet-changing impact beside established World Egg catastrophes;
- “first human civilisation” as a universal title;
- pure-blood descendants with superior bodies, eyes or destiny;
- one lost empire explaining every later style;
- aeon stones, exact Azlanti artefacts, Mordant Spire custodians or the adventure path's villains under new names.

Azlant's best World Egg use is therefore not ancestry. It is **the political ecology of an archipelago of ruins**: colonists, fishers, custodians, salvagers, displaced descendants and rival states disagreeing over a past none wholly owns.

## 7. Pathfinder's Absalom: a risen successor, not a drowned original

### Source fact

Absalom stands on the Isle of Kortos at the centre of Pathfinder's Inner Sea. In its founding story, Aroden—the last Azlanti—raises the island and Starstone from the sea, becomes a god and establishes the city. The Starstone later draws aspirants, pilgrims, institutions and conquerors. The city presents itself as the “City at the Center of the World,” while the Cairnlands preserve the works and wreckage of failed besiegers. Paizo's [Absalom and Starstone Isle overview](https://paizo.com/blog/absalom-and-starstone-isle) states this founding grammar; classic 1E civic detail is in [*Guide to Absalom*](https://store.paizo.com/pathfinder-chronicles-guide-to-absalom-ogl-pdf/).

### Portable structures

- **Centrality is a claim backed by traffic.** Pilgrimage, trade, migration, institutions and siege repeatedly make the slogan true enough to matter.
- **A dangerous remnant anchors the city.** The central object is not decoration; it reorganises law, land, cult and foreign ambition.
- **Failed conquest leaves geography.** Siege camps, forts, graves, causeways and abandoned engines make international history visible around the capital.
- **A founder story legitimates heterogeneous residents.** People with different origins participate in one civic myth without sharing ancestry.
- **The founder's absence reopens authority.** Institutions must govern after the singular founding figure can no longer settle disputes.

### Hard World Egg exclusions

- Aroden's mortal apotheosis cannot be translated literally. In World Egg canon, great power can impersonate divinity, a mortal legend can become the basis of an autonomous Mask, and institutions can authenticate miracles incorrectly; none makes a dead or living ruler the uncreated Lord or produces ordinary mortal godhood. See [[Idol Thrones, Regnants, and Cultural Masks]] and [[Creation and the First Rebellions]].
- No trial may objectively award divinity.
- A founder should not raise an island through a personal god-act unless the event is separately established through an existing creaturely power and remains bounded by that power's nature.
- “Never fallen” should be civic propaganda, not plot armour.

The most useful adaptation reverses the literal source. A later city does not need to be supernaturally lifted whole from the sea. It can arise on a **tectonically uplifted shore, engineered causeways, accumulated harbour fill and exposed upper ruins**, then tell a founder-centred story which compresses generations of communal work into one raising.

## 8. Nythalum collision audit

[[The Lost Civilization of Nythalum]] fixes the following:

- Nythalum was an isolated Giant empire on a great oceanic island.
- Lunarasyl and its monumental spires joined cities, launch vehicles and memory systems.
- crystal memory ecologies, bodily engineering and mechanical continuity belong to its inheritance;
- the Ascension Project was physical travel to the physical Moon;
- launch failure caused earthquakes, eruptions and the island's sinking;
- genuine Continuants, Reconstructions, machines and biological creations remain distinct;
- Nythalum's off-island annexes and [[Lesser Crowns of the Giant Age#The Moon Harbors of Caelene|Moon Harbours of Caelene]] fed the launch economy;
- Ulmaris and the Nine Bearings already occupy neighbouring archipelagic, pilot and client spaces without possessing rival lunar programmes.

These are not openings for reinterpretation. A new inner-sea drowned history must not acquire:

- a lunar programme, launch-spires or secret Egg White phase;
- another crystal-memory civilisation;
- machine immortality or indistinguishable copied rulers;
- a second empire whose fall repeats the same coordinated catastrophe;
- a “true Atlantis” title which demotes Nythalum to an imitation;
- automatic identity with Ulmaris, Caelene or the Nine Bearings without a separate canon decision.

### Could there be a sister civilisation?

Yes, but **sister civilisation** has to mean political relationship, not aesthetic duplication. Three forms remain compatible at research level:

1. **Peer rival:** a separate Giant maritime power interacting with Nythalum. This is the riskiest form because Ulmaris already fills much of the available role and another advanced island empire would crowd the Five Imperial Horizons.
2. **Client or treaty civilisation:** a locally ruled inner-sea state supplying Nythalum while preserving its own law, cult and harbour economy. Caelene already proves this category is possible. The new history would need to be one bounded member or former rival, not the whole harbour network renamed.
3. **Successor civilisation:** post-Unbinding human communities inherit a drowned Giant or mixed port without inheriting its creators' identity. This is the cleanest “sister” relationship: the connection is through ruins, refugees, stolen records and contested service, not equal super-technology.

The third form provides the most room for Package E's braided peoples.

## 9. Five bounded World Egg models

These are comparative models for author selection, not proposals of facts.

| Model | What exists | Main gain | Main collision | Research ruling |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Nythalum alone** | No second drowned centre; Package E encounters only distant Nythalum debris and later false Atlantis claims | Preserves uniqueness and economy | Gives the Thalassian no native drowned-city inheritance | Safe but leaves the user's desired inner-sea space unused |
| **B. Lesser Giant client-city** | One locally ruled port within Nythalum's supply world sinks during or around the Unbinding | Direct sister relationship and mixed Giant-human ruins | Easily becomes “Nythalum but smaller”; may duplicate Caelene or Ulmaris | Viable only with no lunar programme, no crystal core and a sharply local economy |
| **C. Post-Unbinding human city** | A human harbour or small kingdom prospers, then drowns through local seismic, hydraulic and political causes before 350 BR | Strongest historical causality; belongs directly to Package E peoples | Weaker ancient-supercivilisation atmosphere | Highly compatible; myth can magnify its actual scale later |
| **D. | **E. Hidden living city** | A surviving population inhabits sealed or air-filled ruins beneath the sea | Strong Disney living-ruin adventure and moral stakes | Collides hardest with Nythalum survival, Shell habitats and convenient stasis | Reserve for a small surviving quarter, not an intact nation |

### Best-fit synthesis

The strongest research synthesis is **D with a bounded element of C**, possibly inheriting limited material from B:

- an older inner-sea port or island polity had its own trade, shrines, households and coercive order;
- submergence occurred through a local causal chain—earthquake, ground failure, harbour works, flood, fire and political abandonment—not a second global apocalypse;
- some people died, some fled, some moved upslope and some were absorbed by neighbours;
- later communities built a new port on nearby high ground, harbour fill, shoals, causeways or exposed upper works;
- the successor became cosmopolitan because it controlled routes and arbitration, not because its inhabitants shared “Atlantean blood”;
- a dangerous drowned remnant became the focus of cult, salvage law, foreign siege and foundation propaganda;
- later legend condensed generations of labour into one founder who “raised the city from the sea”;
- different households claim descent from refugees, rescuers, looters, conquerors or original inhabitants, and those claims do not all agree.

This creates the emotional bridge the user identified—**Atlantis below, an Absalom-like city above**—while leaving Nythalum the unique lunar, crystal-memory, oceanic imperial catastrophe.

## 10. Consequences that make the model real

If any drowned-city model is selected later, development should answer material questions before naming it.

### Before submergence

- What fed the city, and where were its wells, granaries and timber sources?
- Was it one polis, several ports or a royal island with subject shores?
- Which households, captives, foreigners and dependent villages built its sea works?
- What did it control that neighbours wanted: a strait, arbitration, a sanctuary, storage, pilots or metal exchange?

### During the disaster

- What warnings were understood, ignored or concealed?
- Which district failed first: harbour wall, canal gate, cistern, lower market or sacred basin?
- Who controlled evacuation vessels?
- Which records, cult objects, hostages and grain stores were saved, and which choices caused later feuds?

### After submergence

- Where did refugees settle, and who refused them land or citizenship?
- Which neighbour seized the fields, fishing grounds or sanctuary presidency?
- Did the old government continue in exile, fragment among households or end entirely?
- Who owns salvage: discoverer, descendant, city, temple, crown or the dead?
- What is forbidden to remove because it supports the living shoreline or contains trapped persons?

### In the successor city

- Which foundation account is official, and whose labour does it erase?
- What central institution makes outsiders keep coming: harbour court, pilgrimage, archive, relic, market or diplomatic immunity?
- Which failed sieges or reclamation works remain visible outside the walls?
- How much of the older city is accessible by tide, diving, wells, tunnels or seasonal uplift?
- Do inhabitants call the lower ruins an ancestor, a conquered enemy, a holy grave or stolen property?

## 11. Fit, caution and refusal register

### Strong fit

- a drowned predecessor and later surface successor;
- an inner-sea city whose centrality comes from routes, courts and ritual trust;
- refugees and neighbouring predation after catastrophe;
- shallow ruins containing homes, streets, graves, workshops and port records;
- gradual and sudden submergence operating together;
- a living community holding moral rights against scholars and salvagers;
- conflicting founder stories and descent claims;
- a dispersed ruin archipelago with guardians, colonists and failing preservation;
- one dangerous remnant which reorganises civic law without granting godhood.

### Use with care

- a limited client relationship with Nythalum;
- an inhabited submerged quarter sustained by bounded infrastructure;
- advanced hydraulic, navigational or material craft;
- a founder remembered as “raising” the city, provided the phrase compresses communal engineering, natural uplift or help from an already defined creaturely power;
- catastrophe as judgement in later tradition, provided the historical account preserves physical and political causes.

### Refuse

- another world-spanning Atlantean mother empire;
- human civilisation engineered by aliens;
- pure lost blood conferring superiority;
- a second crystal-powered city or immortality project;
- a second moonward or Egg White Ascension;
- an asteroid or instant global flood repeating established catastrophes;
- mortal apotheosis or a test that awards divinity;
- an intact ancient nation awaiting an outsider saviour;
- archaeology which proves every later legend literally true.

## 12. Decisions for the author

1. Is the desired object primarily a **dead city**, a **living remnant**, or a **later successor metropolis** built over the dead?
2. Did the predecessor fall during the Unbinding, or was its destruction a later human-era event with local causes?
3. Should its relationship to Nythalum be no relationship, trade/client status, political rivalry or only later mistaken identification?
4. Is the predecessor Giant-ruled, human-ruled or a mixed port whose official dynastic story obscures a human majority?
5. What did it do unusually well if crystal technology, lunar travel and machine continuity are excluded?
6. Which established Arkenan–Saronikan city could inherit this history without being replaced, and would a wholly new site distort existing geography?
7. What survives below: ordinary neighbourhoods, one sanctuary, archives, a harbour machine, prisoners, a dangerous creature or only contested objects?
8. What does the successor city claim was “raised,” and what material history contradicts that claim?
9. Who has the strongest present moral claim to the ruins: descendants, current residents, displaced shore communities, religious custodians or nobody exclusively?
10. Which one visual or emotional note from Disney is essential once crystals, princess-battery and outsider-saviour structure are removed?

## 14. Research ruling

There is room in Package E for the Atlantis feeling, but not for a second Nythalum. The cleanest opening is a **regional drowned predecessor plus a later living successor port**, with material submergence, refugee history, salvage law and foundation propaganda doing the work. Its possible Nythalum connection should be limited to trade, clientage, ruins or later misidentification unless a future canon review demonstrates that even that connection adds more than it duplicates.

No city, people, founder, relic, location, date or catastrophe is promoted by this ruling.
