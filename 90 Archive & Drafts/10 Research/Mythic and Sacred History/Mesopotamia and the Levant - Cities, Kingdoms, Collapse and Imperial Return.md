# Mesopotamia and the Levant — Cities, Kingdoms, Collapse and Imperial Return

## Purpose

The ancient Near East offers several histories at once. Irrigated cities produced temples, palaces, workshops, written law and long-distance trade. Mobile herders moved through the same political world and supplied animals, transport, labour and ruling houses. Kingdoms rose from single cities, joined wider territories and then fractured. Conquest dispersed whole populations, while deported craftspeople, merchants and scribes carried skills into new homes. Later literature turned some rulers into heroes, monsters or ancestors without preserving a simple chronicle of their lives.

This complexity suits the early histories of the Jirahar, the Ahdari predecessors of the Ahl-e-Ahd and the Arqeshite riverlands. It also guards against a weak one-to-one translation. The Jirahar need not become Israelites under altered names, Arqesh need not reproduce Assyria, and the proto-Ahdari need not stand in for a timeless desert people. Each World Egg society can inherit a different combination of urban, pastoral, exilic and imperial pressures.

## Cities made from water and work

Southern Mesopotamian agriculture depended upon canals, embankments and organised labour. The fertility of the alluvial plain was an achievement sustained across generations. Fields required survey, seasonal cleaning and agreement over the order in which water reached different settlements. Temples and palaces accumulated grain, wool and labour, while households retained property, workshops and local relationships of their own.

Cities became political actors. Early dynastic rulers fought over farmland, canals, trade routes and prestigious sanctuaries. A victorious king might claim a divine appointment extending far beyond the territory he could reliably govern. The later Sumerian King List arranged several overlapping dynasties into a sequence in which kingship passed from one city to another. That literary arrangement is especially useful for World Egg: later historians can create a clean succession from a period which had several rival crowns at once.

The Akkadian kings demonstrated a different scale of ambition. Sargon’s later tradition carried him from obscure or subordinate origins into conquest, and Naram-Sin adopted an emphatic divine kingship. Their empire joined cities speaking several languages and reached into neighbouring regions. The state’s fall did not erase urban life or royal memory. Ur III subsequently rebuilt a highly organised territorial kingdom around taxation, labour and written administration. When that state collapsed, cities such as Isin and Larsa competed to inherit its authority before Babylon became dominant.

These cycles supply a durable historical grammar:

1. several cities share a civilisation without sharing a ruler;
2. one military household conquers the river system;
3. its officials present temporary dominance as the appointed order of the world;
4. the empire moves labour and concentrates water rights;
5. provincial dynasties survive its fall and claim its titles;
6. later scribes turn simultaneous rivals into a single line of legitimate kings.

Arqesh can use this grammar before its later Assyrian phase. Its first rulers may arise within an older river civilisation whose cities already possess canal law, household archives and local cults. Imperial Arqesh would then be a violent consolidation of existing societies rather than the civilisation that invented them.

## City and camp belonged to one political world

Ancient urban sources often described mobile groups as outsiders, rough people or threats. The surviving evidence also shows sustained integration. Amorite households entered cities, served rulers and founded dynasties. Old Assyrian merchants maintained family firms and expatriate trading communities across Anatolia. Pastoralists supplied animals and moved between grazing country, villages and markets. Political identity changed with marriage, service, drought and access to land.

The contrast between city and desert therefore worked as a claim about status as often as a description of livelihood. A ruler could denounce a rival’s followers as wandering barbarians while employing their relatives as soldiers or marrying into their house. A herding family might retain seasonal movement while owning urban property through a partner. A settled household could return to mobility after war destroyed its fields.

This pattern should govern proto-Ahdari and Tarkhan contact. The earliest well communities, tower towns and open-country camps form an interdependent zone. Grain, wool, salt, metal, pack animals and protection move between them. Raiding and slavery are part of the relationship, alongside adoption, fosterage, marriage and paid service. No civilisation develops in isolation, and no mode of life supplies a permanent ethnic essence.

## The Late Bronze international system

The eastern Mediterranean of the Late Bronze Age contained powerful royal courts linked through diplomatic correspondence, dynastic marriages, gifts, merchants, tribute and war. The Amarna letters preserve rulers addressing one another as brothers while lesser kings petitioned an Egyptian sovereign, quarrelled with neighbours and requested soldiers or gold. Ugarit stood within this world as a wealthy, multilingual port whose palace, merchants and grain stores connected inland production with maritime exchange.

Such systems created prosperity through dependence. A palace could obtain tin, copper, timber, horses, ivory and finished goods from distant regions. It also became vulnerable to failed harvests, interrupted shipping, unpaid troops and rival claims upon the same transport. Skilled workers and dependent farmers bore much of the cost. Royal correspondence naturally preserved the concerns of rulers more readily than the experience of labourers, captives and people outside the palace economy.

World Egg should use a comparable network during its later heroic Bronze Age. Tower settlements, Qahuri river courts, eastern canal cities, gateway ports and open-country carriers can exchange materials which none possesses alone. Bronze weapons and prestige vessels require long routes. A marriage between courts may secure a passage for one generation and produce a succession war in the next. Ships carry grain, mercenaries, pilgrims, captives and stories along the same coast.

## Collapse as a sequence of regional failures

The end of the Late Bronze Age did not occur everywhere on one day and cannot be assigned to a single people. Archaeology shows destruction, abandonment, continuity and reorganisation in different combinations. Climate research supports a prolonged arid phase in the southern Levant around the transition. Warfare, internal revolt, palace competition, migration and failures of exchange also mattered. The groups conventionally called the Sea Peoples are best understood within mixed movements of raiders, settlers, refugees and soldiers rather than as one nation which destroyed an entire civilisation.

Several places survived precisely because palace government disappeared. Households kept farming, pottery continued, local shrines remained active and smaller communities altered their exchange networks. Coastal trade recovered through new ports and shorter regional connections before later Phoenician expansion. Highland settlement increased in parts of the southern Levant. The political map became more crowded with smaller kingdoms, city-states and tribal coalitions.

The World Egg counterpart should therefore be a **Breaking of the Bronze Roads**, approximately c. 1450–1325 BR. It follows the Great Unbinding by many centuries. Its causes would include consecutive poor floods and rains, royal seizure of transport, warfare among river and harbour courts, interrupted metal routes, unpaid troops and movements by sea and land. A few palaces burn. Others are abandoned when dependants leave. Several towns survive and become more important. The collapse ends a particular network of royal dependence; it does not erase civilisation or release humanity from Giant rule a second time.

The period also creates a genuine heroic horizon. Captains defend or sack storehouses, displaced households seek new country, heirs return to ruined seats, and old supernatural bargains fail under conditions their makers never anticipated. Later singers combine several wars and voyages into the deeds of one generation. Archaeology and household traditions preserve a more uneven history.

## Iron Age successor societies

The centuries after c. 1200 BCE saw new and surviving states across the Levant and northern Mesopotamia. Phoenician ports continued older coastal traditions and extended maritime connections westward. Aramaean and Neo-Hittite kingdoms developed in Syria and Anatolia. Highland communities in the southern Levant became more numerous, while the origins of early Israel remain debated and are better explained through mixed local transformation than a single simple conquest.

Iron technology did not instantly replace bronze or produce these states by itself. Political recovery relied upon households, local rulers, religious centres, agriculture and trade. Alphabetic scripts widened some uses of writing, although literacy remained uneven. New royal houses adopted older titles and monuments because antiquity conferred legitimacy.

This phase provides several distinct World Egg developments:

- Jirahar route courts become permanent tower towns without surrendering their translocal relationships.
- Proto-Ahdari well coalitions produce market towns, caravan sanctuaries and territorial protector houses.
- Eastern river cities rebuild on older canal beds and compete to inherit the prestige of the Bronze kings.
- Gateway ports recover through smaller coastal networks and mixed households.
- Tarkhan open-country communities control transport and pasture beyond the reliable reach of walled states.

The resulting world contains cultural relatives on opposing sides of wars, cities whose inhabitants speak several languages and dynasties of mobile ancestry ruling settled populations. These are strengths rather than complications to be removed.

## Assyria, Babylon and imperial recurrence

The Neo-Assyrian Empire built a formidable military and administrative system. Its armies used infantry, cavalry, chariots, siege engines and specialised forces. Conquered rulers paid tribute; rebellion brought sack, execution and deportation. Entire populations were transferred to weaken resistance, cultivate land, build cities and supply skilled labour. The imperial army and capitals became increasingly multicultural through those movements.

Assyrian government also depended upon roads, provincial administrators, storehouses, scholarship and negotiations with local elites. Royal reliefs presented organised terror as proof of invincible rule. The violence was real, while its representation served political aims. A campaign inscription counted victories and punishments, leaving famine, disease and ordinary accommodation less visible.

Babylon possessed a different kind of durability. Its political independence changed repeatedly, yet its temples, scholarship and status as an ancient city retained authority. Assyrian rulers sometimes governed it through members of their own families; revolt led Sennacherib to destroy it in 689 BCE, and his successor rebuilt it. The Neo-Babylonian kings later inherited much of Assyria’s imperial territory and its deportation practices. Babylon became both captor and centre of learning for displaced communities.

The Arqeshite River Empire should combine these functions without becoming a costume copy. Arqesh can emerge from one northern canal city with an old merchant tradition, reform its army, absorb defeated forces and make deportation a deliberate tool of rule. Its kings move engineers, singers, metalworkers and priestly households. Their conquests make the imperial heartland richer and more diverse. Subject cities sometimes prosper inside the road and canal system while remaining unfree.

Qiryath’s destruction in 742 BR then becomes part of a wider imperial history. Arqesh requires the city’s strategic road, skilled households and public submission. The demand for a royal image and priestly hostages expresses that policy. Deportation scatters Jirahar learning through river cities; it also separates families and subjects them to forced service. The empire’s eventual fall around 690 BR follows succession war, failed flood management, rebellion and attack by a coalition of former subjects and neighbouring powers. A single liberator does not bring it down.

## Persian imperial accommodation

Cyrus’s conquest of Babylon in 539 BCE and the Achaemenid Empire supply a further pattern. Persian rulers governed a vast collection of peoples through satraps, tribute, roads, imperial service and local institutions. Cyrus’s Babylonian proclamation presented him in established Babylonian religious language as the ruler chosen to restore proper cult and reverse his predecessor’s offences. The Cyrus Cylinder is a royal foundation text and an act of legitimation, rather than a modern charter of universal rights.

This provides the best grammar for the immediate post-Arqeshite settlement. A successor coalition can issue return decrees, restore sanctuaries and recognise local laws while retaining taxes, garrisons and imperial advantage. Some deportees return; many remain where they have built households and livelihoods. Restored communities then argue over land, leadership and the standing of those who never left.

The Second Qiryath’s consecration around 660 BR should occur within such a world. Restoration becomes materially meaningful without making the new ruler a saint or erasing the interests served by the decree.

## Heroic and sacred memories

### Gilgamesh

The Gilgamesh tradition joins kingship, friendship, violence and mortality. Gilgamesh’s power harms his city. Enkidu’s arrival gives him a companion and rival; their victories include the killing of beings whose deaths carry consequences. Enkidu dies, and Gilgamesh’s grief drives a failed search for escape from death. He returns with knowledge rather than immortality.

For World Egg, the transferable grammar is a river king corrected by friendship and bereavement, followed by a journey which cannot overturn creaturely mortality. A Giant ruin, Idol promise or Egg White route may tempt him with false continuity. His return can improve one city while leaving the dead companion dead. This belongs naturally to Arqesh’s pre-imperial heroic memory or to a rival river city.

### Sargon and the foundling ruler

Later traditions made Sargon a figure of improbable ascent, including service as a cupbearer and a foundling story in which a child is placed in a basket and rescued. The useful element is the ruler whose humble or doubtful origin is rewritten once conquest succeeds. World Egg can place such a founder among canal labourers, caravan officials or deported households. Competing accounts may describe the same mother as a slave, priestess, foreign princess or invented shame.

### Ugaritic royal and divine conflict

Ugarit preserves a port-city world of international trade and a literary tradition in which Baal’s struggles with Sea and Death concern fertility, kingship and cosmic order. World Egg cannot import those gods as aliases. The grammar can inform coastal stories in which human rulers interpret rivalry among a storm Menhir, a sea court and an Idol Mask as a divine royal war. Their interpretations do not merge the beings involved.

### The Hebrew historical books

Judges, Kingdoms, Chronicles, Ezra and related Orthodox Old Testament books provide moral structures for this whole region: local deliverers with serious sins, ambiguous monarchy, divided kingdoms, prophetic rebuke, siege, exile, compromised survival and partial restoration. Their force depends upon preserving rape, kin-slaying, massacre, idolatry, famine, betrayal and repentance where these acts drive the narrative.

The World Egg application should preserve the moral questions while changing historical axes. A Jirahar judge need not replay a named biblical judge. An Arqeshite exile cannot become the Babylonian exile under a new title. The useful inheritance is the argument that power, victory and sacred possession do not prove righteousness, and that restoration never makes past guilt unreal.

## Research conclusions

1. The eastern world should possess urban history before Arqesh and before the Jirahar towers reach their mature form.
2. Mobile and settled communities remain economically and politically entangled throughout the period.
3. A later regional Bronze collapse belongs around c. 1450–1325 BR and must remain distinct from the Great Unbinding.
4. Arqesh should pass through a city-state and merchant phase before becoming a deportation empire.
5. Qiryath’s first destruction gains depth when placed within an imperial programme affecting several subject peoples.
6. The fall of Arqesh should lead to partial restoration, continued diaspora and successor rule rather than an empty east.
7. Heroic traditions should arise from particular river cities, ports and households. Later editors may arrange them into a cleaner age than the evidence supports.
8. Menhir, Idol and Patron activity can shape battles and royal claims without replacing human logistics, ambition or culpability.
