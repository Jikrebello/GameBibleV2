# Ancient Peoples before Empire — Synchronised Historical Ethnography

> [!note] Author-only development record
> The identity framework in this dossier was promoted through [[Ancient Peoples before Atherian Unification - Canon Integration Review]]. The dossier remains the source record for its reasoning.

## The subject of this study

The inner world of 350 BR contained peoples whose identities had developed through more than nineteen centuries of survival, movement and government. Some descended principally from the One Glass People. Others descended from human communities that endured the fall of Giant rule in their own countries. Conquest, adoption, slavery, marriage, manumission and shared work subsequently placed these ancestries inside the same households and cities.

No single word describes every form of belonging found in this history. An Enathi woman could speak Atherian in public, retain river kin and funerary observances, hold Caleran citizenship and marry a man whose grandfather had arrived as a slave. A Jirahar engineer in Arqesh could write the royal language, serve a canal office and remain answerable to a road house and the Orakha. A Vasharan subject could be Arqeshite by city and speech without becoming Vashari. These combinations were ordinary features of ancient life.

The familiar names used by later historians are retained for navigation. They do not all have the same historical character. **Enathi**, **Serathi**, **Vardeni**, **Qahuri**, **Jirahar** and **Vashari** describe peoples or broad cultural communities. **Qarzethi**, **Qiryathi**, **Arqeshite**, **Ortheian**, **Delkosi** and **Sarqeni** could combine civic, regional and cultural meanings. **Caleran citizen**, **Vasharan subject**, **Compact ally**, **royal dependant** and **enslaved person** were legal or political conditions. A dynasty such as House Ubar or House Tiravan was never an ethnicity.

## How ancient people named themselves

The oldest communities rarely possessed a single name embracing everyone whom a later historian places in one category. People named a household, landing, town, sanctuary, stretch of river or acknowledged group of travellers. Wider names became useful when several communities had to distinguish themselves from neighbours, negotiate abroad or claim a shared origin. Governments encouraged broad terms because they simplified taxation and military service. Conquered people could accept such a name for trade while rejecting the ruler's account of what it meant.

This dossier therefore uses three naming layers:

1. **Familiar name:** the stable term by which an encyclopaedia reader can find the subject.
2. **Period usage:** the names people used for themselves in a particular century, including town and household names where no wider endonym existed.
3. **Names imposed by others:** foreign, royal and administrative labels whose meaning might be resisted.

The terms *proto-Atherian*, *proto-Jirahar* and *proto-Ahdari* belong to authorial chronology. They are useful for tracing descent, but they were not nations waiting to receive their final names. Reader-facing history should use the local terms of the period after briefly explaining the scholarly label.

## Unbinding and abandoned worlds, c. 2300–2165 BR

The Great Unbinding destroyed a political order without reducing its human survivors to one condition. In the Aurin country, service households, captives and neighbouring settlements survived Arsuvan. Along the Sacred Basin, canal workers, burial fraternities and village councils took control of works formerly claimed by Giant courts. The Ubar cities retained channels, brick work and local temples. Vashari ancestors held valleys and sanctuary settlements beyond direct river rule. Older shore communities survived around the Gateway and eastern sea.

These populations were Orphaned in the later historical classification because they endured outside the organised Glass host. They did not understand themselves as one Orphaned civilisation. Their loyalties belonged to the people who shared food, work, defence and burial. Some groups had served the same Giant household and divided after its fall. Others had fought that household for generations. Refugees moved between them carrying techniques, cult objects and remembered injuries.

The Glass refuge gathered captives from several Giant horizons and formed them into the One Glass People. Their common life was real and acquired over generations. It did not erase every family memory. The inhabitants nevertheless emerged from the refuge with shared worship, public language, measures, camp offices and experience of collective provision that distinguished them from neighbouring survivors.

## Reassembly and fissions, c. 2165–1900 BR

After the Prophet's death, the Glass People remained connected while their practical interests separated. Households leaving towards the south were identified by captain, craft, destination and camp of origin. No common ancient endonym equivalent to *proto-Atherian* is established for this phase. The useful contemporary description was the **outward households** or a specific **southern league**, and even these expressions covered changing memberships.

The southern leagues occupied inhabited crossings, river landings and coasts. Some settlements arose through agreement. Others began with massacre, enslavement, hostage-taking or seizure of cultivated land. Older inhabitants entered the new communities as allies, spouses, adoptees, dependants, freed captives and slaves. The resulting society retained a substantial Glass inheritance without becoming a pure lineage.

Nearer the desert, road-using and well-holding communities remained interdependent. The people later called Jirahar increasingly organised belonging through recognised households whose marriages, burials and judgements remained valid across several routes. Territorial well coalitions followed a different political course. The wider history of those coalitions belongs to Package C. In the Gateway country, some of their descendants joined established shore populations and formed a new coastal society over subsequent centuries.

In the same period, the Enathi, Serathi and Vardeni became recognisable among the related peoples of the Aurin. Qahuri communities rebuilt Tenaret, Qemaret and Sakhret. Ubar canal towns and Vashari hearth domains developed without reference to Glass descent. Each history possessed its own marriages, rivalries and centres of authority.

## Hearths and first kingdoms, c. 1900–1400 BR

Longer residence made local names more important. Maritime households remembered the Glass, but their daily obligations concerned a harbour, ship, field or patron house. The term **Atherian** is proposed to have gained broad currency during this period among related maritime speakers and settlements. It denoted a cultural relationship before it denoted citizenship in one state. People continued to call themselves Enathi, Serathi, Vardeni or by a particular port while participating in this wider Atherian world.

The Qahuri Basin developed its first long royal house at Tenaret. Royal inscriptions spoke for the river and its inhabitants, although most people still identified themselves through town, occupation and temple. A common Qahuri identity became clearest in dealings with outsiders and during claims to rule both reeds. It did not erase the political importance of Tenaret, Qemaret and Sakhret.

At the Gateway, proto-Ahdari migrants, local cultivators, maritime settlers, captives and freed households became a durable coastal population. They shared markets, intermarried and maintained ports, roads and fields. This was a genuinely Braided formation because the participating communities created a common social world which their descendants inherited. They continued to distinguish town and country, and no single Gateway ancestry replaced the several strands.

Jirahar road houses acquired public names from work, descent or remembered founders. Tower settlements gave permanent residents a strong local identity without severing road obligations. Ubar river people identified strongly with their canal city and neighbourhood temple. Vashari speakers recognised related highland practices across Kavresh, Ruzhan and Daryam while remaining politically divided among hearth domains.

## Bronze roads and city worlds, c. 1450–800 BR

Cities enlarged the number of identities a person could hold. A resident could belong to an inherited people, a neighbourhood, a temple household and a civic body. Recognition mattered because it governed access to land, assembly, relief, burial and military protection. Enslaved inhabitants might live in a city for decades without receiving its civic identity. Freed people could acquire local belonging while remaining tied to former owners.

Atherian cities increasingly used shared speech, religious stories and maritime custom to recognise one another. Each city kept its own citizens and constitution. Caleran, Ortheia, the Arkenan ports and the Saronikan coast belonged to the same broad cultural world without forming a single polity. The Common Sky settlement gave several traditions an intelligible public arrangement at Caleran; adoption elsewhere varied.

In Qahur, crown and regional identity became sharper during the Twin-Reed divisions. A person of Qemaret could regard a Sakhreti as Qahuri in a foreign port and as an enemy of the proper crown at home. The Yasharet court incorporated immigrants and several languages into lower-basin rule. Later restoration propaganda called entire neighbourhoods foreign even when their families had lived in the Basin for generations.

Qarzeth's growing republic made **Qarzethi** a civic identity with privileges unavailable to many people living under its power. Esharat, Sarqen, Belqara, Hazreth, Tammur, Oresh and Hadrath retained their own communities. The phrase **Gateway people** described their shared coastal culture more accurately than it described equal citizenship.

First Qiryath gave the dispersed Jirahar their first large sacred city. A Qiryathi resident might be a gardener, porter, craft worker or foreign-born convert with little experience of caravan life. Road houses retained authority over members living elsewhere. The city belonged to the wider people without containing it.

House Ubar converted the name of Arqesh into an imperial designation. **Arqeshite** could mean a citizen of the capital, a person shaped by Ubar river culture, an officer of the empire or a foreign subject classified by its administration. Those meanings should always be specified. Vashari communities preserved their own identity under alternating cooperation and conflict with the river kings.

## Eastern empires and maritime republics, c. 800–520 BR

Conquest moved people while changing the value of names. Arqeshite deportation divided families and settled craftsmen, administrators and labourers in new districts. Some Jirahar deportees acquired property and office in the river cities. Their descendants could be Arqeshite in speech and urban habit while remaining members of Jirahar households. Others converted, married into local families or lost contact with distant kin.

The destruction of First Qiryath in 742 BR strengthened rather than ended the dispersed character of Jirahar life. Qiryathi survivors did not become the whole people, and those who remained outside the city were not lesser members. Return after Arqesh's fall created conflict between exiles, local survivors, converts and families associated with imperial settlement.

Vashari houses under Arezan Tiravan helped overthrow House Ubar. The resulting Crown of Roads and Dawns governed Arqeshite cities, Qiryath, highland domains and coastal ports. **Vasharan** therefore became an imperial term. A Vasharan official might be Vashari, Arqeshite, Jirahar, Qahuri or coastal by culture and family. Royal service encouraged common court practices without forming a single Vasharan ethnicity.

Ortheia belonged to the Atherian-speaking world but retained an upland royal and military identity. Its kings imposed authority on several Atherian leagues without making Ortheians and Calerans one citizen body. Eryndor's conquest moved Ortheian soldiers and families east. After his death, Delkos developed a mixed Successor court among local islanders, officers, Arqeshite specialists, Vashari connections and Atherian-speaking military settlers.

The Gateway Wars exposed similar distinctions in the west. Qarzeth mobilised citizens, allied towns, subject levies, hired troops and enslaved workers. These people did not possess the same stake in the republic. Child sacrifice was protected by part of the civic establishment and opposed by families and towns within the same culture. Atherian victory destroyed Qarzeth in approximately 520 BR while preserving enough of the surrounding country for its people to reorganise.

## Successor societies and the balance of 350 BR

The world of 350 BR possessed old peoples and recent political identities at the same time. The Qahuri remained a river civilisation divided among crowns. Qemareti, Tenareti and Sakhreti affiliations continued within the broader name. Vasharan officers influenced the Basin without making its inhabitants Vashari.

The Gateway country no longer possessed a republic. Qarzethi refugees and descendants lived among the surviving towns and abroad. Esharat, Belqara, Hazreth and Sarqen retained local identities. The kingdom centred on Sarqen joined these communities under a dynasty of Delkosi, Esharati, Gateway and junior Qahuri relationships. **Sarqeni** described subjects and participants in that royal country, not a new common ancestry created by decree.

Qiryath remained a sacred city under Vasharan supremacy. Jirahar communities lived in towers, river neighbourhoods, road houses and coastal ports. Arqeshite urban culture survived House Ubar through language, water management, schools, temples and household practice. Vashari remained the principal people of the eastern highlands, while the Vasharan Empire governed several peoples.

Around the eastern sea, Tasar and Ilyessa contained older shore families, Vashari and Arqeshite settlers, Jirahar houses and Atherian-speaking merchants. Their inhabitants generally identified through city before accepting a common coastal name. Delkosi identity combined a port and successor court rather than descent from Eryndor.

Atherian identity had become the broadest and most politically mobile term in the western and central world. It could indicate language, public cult and education, membership in an Atherian city or Caleran citizenship. These meanings overlapped without coinciding. Caleran's victory in the succession wars established a new imperial government in 350 BR; this dossier ends at that settlement.

## Boundaries with Packages C and D

Proto-Ahdari settlers appear here only where they participated in Gateway formation or interacted with Jirahar and Atherian routes. Their wells, open-country communities and wider self-understanding belong to Package C. Tarkhan appear as independent Orphaned neighbours, carriers, raiders, spouses, captives and allies. Their internal history also remains with Package C.

Folk and Ättar appear only at established points of contact with southern Atherian settlers and maritime societies. Atherian sources cannot define their internal names or political divisions. Package D will develop those societies in their own terms and return any necessary corrections to the contact boundary before the global 350 BR survey.
