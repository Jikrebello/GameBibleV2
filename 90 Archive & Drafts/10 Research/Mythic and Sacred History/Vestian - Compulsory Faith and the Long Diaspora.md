# Vestian, Compulsory Faith and the Long Diaspora

> [!note] Working proposal
> This revision develops [[Causality Pass - 04 Who May Authenticate a Sign, 0 to 411 AR#04.1 Why proof does not compel belief|04.1]], the Vestian portion of [[Causality Pass - 04 Who May Authenticate a Sign, 0 to 411 AR#04.3 249–398 AR re-sequenced|04.3]], and [[Causality Pass - 04 Who May Authenticate a Sign, 0 to 411 AR#04.4 Compelled professions fail to kindle: the Second Silence|04.4]]. It also uses the Raveth depositions and the Empty Charter clause from 04.6–04.7. The working dates are 377 for accession, 385 for the Profession and 398 for Vestian's death. The canon chronology still places the decree in 380 and his death in 395. [[Chronology of the Known World (AR Dating)]]

## 377 AR · Who keeps the record at Velos

After Lucar's defeat near Kellovar, Vestian kept the depots open and paid the surviving troops. He still needed the grain towns and the support of the people who distributed relief in Caleran. Some Folk communities negotiated to settle under their own elders and send troops when called. Families demanded the return of dependants sold under Lucar, and investigators began tracing some of the sales through merchants' receipts. Vestian guaranteed the terms of particular settlements and retained officers he needed to keep the frontier supplied. Their service agreements would later limit where he could enforce a common profession.

Vestian read Kellovar as a judgement on a realm that claimed two kinds of sacred authority. Cassian's reach at Velos still refused to freeze. Astrion had obtained a real answer from the Red Star in 350. Both signs had been entered in registers by the people whose authority they appeared to vindicate. Vestian wanted one authority to decide which claims the courts, towns and armies could act on. He put the bridge chapel and its winter register under episcopal custody in return for Caleran's public recognition and help supplying the towns and garrisons. The crown kept the river and bridge. The bishop's readers now kept the record that later generations would use to say what had happened there. ## c. 381–384 AR · The last petition and the eastern refusal

At the Celestial Forum, the High Augur asked leave to make one final petition before surrendering his college's seal. The college that had loaded Astrion's standard now asked the Red Star to show it still deserved its office. The augurs set no load and put nothing at hazard. The crowd waited through the appointed interval, then watched the seal go into the bishop's hands. A former augur later recorded that the request had offered the Star no field on which to answer. Vestian took the silence as grounds to proceed.

The order dissolved the colleges as chartered bodies and transferred their registers to readers appointed by the recognised bishops. A civil officer could still record an unusual event. Only an entry certified by a bishop in recognised communion could give a sacred claim standing in an imperial court or public oath. A bishop's readers took depositions, inspected objects and recorded terms, witnesses and rival explanations. Governors still ruled on property, offices and penalties. Disputes over which bishop the crown recognised now also determined whose testimony the governor could receive as a certified sign. Astronomers and surveyors could continue their work under civil appointment. [[The Conversion of the Empire]] · [[Overview of the Astratheon Religion|Astratheon Religion]]

The eastern road command held on to its augurs and the bronze standard from Astrion's test. The standard mattered to officers who had served under Astrion and relied on their own witnesses for appointments and oaths. An order from Caleran to surrender it threatened the standing of those instruments, along with the command's authority over the revenues used to pay its garrisons. Vestian cut the command's access to port grain and called on southern contingents under the recent agreements. At the decisive crossing the eastern augurs sought terms from the Red Star for the army and received none. Officers who could no longer promise either a recognised oath or dependable pay negotiated separately. Vestian guaranteed arrears and kept existing grants open to review. The command surrendered the standard and its surviving record. The last eastern college lost its charter. This was one civil war, fought over which authority could witness the terms on which the eastern army served.

### What a register can establish

The witnesses at Velos could measure the ice line. The augurs at Astrion's test could describe the load and the movement of the crack. Naming the source required other evidence. A Shell script, a Menhir acting within its terms, an Idol counterfeit and a genuine manifestation of the Flame could all leave visible effects. In the proposed legal history, the chartered colleges had decided which such claims entered public law. Vestian gave that power to bishops who had their own reasons to defend particular readings.

**Authorial ruling for this proposal.** Astaroth disclosed a structural fault in 350. The last college's request for validation fell outside the terms of that answer. The White Flame acts freely and answers within its commission. A recognised rite can carry civil consequences, and a genuine manifestation can confirm the particular commission under which it occurs. Neither supplies a verdict on a dynasty, a person's sincerity or an entire people. The working history uses this principle without depending on a decision about the Three Silent Days or the origins of the Great Purge. ## 385 AR · The Profession

The settlement in the east required officers to renew their oaths under the recognised bishops. Vestian made that settlement a rule of government. Magistrates, commanders and publicly paid teachers had to profess the Redeemer's faith before taking or renewing office. Recognised bishops' seals gave congregations standing to receive public stipends, hold endowed churches and supply chaplains. In directly governed towns the court extended the profession to household representatives, recording it on the lists used to recognise civic standing and access to public provision. The terms of particular frontier treaties and foreign crews' safe conducts still had to be honoured.

The court used a reception lamp drawn from parish practice. A person coming freely to baptism sometimes lit a lamp from the assembly's flame and saw it take a white fire. Parish witnesses knew it as a rare event. The chancery expected to see it repeated at public professions and circulated instructions to report it as confirmation of the settlement. Vestian believed a realm professing one faith would be ready for the Redeemer's return. The lamps gave him a visible promise to make to the towns.

## c. 385–389 AR · The Second Silence

In town after town the lamps at the required professions burned yellow. Parishes in some of those same towns, using oil from the same stores and tended by the same people, still recorded occasional kindlings at willing baptisms. The court circulated a handful of apparent successes. In cases the readers could check, several of those people had sought baptism before their summons arrived. That distinction mattered to the readers. The published returns counted everyone who lit a lamp under the decree.

Aurelium's readers collected dates, witnesses and accounts of ordinary flames alongside the reported kindlings. They sent the official totals and kept a fuller set of depositions under seal. Caleran ruled that a properly witnessed profession had civil force and let the chancery use its seal without deciding why the lamps had stayed yellow. Halmenth's bishop withdrew the reception lamp from compulsory ceremonies in his see. In Sermora and the gateway ports, some Redeemer congregations with Jirahar kin spoke of a second silence. Their families had preserved accounts of the sign of acceptance that ceased at Qiryath while the sanctuary's sacrifices continued. They knew how to testify that a rite had been performed and that its expected answer had failed.

Vestian's court blamed forbidden household offerings and accused Jirahar families of withholding their loyalty. That explanation let the chancery protect its account of the decree by asking governors to search homes and inspect communal records. It gave officials a reason to treat even some baptised households as suspect. The court ultimately replaced the lamp with a written profession under seal. Officials could certify another year's compliance from the signed returns. The ceremony stopped producing a public record of the absence that had set the inquiry in motion.

**Authorial ruling for this proposal.** The pattern is real. The Flame continues to kindle freely and gives no endorsement to compelled professions as an act of state. A person in a public ceremony may have sought baptism freely. A yellow lamp supplies no finding about that person's faith, and a white flame gives the state no endorsement of the law. Aurelium's sealed papers contain observations and testimony. Whether the full register survives to 1360 remains open.

## Raveth and the tower orders

At Raveth, baptised families with Jirahar kin lodged copies of depositions in a communal hearing house. Its clerks witnessed delivery and kept the copies alongside the town's leases and debt records. One deposition followed a single lamp from a compulsory profession to a freely sought baptism in the same parish. It burned yellow at the first ceremony and kindled at the second. Another set of papers put baptismal rolls beside the governor's summonses. It showed that officials had treated families as suspect on the strength of their descent even after accepting their professions. When the bishop's office refused to hear the case, the witnesses still had the hearing house's receipts.

A crowd burned the house. Some wanted their rent and debt records destroyed. The clergy who urged them on wanted the depositions out of reach. The governor ordered an inquiry and compensation, and Jirahar leaseholders brought receipts and duplicate entries from neighbouring courts. At Caleran, allied clergy argued that restoring the house would restore a rival authority over the Profession. Vestian withdrew the rebuilding order and let the inquiry lapse. Displaced clerks took surviving copies along the same routes they had used for marriage records and appeals.

The court had begun reviewing eastern tower charters during the inquiry into household worship. After Raveth it gave governors wider discretion to refuse renewal. Communities had to admit inspectors and surrender much of their communal jurisdiction to keep their charters. A refusal or lapse gave officials grounds to seize corporate property. The tribute rolls identified households. Astrion's abandoned survey and its petitions identified the people who maintained the cisterns and held claims to houses and fields. The treasury took stores for garrisons, neighbours acquired fields and some church foundations received rents. The extent of confiscation depended on local alliances, exemptions and the willingness of provincial officers to enforce older protections. By the end of Vestian's reign most surviving eastern towers had been seized or razed.

Some households secured safe conduct to Vashar or the independent dry-margin towns through kin who had left after 70 AR. Others entered port quarters or remained under local protections within the empire. Hosts needed copies of maternal descent, leases and burial claims to know whom they owed shelter or could admit to their courts. Enslaved people and dependants held against debts were among those least able to leave. A few departures used fixed Way-Gates with the agreement of the receiving community. Qiryath's gate stayed shut. [[Vestian Persecutions and the Long Diaspora]] · [[Historical Spine of the Jirahar]] · [[Prophet's Roads and the Underways]]

## The Empty Charter

Vestian ordered the remaining inhabitants of Qiryath removed. The garrison shut the entrances and recorded which homes, waterworks and plots had been vacated. Jirahar petitioners secured limited access by undertaking to tend graves, clear the paths and receive pilgrims. Custodians could enter under the charter. Their families had to find homes elsewhere.

Vestian replaced a permanent prohibition on rebuilding with the words *before the Mesakha*. He expected the Redeemer to return and wanted the city kept empty for him. The Keepers read the words as a condition under which their people might return. The charter named no authority to recognise the Mesakha. Later imperial canonists could claim the authority inherited from the augurs. The custodians could claim the tests that the Keepers had preserved. That is what makes a report of warmth beneath the Silent Gate a dispute over the right to rebuild as well as a claim about a sign. [[Qiryath Reqa]] · [[Mesakha Traditions]]

## What survives Vestian

The next rulers inherit bishops who can give sacred evidence legal standing, provincial officers who enforce that standing and a written oath that can be renewed without a lamp. The Raveth copies give congregations in the gateway ports a way to carry the dispute forward. Some later ask how the state could claim to hold the Flame through a ceremony it had made compulsory. That argument can feed the movement that reaches Halmenth in 411. The move from a claim about state rites to a claim about Isfir's own nature still needs its own history. [[The Velian Church]]

**Continuity to settle on approval.** This version gives the eastern command a surviving role after the challenge of 355–357, with enough revenue and authority to retain its augurs. Whether its leader was Pellan, and whether that settlement requires reopening the account of Savel's retreat, stay open. The proposed 385 and 398 dates need coordinated changes to the chronology and the histories that cite 380 and 395. The draft leaves the earlier Velos settlement under Cassian, his household executions, the Three Silent Days and the custody of the Nails for their own decisions.
