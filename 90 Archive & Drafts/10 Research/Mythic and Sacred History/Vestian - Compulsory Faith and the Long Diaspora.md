# Vestian, Compulsory Faith and the Long Diaspora

Vestian takes power in 377 AR after Lucar's defeat near Kellovar. He makes the Redeemer's faith compulsory in 380. His government dissolves the augur colleges, strips or converts Star temples, and turns older violence against the Jirahar into imperial policy. By his death in 395, most surviving eastern towers have been seized or razed, and Qiryath's remaining settlement has been cleared under the Empty Charter. The sequence and outcomes are canon. The proposed dates, orders, cases and frontier settlements below give them a political history. ## 377–382 AR · The army that gets Vestian to Caleran

The new emperor keeps the inner depots open after Kellovar and sends investigators to compare promised rations with the officers' delivery records. Some officers are removed and made liable for diverted grain. The army is short of experienced officers, and others keep their posts. Affected households press for the return of people sold during Lucar's government. The court recognises some claims where merchants' receipts or witnesses can trace a sale. Others remain unresolved across jurisdictions, and negotiations continue after the armies stop fighting.

By **382** Vestian has agreements with several Folk communities on and beyond the fort line. Some households choose to settle and cultivate specified plots under their own elders, supply named contingents when called and answer to imperial courts for disputes involving their neighbours. Others take safe conduct home, continue trading from independent territory or keep fighting. Previously displaced cultivators can seek review of grants. The agreement saves the treasury the cost of keeping every crossing under permanent occupation, and the court has to preserve protections for soldiers whose households and rites differ from those of Caleran. Officers call the peace a victory. Families still search for people the old command sold. ## 380 AR · The compulsory faith decree

Vestian believes that public authority must answer to the Redeemer. He uses the designation of recognised clergy, inherited from Cassian's restitution cases and widened under Lucar, to decide who can speak and hold property for the official Church. The proposed decree is announced in Caleran and sent to governors with a list of bishops whose signed letters establish public standing. It has three immediate effects.

| Provision | Enforcement and consequence |
|---|---|
| **Public authority professes the Redeemer's faith.** | Governors, paid civic teachers and magistrates must make a profession when taking or renewing office. City ceremonies replace public sacrifice with prayers and distributions administered under the new settlement. Those who refuse lose the office or stipend. Governors take the profession of an officeholder before witnesses. The law establishes no single day for the baptism of every household. |
| **Recognised communion receives the public rights of the Church.** | Publicly endowed worship, church title and official chaplaincies go through bishops accepted by the court. A rival Redeemer congregation can lose its building or relief income when its bishop loses recognition. Governors rule on title and can order handovers. Bishops retain their own processes for communion and discipline, and their decisions can still differ across regions. |
| **State support for Star sacrifice ends.** | The treasury cancels public sacrifice stipends and withdraws augurs from state ceremony. Cities have to keep the old temple supply contracts working or replace them. Instructions over the following years dissolve the augur colleges as chartered bodies. Civil astronomers, surveyors and calendar keepers can take new posts. Estate workers, singers and cooks have to bargain for wages and housing with new custodians. |

Foreign crews and treaty communities initially retain specified terms of service and trade. Existing Jirahar charters remain actionable at first, including some communal courts and burial rights. Those protections depend on governors and the crown agreeing to enforce them. The decree immediately closes some routes into public employment, and later orders do the work of cancelling protections and dispersing communities. Different treatment for recognised Redeemer congregations, Star institutions and Jirahar communities follows from the laws by which each held property and authority.

The Roman edict of 380 was addressed to Constantinople and identified the approved Christian confession and its episcopal exemplars. A law in 381 directed churches to bishops in the recognised communion. The code separately preserves restrictions on sacrifice and domestic cult, as well as a 382 order keeping a temple accessible under limits on sacrifice. Vestian's decree adapts the distinction between official confession, public rights and enforcement against other rites. Its Atherian provisions reach further than the Roman edict of 380 alone. The doctrinal judgement concerning Amaryah and the Redeemer belongs to Halmenth in 411. ## 381–395 AR · Who can enforce the law

Governors inventory endowed temples. The treasury keeps some productive estates to supply the army. Town councils petition to keep halls, clinics, wells and granaries working. Selected buildings pass to recognised congregations. Courts have to decide whether a temple tenant owes an old debt, who pays the wages of its workers and whether a plot held by a priest is private property. Some towns close a shrine without touching its storehouse. Elsewhere crowds destroy images and buildings before title or pay claims can be heard. Later prohibitions, proposed for **391–392**, extend penalties to offerings in private settings and make owners and local officials liable when they knowingly permit them. Household observances and unrecorded rural rites survive.

| See | What its officers do |
|---|---|
| **Caleran** | Its bishop supplies letters of communion and supports the public settlement. Relief stewards resist the treasury's attempts to make access to church bread depend on military lists. A bishop who can petition Vestian over army violence can also use that access to shield a cleric accused of leading a crowd. |
| **Aurelium** | Its copyists compare old deeds with claims of dedication and preserve evidence that some supposed cult lands belong to households. Some clergy support cancelling Jirahar corporate charters. Other readers copy petitions and receipts before provincial officials seize the originals. |
| **Halmenth** | Its stewards keep burial access and ransom channels open across confessions. When officials press them to identify the families they aid, some comply to preserve their grants and others move aid into household custody. |
| **Sermora** | Its bishop and port council negotiate safe conduct for crews and keep commercial sureties in force. Some shipowners carry exiles and books at a loss. Others demand passage fees the dispossessed can pay only by selling tools or signing long service contracts. |

From **c. 384–387** a regional commander withholds assessed grain and pay from Caleran, claiming emergency powers left over from the divided Aster government. Vestian confirms the towns' existing supply agreements and offers arrears directly to units that accept his officers. The coalition loses the ports it needs and breaks. In **c. 392–394** eastern officers and estate patrons again contest the appointment of governors and the disposal of temple revenue. Their supporters include people of more than one confession. Vestian calls in southern treaty troops and uses established garrisons to secure the road depots. Civil war makes those troops and their dependants more important to the centre while transferring the cost of mobilisation to port towns, cultivators and carriers. On his death in **395**, the capital retains one claim to rule. Field treasuries, local bargains and unpaid obligations leave his successors a state whose authority still depends on its regions.

In the Roman Empire, Libanius describes temple attacks and their cost to rural communities. Socrates records the conflict over the Serapeum, Theodosius's defeat of Maximus and his later war against Eugenius, with Gothic troops serving Theodosius. Ambrose's letter about the killings at Thessalonica shows a bishop pressing the emperor over responsibility for violence. These sources give the four sees room to differ among themselves and help explain the price of military reunification. ## 386–395 AR · How the Jirahar lose their protections

**c. 386 · The Raveth hearing house.** A crowd burns a Jirahar communal hearing house after a dispute over rents and debts. The governor orders a criminal inquiry and compensation using witnesses and title records, procedures available since Cassian. A local bishop petitions Caleran to withdraw the charge against the clergy who rallied the crowd. At court, allied clergy argue that restoring the house would restore a rival jurisdiction. Vestian withdraws compulsory rebuilding and allows the inquiry to lapse. Several debtors have reason to keep the destroyed registers from being accepted in duplicate. Jirahar householders seek copies from other courts, demand payment for lost leases and appeal to patrons with influence in the army.

**c. 387–392 · The orders against the eastern towers.** Vestian directs governors to renew only charters whose communal courts accept sharply reduced jurisdiction and whose properties the treasury can inspect. Officers treat corporate title as lapsed where they deny renewal. They use tribute rolls to locate households and older survey petitions to identify buildings, water rights and the families that maintained them. Estates, storehouses and rents pass to the treasury, military suppliers, towns and selected church institutions through different grants. Some governors secure exemptions for indispensable transport households. Others treat Jirahar descent as evidence of disloyalty even for baptised families. The crown accepts seizures in districts whose officials go beyond the published terms. By 395 most surviving eastern towers have been confiscated or razed.

**c. 392–394 · Qiryath is emptied.** Vestian revokes residence at the diminished settlement that survived 70 and orders the remaining houses cleared. The garrison seals approaches to the ruins. The **Empty Charter** forbids permanent habitation and rebuilding, with limited entry for tending graves, making safe the paths and receiving supervised pilgrims. Jirahar petitioners secure those exceptions by undertaking the work and naming custodians who can answer for it. The custodians may maintain the ruins while their families must live elsewhere. Astrion's old survey gives officials names and claims to work from. The Silent Gate stays closed, sacrifice remains suspended, and the destruction of the Second Sanctuary remains the event of 70. **Where people go.** Some families use old refugee contacts in Vashar and independent dry-margin towns. Some enter protected quarters in ports and larger imperial towns, while others depart in ship crews or reorganise into travelling companies. Households carry copies of marriages, maternal descent, debts and burial claims so receiving communities can establish obligations to people arriving apart. Hosts negotiate admission, plots and access to courts. Children, labourers held for debts and families with dependants are hardest to move. Only people near a usable Way-Gate with a willing receiving community can use it. Qiryath's closed gate leaves the roads and harbours indispensable. Jirahar communities predate the dispersal and continue on both sides of imperial borders. Their later contribution to desert scholarship rests on their own history and the terms negotiated with their hosts. In **388 CE** Theodosius initially ordered the bishop implicated in the burning of the Callinicum synagogue to pay for its rebuilding. Ambrose pressed him to amend the order and stop the inquiry. The imperial law of **393** states that Jewish worship was lawful and orders action against people attacking or despoiling synagogues. The Raveth case borrows the struggle over restitution and episcopal pressure. Vestian goes further through subsequent orders that dismantle specific Jirahar protections and uproot communities. That escalation belongs to Atherian history. ## The inheritance in 395

The Church can run relief and teaching across provinces, and it has records that outlast a governor's term. The court can also take a congregation's building by withdrawing its bishop's recognition. Provincial commanders have their own accounts and obligations to treaty households. In Qiryath, the imperial charter turns the displaced people's knowledge into a condition of access to their own ruins. The law that empties the city also leaves a narrow right of custody that later rulers inherit.
