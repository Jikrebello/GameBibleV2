# Historical Atlas of Vathra and the Northern Ground before Atheria

## Scope

The northern ground extends from the Glassward approaches and the western well country to the seasonal lakes and colder grasslands of the east. Qahur lies beyond its southern approaches. Roads from Qiryath and the Arqeshite river cities enter it from the south-east, while the northern Copper Stair passes lead towards mining districts and the farther interior. No frontier marked this country throughout antiquity. Water, winter pasture, safe crossings and the reach of a ruler's armed followers gave each boundary its practical form.

Between the twenty-fourth and fourth centuries BR, the region passed through three great changes. Vathra first joined its wells and grazing country beneath flying Giant courts. The Great Unyoking destroyed that dominion and returned control of movement to dispersed human communities. More than three thousand years later, after the period covered here, the First Catch transformed the eastern Tarkhan into a horse civilisation. The present account ends in 350 BR, while camels, carts, sledges and walking herds still carried most northern life.

Two human histories occupy the foreground. The ancient Tarkhan developed among Orphaned communities which survived outside the Glass refuge. The Ahdari emerged much later from the Near-Glass camp continuum and older well-country populations. They shared roads, marriages, fosterage, markets, raids and captivity. Each retained its own account of the Giant collapse and the Prophet's people.

## The country beneath Vathra

Vathra governed vertically. Its crown-cities crossed the Material sky along prepared currents, while a population many times larger maintained anchor towers, wells, herds, fields, quarries and workshops below. The western part of this system depended on the Hundred Wells of Narash and smaller cistern chains near the Glassward country. The central route crossed the deep-water stations later associated with Tergai. Farther east, seasonal lakes and open grass supported cattle, sheep, hunting and a small number of royal horse estates.

The sky cities required regular descent. Their hollow foundations needed timber, bone, metal and stone. Great woven mantles caught and circulated atmospheric pressure. Anchors joined each city to known places. Human pilots, weavers and route callers carried out the work which made departure possible. Most food and water reached the landing grounds by camel train. The splendid city seen from below was therefore the summit of a broad extraction system.

Vathran officials divided their human subjects by work and obligation. Narashi caravan crowns controlled water, sanctuary and bride exchange along western roads. Tether-town households supplied builders, smiths and mantles. Eastern camps moved royal animals between pasture and winter shelter. Captive workers came from the Copper Stairs, the southern basins and defeated northern communities. These populations spoke related and unrelated languages. They never formed a single Vathran people.

## The War of Altitude and the Great Unyoking, c. 2300–2260 BR

Vathra's last succession opened during the wider crisis of the Great Unbinding. **Ozhur IV**, ruler of the high crown-city Urzavad, demanded additional food, ballast and workers after familiar sky roads began to fail. His cousin **Varakesh of Dazhur** claimed that the lower cloud houses could preserve the empire by taking control of the route calendar. Each court stripped the stations below its rivals. A well might supply one city in spring and lose its remaining animals to another in summer.

The contest became the **War of Altitude**. War-platforms bombarded anchor towers. False beacons drew rivals towards thin air and violent weather. Giant rulers withheld rain from districts which supplied the opposing side. Dazhur cut loose an inhabited lower ward to preserve its own lift; the falling houses killed the people inside them and crushed part of the tether town beneath. Urzavad answered by destroying three western landing fields and executing their human crews.

Resistance joined several existing networks. **Seyra Vatan**, a human mantle-weaver raised in Kel-Orun, distributed altered knot patterns among the weaving houses. **Dorun of Tal-Kesh**, a route-master whose children had been divided between two royal households, coordinated drivers at the central anchors. **Meya of Narash** removed breeding camels from the tribute count and supplied the camps leaving the western road. Enslaved metalworkers forged knives capable of severing mooring cable under tension. Their conspiracy had no single headquarters, and many participants never knew one another.

The first open revolt began when Ozhur ordered Tal-Kesh emptied to lighten Urzavad's approach. Drivers cut the animal traces and opened the royal corrals. Families attacked overseers, seized stores and abandoned four anchors on the same night. Giant clients killed several hundred people at the eastern pens. Rebels retaliated against tether-house families, including children and human servants who had taken no part in the punishment. The memory of liberation never removed this guilt.

The sky cities met different ends. Urzavad struck a rival over Tal-Kesh and fell across the landing country. Kel-Orun descended under the control of Seyra's pilots and the Dynastic Giant **Kelvara**, who had turned against her royal kin after the massacre. The city survived its landing, although hundreds died when two foundation chambers split. Its stones, tanks and metal ribs became material for the first large post-Vathran settlements. Other courts drifted, vanished or entered broken routes whose later appearances remain uncertain.

The Great Unyoking ended Vathra's government. It also destroyed stores, bridges and animal populations upon which subject communities depended. Freedom arrived among wreckage. Several camps which escaped royal seizure died during the following winters because the anchor towns no longer distributed grain. Survivors remembered those deaths alongside the opened corrals.

## The Grounding, c. 2260–2050 BR

The first post-Vathran generations lived in a country filled with useful and dangerous remains. People stripped grounded buildings for cistern linings, roof stone and metal. Wind mantles became tents, saddle cloths and burial covers. A complete royal carpet might rise during the correct weather and drag an untrained rider towards its former destination. Most fragments had no surviving flight.

Human communities reorganised around whatever had endured. Narashi well houses kept camel herds and long-distance knowledge. Tether-town survivors retained metalwork, masonry and textile skills. Eastern families rebuilt cattle and sheep herds from animals released during the revolt. Hunters crossed districts in which no one could safely pasture stock because fallen weapons still answered movement from above.

The old royal horses almost disappeared. Vathra had concentrated its best animals at upland estates and military platforms, where fodder, healers and breeding records could be controlled. Horses died in crashes, were eaten during sieges, were taken south by neighbouring rulers or failed to reproduce in the harsher open country. Chariot houses preserved small lines in a few sheltered districts, but ordinary movement relied on feet, carts, sledges, donkeys and camels.

Political authority became local. Some settlements chose the people who had maintained their water and stores during the revolt. Others accepted former Giant officers because they still controlled armed followers. A few Dynastic Giants survived bodily and ruled short-lived enclaves. Human coalitions defeated or absorbed most of these houses over the following two centuries. Ordinary violence could kill a High or Dynastic Giant. Stories claiming that a hero permanently killed Vathra's Firstborn belong to later legend and conflict with the known nature of Titan continuity.

## The Near-Glass encounter and northern fission, c. 2050–1750 BR

The southern departure from the One Glass People drew many Ark-descended households away from the desert. Those who remained near the Glass continued to use several offices inherited from the Sojourn. Judges travelled between camps, provisioners supervised common stores, and household elders maintained burial and marriage relationships. The Prophet's commission passed to none of them.

Their northern neighbours already possessed wells, caravan circuits and memories of Vathra. Contact began through water and animals. Narashi guides sold passage to Near-Glass camps. Tarkhan ancestors hired themselves as hunters and escorts, stole herds and sometimes took refugees into fosterage. Near-Glass judges arbitrated disputes involving several roads; well rulers enforced access at a particular cistern with walls and armed retainers.

The difference widened across generations. Families committed to travelling judgement and dispersed custody entered the early Jirahar world. Families committed to defended water, guest protection and territorial oath formed the core of the Ahdari. Many older inhabitants joined the latter communities through marriage, adoption, service, manumission and the public assumption of shared obligations. Ahdari ancestry consequently became durable and mixed, while its dominant historical memory remained Ark-derived.

The division had no single day. The story known as *The Walking Court and the Wall That Stayed* preserves one local decision: part of a threatened camp carried its court away, while another part built a wall around food and graves. Families continued to marry across the resulting institutions. A travelling judge might depend on an Ahdari keeper's well, and a defended settlement might invite a Jirahar teacher to settle a blood dispute.

## Camel roads and first assemblies, c. 1900–1450 BR

By the nineteenth century BR, western camel breeding had enlarged the distance a household could cross between reliable waters. Camels carried grain, salt, cloth, ore and passengers; donkeys and carts remained valuable on shorter roads. A caravan of any size required investors, drivers, guards, cooks, healers, water knowledge and agreements with the people controlling each stage.

The Nine Mother-Wells of Tergai were deepened over several generations by Orphaned well houses, former tether-town workers and allied camps. Later Tarkhan tradition treated the chain as ancestral ground because Tarkhan-speaking communities maintained most of its outer stations. Ahdari households also worked and buried relatives there. Rights attached to labour, graves, seasonal use and negotiated protection rather than one royal grant.

Farther west, the old Narashi crowns lost much of their Giant-age authority while preserving influential maternal houses. Their marriage networks joined camel owners, gardeners, ransom brokers and sanctuary keepers. Some crown-mothers protected fugitives. Others sold captives or used sanctuary debts to compel years of service. The Hundred Wells remained a chain of related jurisdictions rather than one kingdom.

In the eastern country, cattle and sheep families met at seasonal lake shores. They raced carts, exchanged foster children, arranged marriages and heard singers recite the Great Unyoking. Assemblies settled access to winter shelter and organised retaliation against raiders. A leader's following depended on food, gifts and proven judgement. Descendants of Vathran officials carried prestigious genealogies, but a poor household could leave them if another camp offered better protection.

## The Breaking of the Bronze Roads, c. 1450–1325 BR

The northern road system belonged to a wider exchange world connecting Qahur, coastal ports, eastern river cities and the Copper Stairs. Bronze tools and weapons depended on metal moving over long distances. Palace buyers promised grain or silver to carriers who delivered ore. Royal chariot houses needed imported fittings, specialist wheelwrights and concentrated fodder.

Several failures converged during the fourteenth century BR. Poor floods reduced southern grain. Rival rulers seized caravan animals and paid soldiers with claims that no market accepted. Drought emptied marginal wells. Armed carriers kept cargo intended for collapsing palaces, while palace guards took food from villages to preserve metal shipments. Raiders attacked convoys already divided by disputes over payment.

The disruption ruined the surviving horse houses. At **Daram Ford**, a chariot lord ordered grain thrown away so that his teams could carry copper ingots through mud. Hungry porters killed him, opened the wagons and distributed the grain among nearby settlements. His horses were divided among rival captains and most died within a generation. Later Tarkhan songs called the episode the **Feast of the Copper Wheels**, preserving both the useful seed and the murdered ruler.

Western communities survived more effectively where control of food remained dispersed. The caravan houses lost distant profits, yet families still possessed camels, goats, gardens and local routes. Some protectors guarded their dependants through the drought. Others sold slaves and poorer kin before surrendering their own herds. The period became a source of honoured names, inherited grudges and competing claims to wells.

## Hearth crowns and market roads, c. 1325–800 BR

Smaller political worlds followed the collapse. Iron tools spread slowly from eastern and upland centres. They made ordinary knives, axes and agricultural implements more available, although good bronze retained ceremonial and military value. Settlements repaired short roads between dependable waters rather than attempting to restore Vathra's span.

The broad name **Tarkhan** first appears in surviving southern accounts during this period. Qahuri and Arqeshite writers applied it to several northern communities whose speech, fosterage customs and assembly practice were becoming mutually intelligible. Some northern singers adopted the word; others continued to identify themselves by a well, route or descent house. Its original meaning remains disputed.

The Ahdari name also widened. It described houses admitted to the obligations of several western wells and protected markets. By the ninth century BR, strangers could speak of Ahdari caravans without implying that every member shared one ancestor. Adoption, recognised marriage, manumission and the acceptance of blood and guest obligations allowed outsiders to enter. Enslaved dependants remained outside full protection until freed or admitted by a household.

One infamous event changed the western routes. During a sacred market season at **Saqara Well**, the protector **Harun Savel** concealed spearheads beneath date sacks after a satirical verse accused him of purchasing peace. His followers killed rival merchants, pilgrims, slaves and children who had entered without armour. Survivors avoided Saqara for nearly fifty years. Trade shifted towards the poorer wells of **Mazar**, enriching the settlement from which later northern market networks grew.

The period also produced local client kings. Qahuri crowns paid western houses to secure grain roads. Eastern city rulers employed Tarkhan scouts and camel carriers. Their subsidies supported poetry, metalwork and fortified camps, while drawing dependants into foreign wars. A ruler honoured as a generous host at home might return from service with enslaved river families in his train.

## Arqeshite pressure and the client houses, c. 800–690 BR

Arqesh's expansion reached the open country through roads, gifts and exemplary punishment. Imperial officers built stations at dependable waters, took children from allied houses as hostages and demanded scouts for eastern campaigns. They could seldom hold the deep interior without local cooperation.

Two related dynasts became the principal clients of the age. **Savan Uruz** accepted an Arqeshite crown, arms and an annual grain payment in return for guarding the north-eastern approach. His cousin **Demer Khol** received Qahuri support and controlled western camel stages. Their households exchanged hostages, intermarried and raided each other's unaffiliated dependants. Singers later presented them as champions of settled and mobile life; both built courts which depended on foreign money and captive labour.

Arqeshite payments failed during the succession struggles after 706 BR. Savan's soldiers killed him when he could not provide their grain. Demer attempted to absorb the unpaid companies and was defeated by camps whose relatives he had previously sold. The client courts collapsed, leaving trained soldiers, poets, smiths and rival claimants across the region.

Open-country leaders joined the coalition against Arqesh for different reasons. They wanted the imperial remount stations destroyed, hostages returned and access to disputed pasture. Some attacked retreating garrisons; others looted subject towns. Arezan Tiravan's Vasharan settlement recognised several northern leaders and purchased continued passage with gifts. It did not make Vashar sovereign over the whole country.

## From Arqesh's fall to Rethmar, c. 690–350 BR

The fall of Arqesh enlarged the power of market towns and caravan houses. Former imperial specialists repaired wells, made iron fittings and served local rulers. Jirahar return journeys, Qahuri rival crowns and Vasharan agents all used northern roads. The country remained politically divided because no single court could feed enough followers to dominate every ecological zone.

Rethmar grew where several western circuits met around dependable winter water. It possessed permanent storehouses, workshops and gardens, but most people present during a great gathering lived in tents and temporary compounds. Water keepers measured the reserve. Caravan captains negotiated road protection. Tarkhan and Ahdari houses heard disputes through their own customs and used respected mediators when a case crossed communal lines.

In 381 BR the Atherian commander **Lethren Ostar** marched towards Rethmar. He expected local leaders to provide water, animals and passage for a wider northern expedition. **Kharuz of the Stone Wells**, a Tarkhan leader, organised foot archers and camel scouts. **Tavisha**, an Ahdari water keeper, moved stores away from Ostar's approach and marked false camps with empty skins. Separated Atherian columns reached dry wells and were attacked before they could reunite.

Ostar died during the retreat. The victors took soldiers and camp followers captive and demanded ransom. Some captives entered local households as slaves when payment failed. Kharuz and Tavisha quarrelled over their treatment: he wanted to exchange officers first for weapons and animals, while she demanded that sick people and water carriers be released before negotiation. Their coalition endured long enough to protect Rethmar, then divided without creating a northern kingdom.

By 350 BR, Vasharan influence reached selected eastern roads. Qahuri crowns competed for northern grain and animal trade. Caleran merchants and commanders knew the approaches to Rethmar but had no secure route beyond it. The Tarkhan country contained well confederacies, grassland assemblies, client remnants and independent towns. Ahdari society included camel circuits, protected markets, fortified wells and households settled within neighbouring states. Neither people possessed a permanent central government.

## Work, family and pleasure

The long northern history rested on ordinary expertise. Camel breeders recorded mothers, temper and milk yield through songs and marked cords. Water keepers learned the depth and taste of each well at different seasons. Children practised loading small packs before they were trusted with an animal. Wheelwrights worked beside leatherworkers because a sound cart required both trades.

Households included close kin, adopted children, apprentices, freed dependants, clients and slaves. Marriage joined routes as well as families. A spouse might bring shares in a well, rights to a garden or obligations towards foster kin several days away. Women of wealthy caravan and water-keeping houses often controlled animals, stores and route investments. Captive marriage and concubinage remained forms of coercion where refusal carried violence or sale.

Gatherings offered more than judgement and war. People raced camels and carts, wrestled, traded coloured cloth, recited comic accounts of Vathran kings and arranged night-long competitions between singers. Children played an unyoking game in which the smallest player won by releasing the team before the cart owner returned. A common wedding practice joined a strip of old sky-cloth to a new camel rope, turning an object of royal movement into a sign that two households intended to travel together.

## Religion in the open country

Northern religion varied by settlement and period. Some Ahdari households preserved prayers to the Lord and fragments of the Prophet's teaching. Others joined local high-place rites, addressed weather powers or treated inherited standards as divine. Tarkhan communities possessed older Orphaned traditions, including sacrifices for rain, animal health and safe travel.

Actual supernatural encounters remained particular. A Zephyraxis pressure could lift a prepared Vathran mantle without approving the ruler who commanded it. Hecatara routes could shorten one journey and lengthen another. Menhir court persons, Egg White Patrons, empty images and Idol Masks appeared under similar human descriptions and require separate authorial identification.

Wrath found purchase in feud and humiliating retaliation. Greed fed upon the sale of water, captives and protected guests. Pride strengthened dynastic claims that one bloodline alone understood the sky. None of these pressures removed human choice. Chiefs arranged raids, families hid fugitives, priests selected victims and communities decided whether to repeat or abolish a rite.

## The northern balance in 350 BR

The northern world at Atheria's imperial threshold was populous, skilled and divided. Rethmar guarded the western approach through cooperation among several communities. Tergai joined ancestral Tarkhan wells to routes used by Ahdari and Jirahar travellers. Narashi houses maintained older western connections. Eastern Tarkhan assemblies dealt with Vasharan clients and the remnants of Arqeshite stations. Fallen Vathran structures supplied building material, danger and political memory across the whole region.

No army could conquer this world by winning one battle. It had to carry water, secure animals, bargain with local keepers and prevent its guides from changing sides. Lethren Ostar's defeat demonstrated the cost of ignoring those requirements. Thalion inherited that lesson when he began turning Caleran power into an empire.
