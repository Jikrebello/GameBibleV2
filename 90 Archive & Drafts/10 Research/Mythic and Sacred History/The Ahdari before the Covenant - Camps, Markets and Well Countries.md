# The Ahdari before the Covenant — Camps, Markets and Well Countries

## The people and their country

The ancient Ahdari formed in the Near-Glass and western well country between approximately 1950 and 1400 BR. Their principal ancestors came from the camp continuum left after the southern Glass-descended leagues departed. Older Orphaned well keepers, Narashi caravan houses, Tarkhan families, captives and immigrants entered the same communities over subsequent generations.

The shared people emerged through a durable social life rather than descent from one founding couple. Ahdari households recognised common forms of guest protection, water contribution, rescue debt, compensation and public oath. Their speech became the principal language of the western camel roads. Marriage, adoption, manumission and admission at a protected well allowed outsiders to belong.

The Ahdari of this period did not possess Al-Ahdiya or a Covenant empire. Mujalib lived almost two millennia after the endpoint of this account. Ancient households preserved portions of the Great Prophet's teaching, forgot others and combined inherited practice with local cults. Their antiquity deserves description in its own terms.

## From the Near-Glass camps, c. 2165–1950 BR

The Prophet's death left several practical offices among the One Glass People. Judges could settle disputes. Provisioners divided common stores. Captains defended a road. Elders spoke for households and remembered kin. None possessed the Prophet's universal commission.

The southern departures removed craftsmen, animals and armed households from the camps nearest the Glass. Those who remained faced a choice whenever water failed. A travelling court could carry judgement towards another well. A settled group could deepen one source, build walls and bind nearby households to its defence. Families tried both solutions for generations.

Contact with Orphaned neighbours changed the available choices. Narashi guides knew western water which the Glass descendants had never used. Tarkhan hunters could move through country where caravan animals found little pasture. Older oasis families possessed gardens, graves and shrine claims. These people entered Near-Glass history as allies, spouses, rivals, servants, slaves and hosts.

The first communities later called Ahdari formed where the defence of a particular well required continuing obligations. Households contributed labour, animals, weapons and stored food. In return they received water, refuge and a recognised share in decisions. The arrangement created protection and power. A strong well could exclude travellers, compel service and turn necessary water into inherited domination.

## The northern fission, c. 1950–1750 BR

The fission between early Jirahar and Ahdari institutions unfolded across many settlements. Jirahar communities invested in travelling judges, route memory and teaching which could cross political frontiers. Ahdari communities invested in walls, draw gear, guarded stores and compensation enforceable at a fixed place.

The difference sharpened during violence. A judge who left with the court could preserve names and decisions while abandoning fields. A well keeper who remained could protect grain and graves while losing contact with distant families. Neither institution answered every danger.

At one threatened camp, travelling custodians ordered a departure while water and food remained. Several households refused because their dead were buried beside the well and a second journey would kill sick relatives. They built a wall and survived the raid. The people who travelled preserved the camp's written decisions; the people who stayed kept its graves and stores. Later versions call the episode *The Walking Court and the Wall That Stayed*.

Intermarriage continued after such separations. A Jirahar judge might marry into an Ahdari water house and retain the right to travel. An Ahdari caravan could ask a road court to hear a dispute involving people from several wells. The fission produced related institutions and overlapping families rather than two sealed populations.

## What Ahdari belonging meant

The earliest communities named themselves by wells, markets and houses. **Ahdari** became a broad self-name during the Bronze-Road centuries. It first described households admitted to a shared body of protection across several western waters. By the ninth century BR, neighbours applied it to caravan and camp populations speaking related dialects and recognising those obligations.

A child born to an Ahdari household ordinarily belonged. A spouse could enter through a recognised marriage and acquire protection from the receiving house. Adoption joined an outsider to burial, compensation and rescue duties. A freed slave required a household, craft association or well community willing to admit them; release alone supplied no animals, shelter or protection.

Belonging did not erase rank. Wealthy camel houses controlled transport and could keep dependent families in debt. Water keepers held knowledge and public responsibility. Armed protectors expected shares from caravans and raids. Gardeners, artisans, hired drivers, slaves and people with no recognised descent possessed different claims on the common store.

Women exercised durable authority where property and expertise supported it. A water keeper could control keys, draw schedules and emergency reserves. Caravan widows inherited route shares and animals. Senior women arranged marriages and fosterage. Captive women and poor dependants remained exposed to forced marriage, concubinage and sale.

## Wells as settlements

A major well was a social landscape. The shaft and draw gear stood at its centre. Cisterns stored water when the source flowed. Gardens occupied lower ground where runoff could be used. Storehouses held grain, dates, salt and fodder. Graves marked the claims of households which had maintained the place across generations.

Permanent residents included water workers, gardeners, potters, leatherworkers and guards. Seasonal visitors brought tents and livestock. During a market, merchants, poets, judges, healers and foreign envoys enlarged the population many times over. A ruler controlled a well only while workers maintained the equipment and armed households accepted the allocation.

Water rights arose from several grounds: digging, repair, inherited use, burial, payment, hospitality and negotiated season. These claims could coexist. They also produced conflict when drought reduced the amount available. Powerful houses sometimes converted an emergency priority into permanent ownership. Communities remembered such seizures long after the original wall or bucket had disappeared.

## Camps and camel roads

Many Ahdari lived for part or all of the year in mobile camps. Movement followed pasture, trade and family obligations. One household might grow dates at a well, carry Qahuri grain during the dry season and move goats after rain. Another specialised in long caravan transport and owned no fields.

A caravan combined several interests. Investors supplied goods and animals. Drivers worked particular strings. Guards protected the vulnerable approach to water. Guides negotiated with route communities. Cooks, healers and repairers kept the company moving. Slaves performed much of the loading and water work in wealthy trains.

Camels extended routes and made western Ahdari houses valuable to settled states. They did not free travellers from water. A large company could empty a minor cistern and leave residents unable to survive. Passage therefore required advance negotiation, restraint in herd size and agreed compensation for damage.

Caravan wealth created towns. **Mazar** began as a group of secondary wells bypassed by prestigious traffic. After the massacre at Saqara, merchants chose its poorer but safer route. Storehouses, animal markets and repair yards followed. The town's later importance rested on this shift rather than a royal foundation.

## Hospitality and sanctuary

Hospitality protected travellers who could otherwise die beyond the next water. A host provided shade, drink, food and a period of safety. The guest accepted limits upon violence and conduct. The exchange could express affection, religious duty, political display and practical alliance at once.

Sanctuary attached to tents, shrines, wells, markets and recognised seasons. Its force came from reputation, retaliation and supernatural fear. A household unable to defend a guest could seek allies or compensation. A household which betrayed a guest might lose traffic and marriage partners for generations.

The protections also admitted abuse. A killer could reach a host before his identity became known. A wealthy criminal might purchase refuge unavailable to his victim's kin. Sanctuary debt could become forced service if a host demanded years of labour for one night's protection. Ancient Ahdari stories praised costly fidelity because betrayal was both possible and profitable.

## Raid, ransom and blood

Raiding formed part of the open-country economy. A leader gathered participants, identified a target and promised shares of animals or goods. Raids answered theft, enforced pasture claims, displayed strength or supplied a camp after drought. They also began through ambition and insult.

The violence reached whole households. Raiders killed herders, removed animals needed for milk and transport and carried away women, children and defeated men. Captives could be ransomed, exchanged, sold, enslaved or forced into marriage and concubinage. Sexual violence occurred during attack and captivity. A later genealogy could value descendants of such a union without making the original act consensual.

Blood vengeance gave vulnerable families a means of deterring murder where no ruler protected them. It could also transfer guilt to relatives who had opposed the killer. Compensation, fosterage, marriage and public submission offered ways to end a feud. A rich house could pressure poor kin into accepting terms which preserved the offender.

Poets shaped these conflicts. Praise advertised a protector's generosity. Satire exposed cowardice, hunger concealed behind wealth or failure to defend a guest. Elegy named the dead and demanded remembrance. A verse could close negotiations or force a chief to answer humiliation with blood.

## The Silent Month at Saqara

Saqara held a protected market during a season when participating groups entered without armour. Merchants sold dates, salt, cloth, tools and animals. Families arranged marriage and ransom. People brought disputes which could not be heard safely in an armed camp.

Around 1280 BR, a satirist accused the protector **Harun Savel** of buying peace because he lacked courage to defend his clients. Harun concealed spearheads beneath date sacks and allowed his rivals to enter the market. His followers armed themselves after the public recitation and attacked.

The killing included merchants, pilgrims, slaves, children and dependants who had no part in the insult. Survivors carried wounded relatives towards wells which had not prepared for them. Several died on the road. Harun seized goods and called the attack a defence of honour, but most caravan houses avoided Saqara thereafter.

Traffic moved towards Mazar. Harun's household lost the revenue it had tried to protect and divided over his succession. Later Ahdari chroniclers used the massacre to explain rules against concealed weapons at a market. The rule did not restore the dead or erase the profit Mazar gained from their flight.

## Religion before Al-Ahdiya

Ancient Ahdari religion contained several overlapping traditions.

**Name-Keeping households** preserved prayers to the Lord, parts of the Prophet's teaching and a refusal to bow before images. Their knowledge varied. Some recited old words without understanding them. Others used descent from the Glass people to claim superiority over Orphaned neighbours.

**High-place rites** addressed rain, safe birth, animal health and travel. Worshippers sacrificed valuable animals, poured water or oil and left marked cloth. A rite might engage a local court person or Menhir correspondence. Its effectiveness within weather or passage granted no moral judgement upon the people performing it.

**Stones and standards** carried the histories of households. Some were memorials or empty cult objects. A few attracted dependent Idol Masks. Wrath grew through vengeance standards; Greed through stones at which people pledged water, ransom and debt as property; Pride through genealogical objects said to prove an untouchable line.

**Patron bargains** maintained particular cisterns, warnings or paths through anchors to the Egg White. A Patron might demand goods, secrecy or service. Predatory relationships turned rescue into inherited debt. These bargains remained separate from human well law.

The Ahdari therefore possessed no common pagan church. Neighbouring camps could address different powers at the same high place and use similar outward forms. Each historical encounter requires its own supernatural classification.

## The boy beneath Ashal Stone

The oasis of **Kor Ashal** suffered three failed seasons around the eleventh century BR. Its ruler revived a local claim that burying a firstborn beneath the high stone would restore the spring. Priests selected the son of a debtor household which lacked armed kin able to refuse.

The child was killed and buried. Rain fell on the eastern road, while Kor Ashal's water remained brackish. The priests blamed an error in the family's descent and demanded another victim. The child's mother broke the altar face with a mason's hammer. The ruler executed her and imprisoned the workers who had helped.

Several caravan houses abandoned the oasis. Two sheltered the woman's surviving daughter, while another sold information about the fugitives. Kor Ashal declined as traffic moved elsewhere. Later reformers cited the killing as proof of widespread ancient depravity, although the event belonged to one cult and met organised resistance from contemporary Ahdari.

The supernatural evidence remains narrow. No rain fell at the spring, and no authorial source establishes that the stone contained a power. A Gluttony or Pride Mask may have encouraged the ruler's demand, but Package C does not need such intervention for the murder to occur.

## Markets, clients and foreign kings

Qahuri, Arqeshite and Vasharan rulers seldom governed the deep well country directly. They paid local houses to protect a road, took hostages from client dynasties and sent grain or weapons to preferred leaders. These relationships created small courts around caravan stages.

A client king drew followers from camps, towns, poets, priests, guards and foreign specialists. His language of sovereignty came from the state paying him; his practical authority came from feeding followers and protecting traffic. When payment failed, soldiers deserted, raided the client population or demanded land.

The rival courts of Savan Uruz and Demer Khol drew Ahdari into the Arqeshite age. Some served as camel carriers and market agents. Others lost relatives to raids or slavery. Harsh experience of client rule strengthened assemblies at several wells, while successful officers founded new households with foreign wealth.

## Ahdari and neighbouring peoples

### Jirahar

Ahdari and Jirahar inherited related Near-Glass histories. They shared family relationships, prayer fragments and road institutions. Jirahar judges travelled beyond particular jurisdictions; Ahdari keepers built enforcement around place. Neither difference prevented cooperation or feud.

### Tarkhan

Tarkhan neighbours supplied camels, hunting knowledge, animal medicine, escorts and fighting men. Ahdari houses supplied grain, market access and relationships with western towns. Marriage and fosterage created mixed households. Captivity and slave sale created other forms of mixture which later descendants remembered uneasily.

### Qahur

Qahuri crowns bought animals and transport, employed desert scouts and sought control of northern grain routes. Ahdari caravans carried river produce. Qahuri military houses also seized wells and settled captives. The relationship joined commerce, patronage and frontier violence.

### Gateway country

Between about 1700 and 1200 BR, Ahdari households entered established coastal settlements around the western gateway. They joined local cultivators, maritime settlers, captives and freed people in a new shared society. The Gateway population became a Braided coastal people with its own civic history. Its ancestry did not make the later republic an Ahdari state.

### Arqesh and Vashar

Arqesh demanded scouts, hostages and route access. Its deportations placed Ahdari families in river cities and foreign artisans at northern stations. Vasharan rulers inherited parts of this network after Arqesh fell and maintained influence through client gifts rather than continuous occupation.

## Tavisha and the defence of Rethmar

By the fourth century BR, Rethmar served as a winter gathering for Tarkhan and Ahdari circuits. **Tavisha** belonged to a family which maintained three reservoirs and supervised emergency distribution. Her authority arose from knowledge, recognised service and the willingness of visiting houses to accept her measures.

Lethren Ostar approached in 381 BR with an Atherian army and demands for water, transport and passage. Tavisha inventoried the stored water, moved filled skins away from the road and sent gardeners and children towards secondary wells. She marked empty camps where Ostar's scouts expected supplies.

Kharuz of the Stone Wells organised the fighting. His camel scouts and foot archers separated the Atherian columns. After Ostar's death, the victors took officers, soldiers, servants and water carriers captive. Fever entered the camp before ransom could be arranged.

Tavisha ordered sick captives and non-combatant water workers released with enough water to reach the first southern station. Kharuz objected because officers could purchase weapons and animals. The assembly accepted Tavisha's triage and Kharuz's ransom plan for the healthy officers. Other captives were exchanged, admitted to households or enslaved.

The episode revealed the limits of their coalition. Tavisha could govern water at Rethmar; Kharuz could command people who had followed him to war. Neither office supplied permanent sovereignty over the other. The gathering dispersed after the danger passed.

## Ahdari society in 350 BR

The Ahdari of 350 BR occupied a broad western world of camel circuits, wells, markets, gardens and settled quarters in foreign towns. Related dialects and shared obligations supported a common identity. Local membership remained strong: a person named a well, house or market when those details mattered.

The people contained pastoral families, caravan investors, drivers, artisans, poets, protectors, farmers, slaves and urban labourers. Some communities preserved Lordward prayer. Others maintained local cults or mixed practices. No ruler or priest spoke for every Ahdari.

Rethmar's victory had checked one Atherian expedition. Caleran merchants continued to seek northern goods and routes. Qahuri and Vasharan agents maintained clients. Tarkhan houses shared several western circuits. The political world awaiting Atherian unification was therefore connected, armed and capable of refusing foreign passage.

## Facts to preserve after review

- Ancient Ahdari are the principal Glass-descended people of the western well country, with durable incorporation of older populations.
- Their peoplehood precedes the Covenant by many centuries.
- Wells, camps, markets and caravan towns all belong to their history.
- Pagan practice is plural and coexists with fragmentary Lordward tradition.
- Raiding, slavery, captive sexual coercion and a specific child-sacrifice cult remain explicit.
- The child sacrifice at Kor Ashal never defines every ancient Ahdari community.
- Rethmar is a mixed gathering and Kharuz and Tavisha hold different forms of authority.
- No ancient Ahdari office inherits the Great Prophet's commission.
