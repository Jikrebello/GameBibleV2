# Romani and Traveller Peoples — Migration, Work, Persecution and Route Memory

## Purpose and terminology

The word *Gypsy* has been applied to several peoples who do not share one ancestry. It arose from a mistaken European belief that the Roma came from Egypt, acquired hostile legal and racial meanings, and is now rejected by many Roma. Some communities and organisations retain forms of the word in their own names. This study therefore uses **Roma** or **Romani** for the people of Indian origin, **Irish Travellers** or **Mincéirí** for the distinct Irish people, and the specific names of other communities. Historical laws and sources retain their original terminology when that language is itself part of the evidence.

This distinction matters for the Jirahar. Their history contains dispersal, specialist travelling livelihoods, guarded speech, hostile settlement policies and repeated attempts at assimilation. Those features can be deepened through several comparisons. They do not make the Jirahar a renamed version of any living people, nor do they permit separate Traveller histories to be blended into one romantic wandering ancestry.

## The Romani diaspora

### Origins and westward movement

Romani belongs to the Indo-Aryan branch of the Indo-European language family. Its older vocabulary and grammar connect it to languages of the Indian subcontinent, while later layers preserve contact with Iranian, Armenian and Greek-speaking societies. Linguistic evidence, supported by population history, places the deeper origin of the Roma in northern or north-western India. It does not identify a single ancient caste, kingdom or migration party with confidence.

The movement west took generations. Ancestors of present Romani communities passed through Persian-speaking and Armenian regions and spent a formative period within the Byzantine world. Greek supplied important vocabulary and grammatical influence. By the late medieval period, Romani groups were recorded in the Balkans and then across central and western Europe. Some presented themselves as pilgrims or penitents and carried letters of protection. Their hosts could initially receive them with alms and hospitality before civic and royal authorities began to treat their mobility, foreign speech and uncertain jurisdiction as threats.

There was never one uninterrupted caravan travelling from India to every later country. Families separated, settled, moved again and entered different political economies. The names Sinti, Calé, Romanichal, Kalderaš, Lovara, Kaale and many others preserve regional histories, occupational associations and internal identities. Religions also varied with surrounding societies. Language, kinship and shared experiences of exclusion sustained a broad relationship without producing a single government or uniform way of life.

### Work, settlement and mobility

Romani livelihoods developed around skills which could travel or serve dispersed markets. Metalworking, repair, horse dealing, animal training, music, entertainment, basketry, woodwork, seasonal agricultural labour and forms of healing all appear in particular histories. Occupational names sometimes became group names. They should not be mistaken for permanent castes: households changed work, combined occupations and settled for long periods.

Mobility could be chosen, inherited, seasonal, imposed or renewed after expulsion. A family might travel because its craft required many small markets, because land ownership was denied, because local authorities limited the length of residence, or because previous violence had made departure safer than dependence upon one patron. Most Roma today are sedentary. The wagon has nevertheless become so dominant in outsider imagination that it can conceal centuries of settled Romani neighbourhoods, urban trades and house-dwelling families.

Dispersed work encouraged several kinds of knowledge. A smith or horse dealer needed repeat customers and safe stopping places. Musicians carried songs across language boundaries. Families maintained relationships in several towns and knew which lord, parish, guild or constable might honour an agreement. Privacy protected people from seizure and also strengthened the authority of those who controlled introductions, marriages and routes. These institutions could shelter vulnerable members, but they could also concentrate power within senior households.

### Enslavement in Wallachia and Moldavia

For centuries, Roma in Wallachia and Moldavia were legally enslaved. Princes, monasteries and private owners treated men, women and children as property. They could be sold, transferred with estates, separated from family members and punished by owners. Some worked on agricultural estates or in households; others travelled to practise a craft while owing labour or payments to the person or institution that owned them.

The system endured into the nineteenth century. Emancipation came through successive laws rather than one act, and freedom did not supply land, compensation or security. Former owners received compensation in circumstances where the people who had been enslaved often received little material support. The history belongs centrally to Europe, not to a distant or exceptional barbarism beyond it.

This experience offers a severe but useful distinction for World Egg history. A travelling household can possess routes, skill and internal dignity while still being owned under the law of the country through which it moves. Mobility does not itself mean freedom. Release from legal bondage also leaves debts, lost children, occupational restrictions and inherited distrust which survive the decree.

### Expulsion, criminalisation and forced assimilation

Across western and central Europe, rulers alternated between licences, expulsion and punishment. Some laws ordered Roma to leave within a fixed period. Others declared their identity or manner of life unlawful, allowing execution, imprisonment, forced labour or transportation. Local officials could seize horses, wagons and children. The gap between written law and enforcement varied, but uncertainty itself made ordinary life vulnerable to extortion.

Forced assimilation could be more systematic. Habsburg policies restricted Romani language, dress and mobility, prohibited horses or wagons, compelled settlement and removed children for placement with non-Romani families. Similar ideas survived into modern welfare and educational systems. The state described the destruction of family and language as improvement. Communities also faced compulsory labour, institutional confinement and the deliberate breaking of occupational networks.

Spain's Great Round-up of 1749 attempted the simultaneous arrest and confinement of thousands of Roma. Men and women were separated, labour was coerced and families were damaged even where the programme failed to achieve permanent elimination. Such events demonstrate why a dispersed people may distrust censuses, fixed surnames and official promises without turning secrecy into an exotic racial instinct.

### Genocide

Nazi Germany and its allies classified Roma and Sinti through racial ideology, subjected them to registration, internment, forced labour, sterilisation, deportation and mass killing, and murdered them in camps and shootings across occupied Europe. The number of victims cannot be recovered exactly because records are incomplete and many killings occurred outside the camp system. The Romani genocide is often called the *Porajmos* or *Samudaripen*, although usage differs among communities.

The persecution did not arise solely from wartime chaos. It built upon older police files, stereotypes, local exclusions and administrative categories. Neighbours, municipal officers and national institutions could all contribute. Survivors then encountered weak recognition and delayed compensation. A World Egg analogue should preserve this accumulation: an imperial register prepared for taxation may later become a deportation list; a respectable hospital may refuse people who escaped a massacre; a child placed in a foreign household may survive and still lose family, language and name.

## Irish Travellers and other distinct travelling peoples

### Mincéirí within Ireland

Irish Travellers are a distinct Irish ethnic people with their own shared history, kinship and cultural traditions. The Irish state formally recognised their ethnicity in 2017. They are not a branch of the Roma. Their deeper formation remains debated, partly because hostile settled institutions preserved more written evidence than Travellers themselves. Accounts that derive them solely from people displaced by the Great Famine or by Cromwell are too simple, although conquest, eviction, famine and industrial change affected later mobility.

Traveller life developed within Irish society. Seasonal agricultural labour, tinsmithing, mending, horse dealing, market work and the movement of goods linked families to settled farms and towns. Tinsmiths supplied and repaired household vessels before cheap manufactured wares weakened that economy. Fairs joined trade, courtship, news and performance. Family connections could cover a wide country even when one household travelled along a familiar local circuit.

Cant, Gammon or Shelta provided a guarded speech whose vocabulary and use varied. It supported recognition and privacy in a society where outsiders often watched Travellers as potential criminals. Oral memory, nicknames and family knowledge could be more reliable within the community than official records which misspelled names or recorded only arrests, workhouses and roadside removals.

The history includes poverty, prejudice and harsh internal pressures. Senior relatives could exercise strong influence over marriage, work and disputes. Feuds might outlive their immediate cause. Women carried heavy responsibilities for household continuity and could face restrictions from both Traveller expectations and settled institutions. These realities require particular evidence and human characters. They cannot be explained by treating the people themselves as naturally violent, secretive or primitive.

Irish policy repeatedly pursued settlement and assimilation. Local authorities restricted stopping places, cleared roadside camps and placed families in sites designed with little regard for livelihood or kinship. Children experienced segregation, weak schooling or removal. The decline of travelling trades made dependence worse. State reports often described the community as a social problem before recognising it as a people whose rights and preferences mattered.

### Scottish Travellers, Romanichal, Yenish and occupational travellers

Scotland contains several distinct histories. Scottish Travellers possess long local roots and cultural traditions; Romanichal families share the broader Romani diaspora; Irish Travellers arrived through their own movements; and travelling showpeople developed around fairs and entertainment. Government and popular language often compressed them into one category. Earlier Scottish laws threatened expulsion and death, while later institutions pursued settlement and child removal.

The Yenish are another non-Romani travelling people associated especially with central Europe and Switzerland. Fairground families, barge communities, seasonal workers and modern travelling movements possess still other origins. A historical atlas should identify which people is involved before borrowing language, occupation or persecution. Similar relations with settled government can recur without proving common ancestry.

## What these histories contribute to the Jirahar

The Jirahar already possess a controlling origin. They descend principally from one Ark-derived branch of the Near-Glass camp continuum. Their portable courts and sacred roads began before their great deportations, and the destruction of Qiryath later enlarged an existing diaspora. Romani and Traveller histories therefore deepen **how dispersion works**, rather than replacing Jirahar ethnogenesis.

### A people larger than its travelling households

The strongest correction is demographic. Jirahar society should include tower citizens, village households, foreign-quarter families, sailors, craftsmen, advocates, soldiers and farmers alongside Road, Sea and Turning Companies. A sedentary Jirahar family does not become less Jirahar. Conversely, a non-Jirahar person with a travelling trade does not become Jirahar.

Several regional branches can preserve different accents, occupations, marriage customs and memories of departure. Outsiders may treat these branches as separate castes or mistake one famous travelling company for the whole people. Jirahar themselves may argue over whose pronunciation, genealogy or road custom preserves the oldest form.

### Route economies

Jirahar mobility needs a material basis in each country. Repairing tools, assessing animals, translating, music, medicine, river transport, seasonal labour, small metalwork and carrying news can sustain a route. Certain trades should rise and decline with technology. When cheap cast vessels destroy the work of travelling coppersmiths, families must settle, change occupation, enter illegal markets or leave for another region. The change can produce an actual historical generation rather than a timeless fantasy caravan.

Trust is economic. A household relies upon repeat customers, safe yards and introductions. A host town relies upon people who can repair a mill before harvest or move animals around a flooded road. Theft, fraud and violence occur as they do in settled society; they damage relationships precisely because those relationships matter. Jirahar law must therefore judge wrongdoing by insiders as well as protect the community from foreign authorities.

### Language, privacy and divided memory

The Jirahar can possess formal sacred and legal registers, regional vernaculars and one or more guarded road cants. These need separate histories. A cant may arise during an era of toll evasion or child seizure rather than descending unchanged from the Prophet. Arqeshite, Tarkhan, Ahdari and Atherian words can preserve actual periods of contact.

Privacy serves several purposes. It protects Way-Gates, escape routes and endangered families. It can also protect an abusive household head, conceal a coerced marriage or prevent a victim from appealing beyond one company. The same custom can therefore be remembered as survival by one family and captivity by another.

### Host-state power

Jirahar relations with states should include more than generic prejudice. Particular governments can:

- grant market and burial rights in exchange for taxes or technical service;
- own Jirahar households under slave law while permitting them to travel for work;
- expel companies and seize their animals;
- compel settlement while destroying the trade that could support it;
- remove children into foreign religious or military households;
- use census lists for deportation;
- recruit translators, musicians, physicians and intelligence carriers;
- protect one branch while outlawing another;
- turn a tolerated quarter into a prison during war.

These policies produce different Jirahar relationships with authority. A tower whose charter saved refugees may defend the crown that issued it. A family whose children were taken by the same dynasty may regard that loyalty as betrayal.

### Internal authority and fault

Persecution does not make every Jirahar institution righteous. Wealthy route houses can monopolise patronage, exclude converts, underpay dependants and arrange marriages for commercial advantage. A tower council may sacrifice a remote company to protect its own charter. A travelling captain may answer insult with collective retaliation. An informer may act from greed, fear, love or an attempt to expose genuine abuse.

These faults should arise from particular people, offices and pressures. They must never become a racial explanation of criminality. The dramatic strength lies in the conflict between institutions built for survival and the people those institutions sometimes crush.

## Material for later historical development

### The generation of seized children

A successor kingdom permits Jirahar craftsmen to remain but forbids the children of travelling households to move with them. Officials place children in military, monastic and artisan schools. Some are beaten for speaking their family dialect. Others gain education and later return as advocates, officers or strangers unable to remember their kin. The event permanently divides one tower's teaching about foreign learning.

### The market that needed its outlaws

A city expels a Jirahar metalworking circuit after a theft panic. During the following winter its pumps and mill fittings fail. The council secretly recalls the same workers without restoring their legal status, forcing them to choose between saving hungry customers and accepting arrest once the repairs are complete. A local guild member hides their families and loses his workshop.

### The road name that crossed an empire

One road-company name appears in several languages across countries whose chronicles claim the Jirahar arrived at different dates. The name belongs partly to a trade, partly to an adoption network and partly to descendants of deportees. Reconstructing it reveals several migrations rather than one legendary caravan.

### The respectable register

A reforming government records Jirahar families to distribute winter food and schooling. A later ruler uses the same register to seize horses, close camps and deport households. The clerks who created it divide between denial, resistance and collaboration. The episode gives later communities concrete reasons to argue about registration without turning every written record into another mystical relic.

## Comparative boundaries

1. The Jirahar are Ark-derived. Roma originated in South Asia, while Irish Travellers formed within Ireland. No ancestry is transferred.
2. Jirahar dispersion begins before Qiryath's destruction and expands through exile. It does not reproduce the Romani migration route.
3. The Orakha and the Prophet's Roads come from World Egg sacred history, not from Romani or Traveller religion.
4. Specialist mobility remains one Jirahar livelihood among several.
5. Host persecution can be severe and systematic. It does not prove that every outsider society behaves identically.
6. Internal wrongdoing remains possible and explicit. It is attributed to people and institutions, not blood.
7. No living Traveller tradition supplies a reusable fantasy species, secret magic or inherited criminal disposition.
