# Atheria before the Imperial Founding — Palace Remnants, Maritime Rivals and the Queen’s Inheritance

Atheria’s republican expansion can acquire greater depth through the addition of an older maritime rival and the country that survives its defeat. That history would stand upon an earlier landscape of ruined Giant palaces, independent human recovery and mixed settlement. The two developments belong to different generations and solve different gaps in the account.

The palace aftermath gives the first southern settlers established neighbours, dangerous surviving rulers and inherited skills to encounter. The republican wars explain how Caleran comes to sustain fleets, tax overseas possessions and reward commanders whose influence reaches beyond annual office. The queen later inherits a country shaped by that conquest, substantial rebuilding and a change of government.

This dossier preserves the proposals considered before canonical integration. [[Atherian Palace and Maritime Histories - Integration Review]] records the selected palace aftermath, heroic cycle, Gateway Republic, wars and surviving country. The subsequent [[Imperial Founding - Authorial History and Integration Review]] selects Thalion as the heir and imperial founder and Route M for the royal house: Nerethan of Delkos married Hadrima of Esharat. Earlier open-choice language below describes the research stage; unselected dynasties, campaign outlines and imperial architectures do not override the canonical descendants.

The present revision gives the Trojan siege and homecomings a principal place in that aftermath. It also adopts three linked proposals for the final republican war: actual child sacrifice is a major reason for intervention; the capital is deliberately razed and heavily salted; and the lesser towns survive as the communities from which the queen’s country develops. These are World Egg research choices. The companion Carthage study separately records what the ancient evidence supports.

Historical foundations are developed separately in [[90 Archive & Drafts/10 Research/Mythic and Sacred History/Palaces, Sea Migrations and the Long Fall - Mycenaean Research and World Egg Comparison.md|Palaces, Sea Migrations and the Long Fall]] and [[90 Archive & Drafts/10 Research/Mythic and Sacred History/Carthage and the Making of Rome's Overseas Republic - Peoples, Punic Wars and Destruction.md|Carthage and the Making of Rome’s Overseas Republic]]. Those dossiers distinguish ancient evidence, later literature and modern interpretation.

## Existing history and the space for expansion

Arsuvan’s destruction already has identifiable participants. Arveth’s demands, conflict among royal houses, Lethra’s resistance and escape work, and the deaths surrounding that struggle remain the foundation of the Aurin account. His death and the disintegration of the kingdom around 2260 BR cannot be moved forward to give later settlers a more dramatic conquest. [[01 Canon Reference/History/20 Prehistory/Arsuvan - The Giant Kingdom of the Aurin.md|Arsuvan]] remains a regional kingdom within the many-state Giant world.

The Glass Sojourn continues through approximately 2165 BR. Its people form a shared society under the Prophet, sustained in their refuge, before his death and the subsequent southern separation. The independent communities outside the Glass have their own succession of generations during that century. The earliest southern settlers consequently enter a changed world rather than join the original liberation campaign.

The established maritime account then provides growing communities, regional powers and particular later crises. [[01 Canon Reference/History/30 Exodus and First Civilizations/Southern Settlement and Maritime Atheria.md|Southern settlement]] and [[01 Canon Reference/History/30 Exodus and First Civilizations/Arkenan and Saronikan Cities before the Empire.md|the eastern maritime cities]] retain their chronology. The new material would expand their early encounters and distinguish the later failures of exchange from the principal Giant collapse.

By c. 800 BR, the twelve-city Celestial Compact begins the established progression into republican institutions. The [[01 Canon Reference/History/40 Atherian Imperial Age/The Celestial Compact and the Caleran Republic.md|republican account]] already describes unequal military contributions, Nerava’s supply dispute, overseas expansion and powerful commanders. The proposed wars provide a major connected history within that framework. They do not create the Compact or become the explanation for every republican grievance.

## A long aftermath with two heroic layers

The first heroic layer belongs to people living through the fall of Giant government and its immediate aftermath. Their achievements can include releasing captives, keeping cultivated land in use, escorting a household through rival armies or destroying the smaller stronghold of a predatory heir. Some are Orphaned people remembered by their descendants before Atherian settlers reach them.

The second layer belongs to mixed southern and maritime societies after c. 2130 BR. Incoming households encounter survivors, seize land, marry into local communities and fight remaining dynasts. A later Atherian version may incorporate an older local hero into its own ancestral sequence. Another tradition may correctly remember a settler’s victory while omitting the inhabitants who supplied guides and food.

These layers allow a heroic age to develop without assigning every protagonist to one ethnic origin. They also distinguish an old Giant kingdom from the smaller powers surviving it. The former palace can be ruined while a cadet house retains a quarry, a defensible estate or a landing supported by captives.

### Three possible regional encounters

These are episode outlines for later development, not new Garden roots or completed biographies.

**The household at the abandoned landing, c. 2200–2170 BR.** A former palace landing remains useful because its crews know the river and can repair boats. A detached High Giant demands workers and provisions while competing with another surviving house. When the rival offers protection, local households help its troops reach the landing. The bargain removes one ruler and installs another with a smaller force and equally concrete needs. Families begin dispersing to minor landings beyond easy collection. Its story concerns the narrowing of royal control and the difficult interval between revolt and independence. Lethra is not brought forward to lead it, and its later tellers cannot be living human eyewitnesses in the settlers’ age.

**The returning hostage, c. 2050–1975 BR.** A child sent from a shore household to a surviving dynast grows up among servants and guards. As an adult, the hostage guides a mixed local and settler force into the estate after the ruler threatens a younger relative. The ruler is a giant-blooded human whose reputation has magnified his ancestry. His death is followed by a dispute over the captives and stored grain: some liberators attempt to retain people as spoils. The protagonist’s victory therefore changes an immediate family danger without resolving the conquerors’ intentions. The selected Persean element is coercion against a household and a dangerous return, with different upbringing, adversary, means and aftermath. It remains separate from the Hostage Children of Oak and Glass.

**The two brothers’ winter, c. 2000–1900 BR.** Two genuine High Giants divide a detached hill estate after their parent’s death. Their feud draws human dependants into successive raids and leaves one district without seed. A coalition of cultivators and new settlers helps defeat the stronger brother, whose body is denied burial by his victorious sibling. The coalition then fractures over the survivor’s demands. The Theban element is a dynastic conflict spreading bodily cost beyond its family, including the treatment of the defeated dead. Exact participants and site remain open; the episode neither restores Arsuvan nor supplies Caleran with another founding fratricide.

The expanded Trojan and homecoming research now supplies a principal regional cycle, developed below as *The Siege and the Returnings*. The three shorter encounter outlines remain separate possibilities rather than compulsory preliminary adventures of its captains. The existing Heraclean Sleeper retains his household catastrophe. Avren, Caleth and Ravessa retain their separate founding history, and no new episode gives their wolf-fosterer control over the whole aftermath.

### The later maritime disruptions

The problems around 1600–1400 BR belong to the society formed after these encounters. Shipping obligations, regional wars, damaged harvests and the use of supplies for dynastic ambition can interrupt a network that had previously worked. The Kassilon grain seizure at Erenost, c. 1550–1500 BR, retains its own participants and consequences.

That episode should be explained within its local maritime sequence. It must not be redated to the Unbinding, assigned to Arveth or turned into the event that destroys every Atherian palace. The older Giant collapse remains the major break in political sovereignty. Subsequent disruptions demonstrate the vulnerability of human recovery to human decisions.

## The Siege and the Returnings

**Working proposal, c. 1980–1930 BR.** The siege belongs approximately to c. 1980–1960; the homecomings and their consequences occupy the following generation. These bands permit a long war and several journeys without assigning every captain a fifty-year voyage. “High Harbour” is a provisional handle for a stronghold on the Arkenan–Saronikan maritime approaches. It is separate from Caleran, Arsuvan’s former capital and the later Gateway Republic.

### The harbour and the assembled ships

High Harbour survived the collapse of its parent kingdom under a detached Giant house. Its ruler controlled a defensible landing and the storehouses above it, collecting grain and labour from nearby settlements. Human craftspeople maintained the harbour works, and foreign households received permission to trade there. Some prospered through royal service; others supplied captives or saw their own relatives taken.

A conflict over seized ships and detained households brought several shore communities together. Glass-descended captains joined established Orphaned settlements and mixed maritime households. Each had particular losses and ambitions. A shipmistress wanted her husband and apprentices released; another captain wanted the royal storehouses; an inland ally wanted an end to the ruler’s collection parties. Their assembly acquired a leading commander through contributions and influence, leaving other captains able to withdraw.

The Giant house had supernatural allies of its own. A tide-court mistress had maintained the harbour’s protected water in exchange for the opening of an old tidal channel at specified seasons. She valued the relationship because it brought matter and travellers through her own territory. A Pride Mask appeared in the palace under the likeness of an ancestral ruler and promised that the house could recover the authority of its predecessors. Its encouragement kept the court fighting after several dependent settlements had offered terms.

### The quarrel among the besiegers

The coalition lived from ships, raided stores and contributions brought from home. Its commander allowed captives to be distributed among leading households. A woman taken from a neighbouring sanctuary was held for sexual service after her father offered ransom. A wind-court envoy, personally attached to the sanctuary’s household, withdrew the assistance she had been giving the supply vessels and diverted a returning convoy. Food spoiled offshore while the commanders argued over the woman’s release.

The captain who challenged the commander had accepted enslaved people himself. His dispute combined anger at the refusal of ransom with fear that the senior household was taking a greater share of everything. When the commander seized part of his following and stores, he withdrew his ships from the next assault. Companions who depended upon those ships remained exposed on the shore.

The captive woman eventually escaped with assistance from the shipmistress, whose detained husband had died in the harbour. They then helped organise passage for other captives. Their subsequent work gave later shore tellers a history different from the captains’ quarrel over honour. The commander remembered in victorious songs as the man who held the coalition together was also the man who had ordered her rape and confinement.

### The battle of tide, wind and bronze

The greatest fighting developed around an ancient breakwater and the tidal channel beneath the Giant stronghold. The defenders repaired the old works; the besiegers drove heavy structures against them. At this concentrated point of resistance, Astaroth appeared as a mailed figure among the blows, turning the impact of one machine into its own destruction. Fighters on both sides saw the figure and later claimed that the Red Star had fought for them.

A local tide manifestation rose through the wreckage as the channel filled with timber and bodies. It tore away part of the besiegers’ landing and carried men beneath the harbour. The wind envoy intervened from the exposed headland, driving water and spray back across the defenders’ upper works. Her intervention gave several ships room to escape and left an inhabited quarter exposed to falling masonry. The powers’ rivalry changed the battlefield within the same afternoon.

A human fighter reached the mailed apparition after a pilot showed him where the old pier concentrated the force of the incoming sea. The fighter broke the anchoring bronze at the moment of impact and struck the visible figure as it came apart. Later songs described blood flowing from the god’s armour. In the proposed authorial account, the local manifestation ended; Astaroth’s sovereignty remained whole.

The fighter’s companion died during the retreat from the pier. A Wrath Mask subsequently visited him in the companion’s appearance, urging him to avenge the death upon every household supporting the harbour. The voice could repeat remembered words and show wounds. It could not return the dead person. The bereaved fighter accepted its proposed revenge and killed prisoners who had played no part in the companion’s death.

### Burial, deception and the sack

A father from the harbour asked to recover the body of a son killed among the defenders. A Hecatara-associated guide brought him to the enemy camp by a temporary shore path, at the price of returning by the long inland route. The meeting secured one body and a pause for burial. Its later telling remained important because people from opposed communities recognised the grief it described.

The city fell through a coordinated withdrawal, a changed tide and information supplied by inhabitants who expected negotiated terms. The attackers returned through exposed harbour works before the defenders could close them. This replaces the wooden horse with an event belonging to the site’s construction, divided population and supernatural relationships.

The genuine High Giant ruler died in the fighting. His bodily death required no severance of a Titan and ended no wider sovereignty. The ancestral Pride Mask appeared above the palace after he fell, calling upon survivors to continue resistance. Craftspeople destroyed its speaking image while trying to escape the burning building. That deprived it of one established means of appearing; it did not overthrow Pride’s Throne.

The victorious commanders broke promises to households that had admitted them. Soldiers murdered inhabitants, raped captives and divided people for enslavement. One officer assaulted a woman who had taken refuge beneath the tide-court mistress’s protection, then took her aboard his vessel. The mistress turned against his company. Other victors defended him because they wanted to retain his crews and share of the ships.

Some inhabitants escaped through the shipmistress’s small craft; others fled inland or were carried away as captives. The harbour’s fall ended a particular surviving Giant court. It also established human occupiers whose conduct supplied new reasons for hatred.

### Three homecomings

**The captain pursued by the tide.** The officer who had violated the refuge left with captives, treasure and surviving companions. The tide-court mistress used linked waters and local envoys to pursue his ship. Her retaliation struck rowers and captives as well as the officer. At one refuge his men were trapped by a predatory court being; it killed and consumed several companions before they escaped by blinding its temporary bodily form. The captain’s boast advertised their next destination to the pursuing court.

The captive woman survived the first wreck and escaped his custody during the landing. She joined a community whose boats recovered people from the following disaster, giving her a life beyond the offence and its supposed punishment. The captain eventually returned to find his household governed in his absence. He killed rivals and ordered the hanging of enslaved women whom he accused of assisting them. Some had acted under coercion. The restored house acquired a continuing blood feud, while the tale of his sea adventures preserved his courage and resourcefulness.

**The ship received by the changing hostess.** A different crew reached a sheltered place held by a Faewild court person skilled in Typhonius-related alterations. She treated the wounded and fed the survivors, then changed several bodies to keep them within her household. Human awareness survived their altered limbs and voices. One sailor negotiated release by surrendering an original bodily trait; another chose to remain with a companion who could not safely travel.

The hostess offered the captain a prolonged bodily life and a place beside her. He wanted to return to the people who had known him before the war. He left with fewer companions and a changed understanding of what survival could cost. This episode carries Circean transformation and Calypso’s detained homecomer as separately identified contributions; it does not import their complete biographies or place every enchantress in one species.

**The boats that chose another shore.** The shipmistress and several recovered captives discovered that their expected landing had been occupied by one of the victorious allies. They could return under that household’s authority, seek another patron or establish themselves elsewhere. They chose a smaller shore already inhabited by families willing to receive their skills and labour.

Their first winter depended upon shared food, repaired boats and disagreements over work. A later feast remembered the first vessel they had made together. In one telling, captive women burned the ships to prevent the men carrying them back into slavery; in another, the unusable hulls were dismantled by common agreement. The existence and causes of a deliberate burning remain a received variant until selected. The settlement is not the Harbor Founded Twice, and it does not become an ancestor of every later maritime people.

### What the cycle leaves behind

The siege can leave a broken bronze fitting displayed as a wounded god’s armour, a changed landing where families refuse to abandon a corpse, and songs that give the captive woman and the shipmistress larger roles than the commanders’ versions. The returners leave different inheritances: an embittered ruling house, a bodily alteration passed into local memory and a boat-building feast.

These are proposed consequences rather than new Garden promotions or fixed 1360 discoveries. They provide a connected heroic history whose later tellers can disagree about people they actually inherited. No supernatural participant needs to have known the whole future, and no victorious captain becomes the sole founder of Atherian civilisation.

The comparison borrows the *Iliad*’s coalition quarrel, visible divine intervention, grief and burial, followed by selected sack and *Odyssey* episodes. It changes ancestry, geography, cause of war, siege method, supernatural identities and the returners’ later societies. The rape, captivity, killing, cannibal predation and executions belonging to the selected elements remain explicit. The full Roman founding cycle, the existing Sleeper’s family murder and the Caleran founders remain separate.

## The proposed gateway society

The leading location is the country of the later **Qabir–Zanthir gateway**, where desert approaches, cultivated ground and Thalassian shipping meet. “Gateway Republic” is a working handle used here for readability; neither it nor “Second Harbour,” used below for a rebuilt royal port, is an authorised historical name.

The proposed coastal offshoot develops after the northern fission, through repeated movement from emerging proto-Ahdari communities into existing shore settlements. A broad interval of c. 1700–1200 BR gives room for several arrivals. Some households bring experience of defended wells, guest obligations and restitution; local families contribute cultivation, coastal knowledge and older regional traditions. Further maritime settlers and freed captives enlarge the population. No single arrival creates all its people.

The harbour’s initial attraction is dependable exchange between food-producing districts and vessels moving along the inner sea. Families build warehouses, maintain landing places, cultivate nearby ground and provide food and water to travellers. Successful households acquire land as well as ships. Political influence follows the ability to organise repairs, finance a voyage or protect a season’s stored produce.

Between c. 1200 and 850 BR, the principal port could become the centre of an association of unequal settlements. Some retain their own elders and cults; others are held through garrisons or tribute. The capital’s elected civic heads, a council of established households and an assembly of recognised citizens develop through particular disputes. Their origins need not reproduce the Common Sky settlement or Caleran’s royal sequence.

Public festivals bring rural offerings, crews and visiting households into the capital. Boat-racing, meals after repair work, wedding gifts brought from distant ports and affection between relatives separated by voyages can give the society its ordinary texture. Enslaved workers cultivate estates, carry goods and labour in households; their skills contribute to prosperity from which they receive unequal benefit.

The civic religion combines shore practices, remembered desert customs and selected older images. This period precedes Mujalib’s revelation by many centuries. Proto-Ahdari descent does not make the republic a Covenant state, and neither its ceremonies nor its political offices are early versions of modern Zanthiri institutions. Its subjects can share ancestry with neighbouring communities while differing in citizenship, language or worship.

One influential cult in the capital develops the sacrifice of children as an offering for the city’s preservation. Its priests serve a bronze-faced dependent Mask of Gluttony, publicly understood as an ancestral provider. Some prominent families give their own children in the belief that the offering protects their house. Others buy children or use captives in their place. Officials eventually extend demands to dependent communities, whose families have little power to refuse. The practice has opponents within the republic as well as beyond it.

The Mask’s appearances and appetite are real within this proposal. It consumes bodily offerings and encourages the belief that another death will make the city safe. The murdered children remain human persons; their souls do not become parts of the image. Ordinary temple fires belong to the human rite, with no infernal fire realm or new Idol sovereignty implied.

The initial public offices should remain modestly specified until the political history is approved. Two annually selected harbour magistrates and a separately appointed campaign commander would give the maritime state a useful contrast with Caleran’s developing offices. Whether these numbers survive drafting depends upon actual institutional history; the research is recommending a division of responsibilities rather than declaring a copied Carthaginian constitution.

Qabir remains the strongest combined choice because the republic and kingdom both need people, food and revenue beyond a capital’s harbour. The present [[01 Canon Reference/Peoples and Cultures/Ahl-e-Ahd/60 Realms and Peoples/Emirate of Zanthir/Emirate of Zanthir Overview.md|Zanthir overview]] supports the physical and economic setting. It supplies no proof that its current population or institutions existed in republican times.

The [[01 Canon Reference/Peoples and Cultures/Sestaran Concord/60 Realms and Peoples/Grand Duchy of Fiorenzia/The Grand Duchy of Fiorenzia Overview.md|Fiorenzian alternative]] would produce a different war: the rival’s access to sea power depends upon relationships between an inland capital and coastal partners. This is credible but requires more new geography and political structure. Its strength is cultivated wealth, not a convenient substitute Mediterranean coast.

The [[01 Canon Reference/Peoples and Cultures/Thalmyric Empire/60 Realms and Peoples/Maritime Kingdom of Saronika/The Maritime Kingdom of Saronika Overview.md|Elmarai alternative]] gives the strongest immediate naval setting. It would make the queen more closely related to the old maritime Atherian world and more dependent upon supplying scattered islands. That could be compelling, but it changes the desired balance of royal agricultural wealth and cultural distance.

Caleran itself stands at the Aurin mouth on the outer coast. It cannot launch directly across the inner sea as though the intervening country were water. Inner-sea campaigns require cooperating Arkenan or Saronikan ports and landward supply links from the Compact’s interior. This separation is one of the most useful transformations from Rome: naval success increases Caleran’s dependence upon other cities even as it tries to dominate them.

## A provisional republican-war sequence

The following dates are working bands within the requested c. 650–500 BR period. They establish order, plausible careers and time for recovery; they are not conversions of Roman dates by a fixed offset.

### First war: who controls the landing places?

An inner-sea port changes allegiance after a dispute between its garrison and local households. Both the Gateway Republic and an Atherian maritime ally claim that intervention will protect their partners. Caleran’s leading houses disagree: some expect commercial access and military prestige, while others fear the cost of defending a distant port. Their eventual commitment expands as rival forces occupy neighbouring anchorages.

Caleran’s commanders obtain ships and pilots from cooperating cities. Grain, rope, timber and replacement equipment move overland towards those ports and onward by sea. Repeated losses force new levies. A shore garrison can survive a lost battle if its harbour remains supplied, while a nominal victory brings little benefit when an army cannot remain through the next season.

The gateway government protects its approaches and attempts to divide the Atherian coalition through selective concessions. Its commanders use fortified ports, experienced crews and raids upon vulnerable supply. Inland subjects are required to provide more grain, and crews remain away beyond the season their families expected.

The war ends when a relief movement fails and the capital can no longer sustain its distant positions. Caleran gains access and payments through a settlement whose beneficiaries include some allied ports. Other cities receive burdens with little corresponding influence. Deaths at sea, devastated settlements and captive sales remain explicit. The outcome creates an overseas commitment rather than immediate control of the entire sea.

### The payment-and-subject revolt

The defeated council attempts to reduce or delay payments to returning troops. Rural districts, already subjected to wartime exactions, refuse another collection. Several groups join the revolt for different reasons: paid foreigners fear dismissal without wages; local levies want to return to households; cultivators demand the removal of collectors and garrisons.

A negotiator is seized after commanders undermine confidence in the offered settlement. Rebel leaders order torture and the killing of prisoners to prevent reconciliation by their followers. Government forces respond with executions and the killing or enslavement of captured dependants. The revolt becomes harder to end as those responsible for atrocities fear what surrender will bring.

The commander who eventually restores the capital’s position relies upon a neighbouring ruler whose support has a price in territory and marriage. Caleran initially benefits from a stable gateway government, then seizes an exposed anchorage while its rival remains exhausted. This conduct strengthens a grievance that later advocates of renewed war can make concrete.

The episode remains a conflict within the rival’s existing society. It does not invent a uniformly foreign mercenary underclass or attribute every rebel’s actions to one charismatic leader. Families on both sides lose people whom a later civic victory festival cannot restore.

### A military household rebuilds its resources

A senior commander receives authority to secure revenues and military access beyond the capital’s weakened core. The leading location is an established chain of coastal settlements and nearby productive districts on the inner sea, rather than an invented western continent. Exact ports and resource sites require selection against the map.

The command combines conquest with agreements among local rulers. Soldiers marry, captives are put to work and some towns receive protection against their own enemies. A successor within the commander’s household retains experienced officers and clients. The capital gains revenue while becoming less able to replace the family that supplies it.

This is the Barcid contribution: recovery through a military household whose public service creates a partly independent source of power. A mine can be selected later where geology and existing geography support it. No silver mountain, island chain or imperial province is created simply to reproduce Iberia.

### The second war: an invasion of relationships

The household’s next commander attacks Caleran’s coalition by offering selected allies reduced obligations and freedom from recent levies. The army reaches a friendly or captured landing on the inner-sea side, then moves through established approaches towards inland allied country. The difficult passage is a combined coastal and upland operation. It does not require a literal Alpine replica or a march from an unmapped continent.

Its supply depends upon friendly ports, pack transport, captured stores and successive harvests. Caleran’s first armies suffer serious defeats while trying to protect allies and impose battle. Some communities defect; others fear the invader’s nearby partners more than they resent Caleran. A fortified town that remains loyal can interrupt the invasion’s supplies even after a spectacular victory elsewhere.

Caleran’s delaying commander conserves forces and contests forage, accepting the destruction of property beyond immediate protection. Rival politicians demand a decisive battle. Meanwhile, another command attacks the gateway’s overseas base, preventing replacements from reaching the invading army. A separate naval effort keeps enough allied ports open to support a campaign against the rival homeland.

A local border ruler joins the Atherian expedition to defeat a competing dynasty and gain territory. These neighbours possess their own disputes and settlements; the war changes their balance without creating their societies. Cavalry arrangements must be established locally if used. The much later Tarkhan horse tradition is unavailable, and neither elephants nor another large war animal are required for this sequence to work.

The invasion is eventually withdrawn. Its commander has won battles, preserved loyalty among veterans and caused immense destruction, but failed to replace Caleran’s network with a durable coalition. The gateway accepts restrictions upon war-making and naval power. The Atherian victor gains prestige that troubles other senate houses.

### Recovery and the decision to destroy

Over roughly two generations, the defeated republic repairs cultivated districts and revives trade. Its government must meet external payments while dealing with former soldiers, returned captives and unequal losses. A reforming commander could seek civilian office, challenge privileged exemptions and eventually be driven into exile. This remains an available Hannibal-derived career, separate from the later queen.

The neighbouring ruler rewarded by Caleran, followed by heirs with their own ambitions, encroaches upon disputed districts. Gateway petitions achieve inconsistent results. Some Caleran senators favour a workable subordinate state; others argue that renewed wealth will make future resistance possible. Suppliers, provincial clients and military houses have different interests in continued peace.

During these pressures, the capital’s sacrificial authorities demand increasingly costly offerings. The deaths become harder for dependent towns to avoid. Families hide children with relatives, refuse collection parties or seek shelter beyond the republic’s control. Temple workers who leave the capital describe killings they have witnessed, while escaped captives lead others to the places where children awaiting sacrifice are held.

The Atherian destruction advocate gives these accounts a prominent place in the campaign for intervention. Several supporters sincerely want to end the sacrifices and protect the threatened households. Other senators seek a secure sea route, land or the removal of a recovered rival. The resulting majority depends upon both kinds of support. Within this version, the protection of children materially helps bring the decision to war; it is not merely a claim invented after victory.

Attempts to secure the children’s release and dismantle the institution fail. The capital’s ruling faction protects its priesthood and demands the return of fugitives. Some lesser towns then offer supplies or passage to the Atherians in return for protection. Their defection weakens the capital while giving the invading army food, pilots and somewhere to receive families leaving the contested districts.

The policy adopted by Caleran distinguishes the capital from the rest of the country. Lesser towns that accept the settlement are to retain their inhabitants and civic communities. The capital must surrender its weapons, release the captives and accept the destruction of the sacrificial establishment. After an initial surrender, the demand expands to abandonment of the city itself. Households that opposed the sacrifices now face removal alongside the authorities responsible for them, and renewed resistance draws in people with very different motives.

The final siege combines blockade, siege works, shortages and close fighting. Atherian soldiers rescue children from places of confinement and destroy the apparatus of sacrifice. Other detachments kill inhabitants, rape captives and seize people for sale. Commanders who permit those acts remain responsible for them. The ending of the sacrificial institution is a real result of victory, as are the ruined households.

The capital is subsequently burned and razed. Salt is brought through the captured harbour from coastal stores and spread heavily across the temple enclosure, selected residential ground and gardens; surviving cisterns are spoiled and broken. This is a deliberate act of destruction and exclusion, requiring labour and transport. It becomes the most remembered image of the victory.

The salt does not poison every field in the country or make the ground eternally sterile. Demolished waterworks, population removal and armed restrictions on rebuilding prolong the capital’s desolation. The lesser towns retain working wells, cultivated ground, workshops and landing places. Some have suffered siege, requisition or loss of relatives, but the final policy spares them the capital’s planned erasure.

### The end of the sacrificial cult

The capital’s Mask loses the repeated offerings, captive supply and protected sanctuary that sustained its local presence. Its image is broken and its serving chambers dismantled. The destruction advocate presents the salting as the final refusal to feed it again. Salt alone has no universal power over Idols; the decisive change is the ending of the organised sacrifices and the destruction of this local means of manifestation.

Priests who ordered killings and officials who delivered children face capture, execution or flight. Families who opposed them recover some living relatives and learn the fate of others. A surviving parent may support the destruction of the sanctuary while opposing the sale of neighbouring children by the victorious army. These positions arise from particular experiences and do not require a uniformly guilty or uniformly innocent population.

This history leaves the surviving towns with an important claim of their own. Their households sheltered children and helped end the capital’s demands; they can remember liberation alongside the losses and obligations imposed by Atherian rule. The later dynasty will seek authority among people who already possess that account of themselves.

### Caleran after victory

The wars enlarge the resources available to senate households and provincial commanders. Naval obligations become habitual, transport associations gain bargaining experience, and returning soldiers expect rewards. Families can prosper through supply, acquire enslaved workers or suffer because their land has been damaged during absence.

Nerava’s established dispute belongs within these middle republican pressures. The wars can explain part of the repeated demand for boats and carts; they do not absorb the dispute’s independent grievances or turn every river association into a naval reform. Its partial settlement still benefits recognised owners more reliably than hired workers.

This history leaves more than a century for additional provincial politics before either imperial-founding architecture. No direct line of descent is required from the early victorious commander to Thalion. A remembered family name, a command precedent or a disputed colony may matter to later politicians without the same household controlling every generation.

## The queen’s country after destruction

Both alternatives begin from the same loss and survival: the republican capital is razed and salted, many inhabitants are killed or enslaved, and the lesser towns retain communities under an unequal arrangement supervised by Atherian authorities. The capital’s sacrificial demands have ended. The surviving towns become the political and material foundations of the later kingdom.

Survival occurs across a country wider than its capital. Some cultivated districts retain inhabitants; others receive settlers or fall temporarily out of use. Minor ports, fishing landings and inland towns carry skills and religious practices into the rebuilding. Refugees include people who escaped before the final siege and those later freed, ransomed or allowed to return. Their descendants need not recover the property their families once held.

“Second Harbour” describes a proposed repaired or enlarged lesser port spared in the final settlement. It becomes the leading candidate for the royal seat while the old capital remains partly ruined. The name is only a research handle: the place already has inhabitants and a history before it acquires royal importance. Exact siting, distance and its proper name remain for the geography pass.

### Route L: a locally rooted dynasty

In the first route, a family from a surviving provincial town gains responsibility for collecting produce and supplying an Atherian garrison. Its earlier standing comes from land, transport and religious patronage outside the destroyed capital. Some of its members supported surrender; others sheltered fugitives, leaving an inheritance that descendants disagree about.

During c. 500–470 BR, the household organises repair of irrigation and storage, attracts displaced craftspeople and secures permission to expand the surviving harbour. Its work increases food production and shipping, while compulsory labour and preferential land grants make recovery unequal. Households able to prove useful service recover standing more quickly than the poorest survivors.

The family consolidates power through marriages into several surviving communities. A later head acquires an emergency command during a local succession conflict or interruption of Atherian supervision. Recognition as ruler follows bargaining with provincial commanders who want reliable revenue and secure routes. Royal authority therefore grows from delegated responsibility, local alliances and the ability to maintain order.

By c. 450–400 BR, the crown governs the cultivated district and a group of dependent ports. It pays tribute or supplies military assistance while retaining control over household succession and internal patronage. Atheria keeps leverage through garrisons, treaty rights and support for rival claimants. Its authority is substantial but exercised through people whose interests do not remain identical to its own.

The later queen inherits both the achievements and accusations of this reconstruction. Her dynasty comes from one of the spared towns. Its alliances, marriages and eventual command of Second Harbour give her a territorial claim reaching beyond personal descent. She must negotiate with surviving urban families whose independence predates the crown. Her religious programme honours the country’s dead while maintaining the rejection of child sacrifice on which important local loyalties depend.

### Route M: an incoming maritime dynasty

In the second route, Atherian authorities entrust the region to a commander from an established maritime Atherian society. The appointment initially concerns harbour defence, supplies and the supervision of dependent towns. The incoming household brings officers, specialists and relatives, but relies upon local cultivators, pilots and administrators to remain.

During c. 500–470 BR, the commander establishes a residence at Second Harbour and directs repairs through existing communities. Marriage into an influential family of a spared town provides knowledge, credit and political connections. The spouse’s family has interests of its own and can resist attempts to place every recovered estate in foreign hands. Migrants and survivors work together while maintaining unequal access to military protection and land.

The governor’s children grow up within overlapping languages and religious practices. A disputed succession in the wider Atherian command system gives one of them the opportunity to secure hereditary recognition. Rival senators prefer a dependable client to a costly direct occupation; some neighbouring towns support the household because it restrains predatory garrisons or competing settlers.

By c. 450–400 BR, the dynasty possesses a royal court, a rebuilt port, cultivated revenue and a military following recruited from several communities. It adopts local ceremonies while retaining maritime familial connections. Citizenship and standing can be stratified, with privileged immigrant households, recognised older families, freed people and subject cultivators receiving different opportunities.

The queen’s later authority rests upon this developed kingdom and its ancestry in both incoming and spared-town households. She can claim to inherit the communities that preserved the country, while her dynasty’s control of grain, ports and protection gives that claim practical force. Local temples, inland communities and foreign commanders expect different things from her. Support from an Atherian patron secures her throne and gives that patron leverage over it.

### Assessment of the two routes

Route L gives the strongest continuity of local political families and a substantial argument about collaboration. Route M produces a more pronounced maritime royal overlay, providing the clearest counterpart to the dynastic and cultural complexity associated with Cleopatra’s Egypt. Both require several generations of work and negotiation.

**Route M is recommended for further drafting**, with a powerful local spouse and surviving town institutions essential to its consolidation. This recommendation remains unselected. Route L is equally viable chronologically and should be retained until the author chooses the kind of royal household desired.

Under either route, the inhabitants continue to include descendants of the destroyed republic, earlier shore populations, migrants, freed captives and foreign military households. The country becomes recognisably different from its republican predecessor. Its prosperity cannot restore those killed or recover every stolen holding.

## Supernatural possibilities with limited effects

The campaigns work through human choices and material arrangements. The following interventions could materially affect selected episodes without governing the whole history.

**A sea passage bought at the cost of an anchorage.** A local court person associated with Thalassor can maintain a particular passage during a withdrawal if an older harbour work is opened to the water. The commander accepts because the fleet can escape; households beside the work lose shelter and stored food. The successful bargain establishes the passage’s effect, not the moral ownership of the coast. It remains distinct from the already proposed queen’s later withdrawal and from the Court of Salt and Teeth; select one historical use before writing both into the same country.

**The capital’s sacrificial Mask — adopted within the research proposal.** A bronze-faced dependent Gluttony Mask exploits the belief that the city must consume irreplaceable lives to preserve itself. Its priests and their patrons order actual child sacrifices; internal opponents and threatened households contribute to the coalition against it. The final war breaks its local sacrificial establishment. The Mask is separate from a former Giant ancestor, every murdered child and Gluttony’s continuing Throne. Political opposition to it confers no inherited prophetic commission upon an Atherian commander.

**A wall that outlasts its defenders.** Astaroth-related craft or correspondence can help a selected fortification withstand assault while hunger reduces its population. Priests call the endurance evidence of victory to come; officers choose whether to maintain that claim and prevent evacuation. A surviving wall demonstrates physical resistance. It cannot determine the justice of the war or guarantee the safety of those within it.

**Royal religious borrowing.** A later dynasty can sponsor local ceremonies, claim ancient descent and use Atherian public titles to address different constituencies. Actual Menhir, Masks and the Boatman retain their distinct identities behind those interpretations. Recognition by a cult does not create a new divine person or convert the dead monarch’s human soul into an office.

The sacrificial Mask is now part of the revised fictional proposal. The sea passage, enduring wall and particular royal ceremonies remain alternatives for further selection. The earlier heroic cycle has its own supernatural participants and does not share this Mask, capital or war. No new Idol succession, pre-Redeemer manifested Saint, permanent heavenly army or supernatural master conspiracy is proposed.
