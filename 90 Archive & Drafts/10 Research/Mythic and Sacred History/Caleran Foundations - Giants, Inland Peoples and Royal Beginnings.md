# Caleran Foundations — Giants, Inland Peoples and Royal Beginnings

This proposed history follows the Aurin basin from the last Giant kings to the republican struggles preceding Aurelius Thalion. Its principal subjects are Arsuvan, the regional kingdom destroyed during the Great Unbinding; the Enathi, Serathi and Vardeni peoples who emerged from its human survivors and neighbouring communities; and the royal households through which Caleran became a meeting place of inland and maritime societies.

The account is written as connected history for review. Its new names, events and interpretations remain proposals. The [[90 Archive & Drafts/10 Research/Mythic and Sacred History/Caleran Foundations - Sources, Collisions and Canon Review.md|companion review]] separates existing canon, new historical additions, received stories and unresolved questions. The narrative ends before Thalion takes power around 350 BR.

## The Aurin country

The Aurin descends from the highlands around later Veridun towards the north-western coast. Its lower reaches connect cultivable river land with the estuary at Caleran. Boats can carry heavy cargo through this country more cheaply than pack animals, although shallows, changing channels and seasonal water levels make local knowledge indispensable. The approaches to the uplands supply timber, grazing and metal; their roads bring mountain produce towards the river landings and the sea.

Arsuvan occupied a succession of these connected districts. Its strongest rulers held the lower river, cultivated terraces above the floodplain and several approaches to the mineral-bearing uplands. Their authority weakened beyond the routes along which grain and soldiers could move. Coastal shipping carried their goods farther than their armies could routinely march. Much of the country between their principal strongholds remained under subordinate households whose cooperation required repeated negotiation, marriage or force.

Four places recur in the regional history. **Sarven**, the Giant royal centre, stood on a raised terrace overlooking agricultural land upstream from the estuary. **Nerava** grew around an inland river landing where boats transferred cargo to pack trains. **Thesra** occupied a defensible hill near the meeting of lowland and upland routes. **Olveth** developed in a mountain basin with summer pastures and access to ore workings. Their precise sites remain to be selected within these broad relationships. Caleran's later prominence at the river mouth drew upon all four districts.

## Arsuvan and its royal houses

### Fields, mines and the palace at Sarven

The oldest ruling house followed in this account is **Iskaren**. Its kings possessed genuine Giant ancestry and inherited exceptional strength. Palace tradition traced them to a warrior who had descended bodily from a red star. Successive rulers repeated this claim at their accession, presenting victories, endurance and imposing bodies as signs of their celestial birth.

Under **Veskar Iskaren**, Arsuvan joined river estates to the routes leading towards Thesra and the uplands. This enlarged the supplies available to the palace: grain and flax from the river country, cattle and wool from higher valleys, timber for boats and building, and metal from workings beyond Olveth. Sarven maintained foundries and large workshops whose human artisans produced agricultural fittings, arms, fastenings and ceremonial vessels. Royal ships exported metalwork and cloth and brought back salt, luxury goods and materials unavailable within the basin.

The palace concentrated skilled people as well as produce. Captive carpenters lived beside hereditary service households; potters married into boat families; astronomers employed children who could remember long sequences of observations. A household might enjoy considerable freedom in its own courtyard while remaining unable to refuse the ruler's demands for labour or surrender of a child. Favoured servants could acquire property and slaves. Their prosperity depended on a political order that separated them sharply from the people driven into the mines or worked on the royal fields.

The royal granaries supplied dependants during bad harvests and furnished armies during campaigning. They also enabled punishment. An estate that resisted a levy could lose access to stored grain while its own harvest was seized. Families escaped through the river reeds, took shelter with upland relatives or attached themselves to a rival lord. Recapture brought sale, beating or execution. Boat crews concealed fugitives under cargo, sometimes from kinship and sometimes for payment; other crews delivered them back to the palace.

### The marriage of the two houses

**House Velketh** held upland estates and workshops concerned with animal breeding, grafting and bodily alteration. Its rulers possessed a different Giant ancestry. Their ceremonial histories described a mother who had assembled her children from the creatures of the mountains and placed a living star inside each breast. These stories justified the family's authority over breeding houses and the people compelled to serve them.

**Tarvessa Velketh** married into the Iskaren line during a period of interrupted ore deliveries and rivalry between the upper valleys and Sarven. She brought armed dependants, access to several workings and skilled breeders whose pack animals could manage difficult approaches. Her marriage made her a ruler of the combined kingdom. She supervised upland appointments, received petitions from her own dependants and intervened in the allocation of labour. Sarven's later ceremonial art placed her beside the king beneath a shared canopy of stars.

The union increased the kingdom's resources and sharpened disputes within both houses. Cadet families lost control of tolls and demanded compensation. Royal marriages multiplied, giving some branches greater inherited power while others relied on soldiers, wealth and machines made by their servants. Human members of these extended households occupied positions ranging from enslaved attendants to acknowledged relatives. Palace genealogies concealed these differences whenever a claim of undiluted celestial descent was useful.

### Astronomy and royal religion

Sarven's observers worked from open terraces with fixed sighting stones. They compared the return of visible stars with river conditions, breeding seasons and the practical timing of voyages. Their accumulated observations had agricultural and navigational value. The crown controlled access to this learning and used the ceremonial calendar to assemble tributaries, display captives and announce campaigns.

The palace associated its martial ancestors with the power later worshipped as **Astaroth**. Velketh houses preserved rites concerned with the changes later assigned to **Typhonius**. Other practices grew around routes, predatory animals, musical performance and the hidden workings of large buildings. Their images and explanations varied between estates. The later Astratheon pantheon had yet to acquire its familiar public form.

At Sarven, the ruler's place beneath the stars became an increasingly elaborate claim to supremacy. Captured chiefs were made to kneel in the positions assigned to conquered constellations. Some were killed before assembled dependants. Court visions praised the sovereign as the measure against which every lesser being should be judged. These ceremonies left a particularly hostile memory among the river households, whose descendants continued to use the astronomical learning while destroying or covering images of the kings.

Foreign connections added to the palace's knowledge. Arsuvan bought metal and specialist work through intermediaries trading towards the Copper Stair kingdoms. Caravans carried stories of Kharad's marriage politics, and sea merchants handled goods that had passed through Ulmaris. Such connections were intermittent and often indirect. Arsuvan's prosperity rested chiefly on its own basin and approaches; the other kingdoms followed separate histories.

### The collapse during the Great Unbinding

The Great Unbinding broke the wider systems on which Arsuvan's rulers had learned to rely. Merchants ceased to arrive regularly, defeated Giant households demanded hospitality and armed clients carried disputes into the basin. News of the Prophet's warnings and the destruction of distant royal powers travelled with fugitives. Some human families left to seek the gathering remnant. Most inhabitants of the Aurin country remained, facing the collapse where they lived.

**Arveth**, the last ruler to command Sarven and its principal river estates together, attempted to support his army by taking grain already reserved for seed and winter food. Velketh relatives withheld upland supplies until he recognised their preferred successor. Fighting between their retainers interrupted the pack routes. Human soldiers deserted, while several service households barricaded their workshops and began exchanging their own goods for food.

At Nerava, a boat organiser named **Lethra** persuaded crews to move seed grain and families by night while the royal escorts slept at an upstream landing. She used concealed boats and channels known to local fishers. When the theft was discovered, Arveth's officers hanged members of two crews who had remained behind. Their relatives continued the crossings with fewer boats. The rescued seed supported the first planting after the palace lost control of the district.

Arveth marched downriver to recover the stores. His opponents cut the bank beside a narrow towpath after rain had softened it, trapping the baggage carts in mud. Upland deserters and local fighters attacked the immobilised force. Arveth killed several attackers before they brought him down with heavy quarry wedges and spears. His bodily death ended his rule. The struggle continued around Sarven, where rival claimants burned storehouses and carried off skilled captives before abandoning parts of the palace.

By about 2260 BR, unified royal government had ceased. The river settlements divided surviving stores; Thesra's households fortified their approaches; upland communities reopened workings whose overseers had fled. Many people died from hunger, exposure or raids during the following years. Others recovered freedom and reunited with relatives from whom royal service had separated them. No single successor inherited the whole basin.

## Three peoples after the Giants

Between about 2260 and 1900 BR, related regional communities developed into the peoples later called Enathi, Serathi and Vardeni. The names became useful gradually, as speech, marriage, festivals and political loyalties acquired recognisable local forms. Their shared past included service under Arsuvan, resistance to its rulers and contact with human settlements that had survived beyond its most closely controlled districts. Ancestry ran through many households.

### Enathi

The **Enathi** occupied the Aurin waterways and cultivated lowlands. River movement shaped their settlements: dwellings clustered above common flood levels, working land extended along accessible banks, and families kept boats for transport as well as fishing. Household prosperity depended on grain storage, maintained embankments, animals available for ploughing and the ability to move a harvest before bad weather spoiled it.

Upstream grain villages, the landing families around Nerava and estuarine fishing communities developed different interests. Farmers wanted dependable channels and affordable transport. Boat owners profited from carrying produce and could exploit shortages by withholding their vessels. Estuary households valued access to salt and maritime markets. Their gatherings frequently involved shared meals and boat races as well as decisions about work on the banks. Children learned the river through errands, fishing and games in the shallows under older relatives' supervision.

Nerava's boat families remembered Lethra as an organiser whose authority came from persuading people to undertake dangerous work together. Her descendants preserved a small ferry business for several generations; unrelated crews later adopted her name in their songs. The first-planting meal associated with her rescue used grain boiled with river fish, making a celebration out of food once saved in desperation. Its ingredients changed with local means.

The Enathi honoured ancestors within households and carried offerings to places associated with the river, crossings and the return of crops. Some rites retained astronomical dates learned under the Giants. Others followed observable water and weather conditions. The people who tended these shrines also farmed, healed animals, brewed drink or maintained boats. Religious authority was distributed among families and specialists, and disagreements over a failed harvest could become arguments about leadership.

Enathi communities fought one another over channels, moorings and fields. Captives could be held as servants or sold. At the same time, adoption and marriage allowed outsiders to enter households and learn occupations. These practices gave later maritime newcomers several ways into river society, with markedly different consequences for settlers who arrived as guests, employers, soldiers or owners of conquered land.

### Serathi

The **Serathi** lived chiefly in hill towns and older settled districts between the river plain and the upland approaches. Thesra became an influential centre because its defensible position brought together a market, workshops and religious teaching. Other towns resisted its efforts to speak for them. Each maintained its own prominent houses, festivals and alliances.

An ancestor house combined a recognised family, its dependants and the buildings through which it displayed continuity. Living members maintained tombs, sponsored meals and taught children the achievements and quarrels of earlier generations. Women could bring workshops, land and important ritual responsibilities into a marriage. Their authority varied between towns and households; a wealthy woman's ability to employ workers or influence a king gave little protection to an enslaved woman within the same establishment.

Serathi craft included fine metal fittings, decorated pottery, woven cloth and the repair of inherited instruments. Religious learning drew on surviving astronomical observations, the arrangement of sacred space, seasonal signs and the interpretation of unusual events. Teachers argued about these matters and competed for patrons. A successful house could attract pupils from several towns, giving its views influence far beyond its own territory.

Banquets were occasions for music, courtship and political display. Hosts prized performers who could recall old songs and improvise new verses about the guests. Potters painted dancers, wrestlers and household animals alongside more solemn scenes. Families lent vessels for a neighbour's wedding and complained for years if they came back chipped. These habits travelled with Serathi spouses and craftspeople into river settlements and later Caleran.

Hill-city rulers accumulated land and dependants, sometimes through purchase and marriage, sometimes through seizure. Their tombs enlarged as poorer households became indebted to them. Rival houses maintained armed followers and occasionally destroyed one another's workshops. The resulting refugees carried skills into neighbouring communities, including those which had originally competed against their patrons.

### Vardeni

The **Vardeni** inhabited mountain valleys, pastures and mining districts connected to the Aurin by a series of approaches. Some households moved animals seasonally while keeping permanent farms in lower valleys. Others worked mines, made charcoal, traded pack animals or supplied the food on which those occupations depended. Olveth became a meeting place for these different groups.

Valley leaders needed the support of households able to provide animals, skilled workers and fighters. A successful leader could organise a shared pasture, repair a dangerous approach or keep a mining settlement supplied through winter. Ambitious families used these responsibilities to demand service from their neighbours. Disputes over grazing or a rich working sometimes divided communities whose members remained linked by marriage.

Metal production required sustained cooperation. Ore carriers, charcoal burners, smiths and farmers all contributed to a finished tool. The loss of one road or one reliable source of fuel could leave a workshop idle. Vardeni smiths consequently maintained connections both within the mountains and with lowland merchants. Their products included household knives, plough fittings and cooking equipment as well as weapons. Competitions in forging useful objects accompanied some seasonal gatherings, and a well-made pot could remain a family's prized possession long after its maker's name was forgotten.

Religious practices concerned the safety of animals, the fertility of particular places and the dangers of hunting and travel. Stories about Orionis gave hunters language for experiences in which they had felt themselves pursued. Other rites addressed powers that later Star theology incorporated only imperfectly. Some valley households continued offerings to the land and sun despite the lowland teaching that associated the Sun with Giant tyranny.

Vardeni migration into the lower country occurred through employment, marriage, hunger and military service. Lowland rulers recruited upland soldiers and pack drivers, then tried to keep their families dependent upon royal wages. Valley communities also sheltered people fleeing those rulers. Over time, the same routes carried ambitious young fighters outward and displaced families back into the hills.

### Relationships among the three

The three peoples shared related speech, many domestic practices and memories of Giant rule. River grain sustained upland settlements; upland metal and animals supported the river economy; hill-city workshops and markets linked both. Marriages moved knowledge along these routes. Religious differences usually followed a particular town, teacher or powerful house more closely than a clean boundary between peoples.

Their descendants would enter Atherian society under unequal circumstances. Some became citizens, landowners or temple patrons. Others were enslaved, dispossessed or driven into less accessible country. Many retained local affiliations alongside an Atherian language or civic identity. Their contribution to later heartland society cannot be reduced to a single conquered population disappearing into its conquerors.

## Maritime settlement and the river towns, c. 2050–1400 BR

Proto-Atherian settlers reached the Aurin country after the southern separation of the Glass People. They brought households accustomed to collective provisioning, inherited religious memories of the Prophet and military organisations developed during migration and settlement. They encountered occupied fields, active workshops and river communities whose experience of the Giant collapse had been independent of the Glass refuge.

The earliest substantial newcomers at the estuary established a defended landing and demanded provisions from neighbouring settlements. Fighting followed when their captains tried to turn temporary supply arrangements into permanent possession of cultivated land. Enathi families lost houses and boats; captives were sold to pay armed followers. Other communities allied with the newcomers against existing rivals or arranged marriages intended to secure a share of the enlarged trade.

The consequences varied within families. A boat owner might prosper by supplying a foreign garrison while a cousin lost land to its commander. A Serathi craft household could gain maritime customers and later find its apprentices compelled to work for the same customers' sons. Soldiers settled among the people they had fought, sometimes bringing captive spouses and sometimes entering marriages negotiated between households. These relationships became foundations of subsequent communities, accompanied by memories which their descendants found difficult to reconcile.

During the heroic-maritime centuries, roughly 1900–1400 BR, ship-owning houses connected the Aurin mouth to a wider Atherian world. Their wealth came from carrying food, timber, metal and captives, hiring out fighters and establishing settlements around useful anchorages. Inland rulers acquired imported vessels, songs and weapons. Maritime households adopted local building methods, religious specialists and kinship connections useful beyond the coast.

Religious change accompanied these relationships over many generations. Households preserved the Prophet's story while giving increasing attention to powers associated with a voyage, a harvest or a military success. The Lord's place in some recitals became that of a distant creator, while local offerings and borrowed rites occupied more of public life. Maritime singers recast the Prophet as a heroic liberator favoured by the stars. Inland teachers supplied observations and sacred interpretations inherited from the Giant period. By the time of Caleran's kings, these practices already had long histories among people who disagreed about their meaning.

Nerava benefited from supplying this trade. Thesra's workshops attracted foreign apprentices, while Olveth's pack breeders found new markets. The growing exchange supported larger households and more elaborate public feasts. It also exposed communities to distant failures: a defeated sea-king could leave debts unpaid, divert grain towards his fleet or return with armed men to demand what he could no longer purchase.

In the later part of this period, rivalry between shipping houses and their inland partners damaged several established supply routes. Some landings were abandoned after raids; others survived by changing patrons. Caleran's estuary district remained inhabited through these changes, although settlement shifted between usable banks and defensible ground. The city that emerged in the following centuries drew together communities with long and uneven histories of cooperation.

## The wolf and the founding siblings

### Ravessa's household

Around the twelfth century BR, **Ravessa** led the Faewild household of **Istren**, whose members hunted and maintained refuges along a wooded approach above the lower Aurin. She appeared as a large grey she-wolf and could speak to humans. Her household belonged to the predatory world associated with Orionis. It had its own attachments, rivalries and dependants.

Ravessa's principal opponent was **Orsul of Nereth**, the head of a neighbouring household. Nereth had taken a sheltered hunting basin and an approach through which Istren's dependants could move between their Faewild homes and a particular woodland in the human country. The loss crowded Istren into poorer ground. Ravessa wanted an allied human ruling house that could keep that woodland accessible, shelter her messengers and supply trained fighters and metal weapons during a limited attempt to recover the basin.

She watched the quarrels around the estuary because human settlement was already altering access to the woods. A hostile fort could obstruct the route she needed. A friendly household might guard it. Raising children with a plausible claim among the estuary's people offered her a longer and more dependable influence than hiring whichever captain happened to command the landing.

### Rescue and upbringing

**Avren** and **Caleth** were the young sons of **Meral**, an Enathi boat owner, and **Damaris**, a soldier from a maritime-derived household. Their parents had helped a coalition of river families resist **Odrava**, a captain who sought exclusive command of an estuary landing. When the coalition failed, Odrava killed Damaris, imprisoned Meral and ordered the children taken to a disused landing and drowned. Avren was old enough to speak; Caleth still needed help to eat.

The two men sent to kill them left them tied in a grounded boat, expecting the incoming water to finish the task. Ravessa found the children while moving through the woodland approach. She tore through the rope, carried Caleth and led Avren into the trees. Her choice was deliberate: she knew who their parents were and what their survival might mean to the families defeated by Odrava.

Ravessa brought them to a refuge near the passage into Istren's country. A human dependent, **Meral's former neighbour Alvessa**, helped feed and clothe them. Alvessa had sought shelter there after losing her home during the fighting. She could teach the children river speech, cook their food and carry out the daily work for which a wolf's body was poorly suited. Ravessa supplied protection and food, taught them to move through the wood and took an attentive interest in their growth. The boys learned the habits of her household and called her their second mother.

Their childhood contained ordinary pleasures within an unusual home. Avren delighted in following tracks and guessing where a hunted animal would turn. Caleth made small boats from bark and persuaded Alvessa to float them in every available stream. Ravessa tolerated their games until they interfered with a hunt. Both children learned that her affection could coexist with impatience, possessiveness and expectations they had scarcely begun to understand.

Meral escaped captivity several years later with the help of people who had continued to trade through the landing. She found her sons through Alvessa. Rebuilding her relationship with them took time: they remembered her unevenly, and Ravessa resented any proposal that might remove them from her influence. Meral resumed boat work among relatives and gradually brought the boys into contact with their human kin. Their adolescence passed between these households.

### The struggle at the river mouth

As young men, the brothers joined opposition to Odrava's successors. Avren gathered fighters from dispossessed households and from men whose seasonal employment had ended. Caleth worked with boat owners and grain suppliers, including relatives who had accommodated themselves to the existing rulers. Ravessa supplied guides along one wooded approach and expected their success to bring the promised human assistance.

The brothers' supporters seized a fortified landing after its provisioners refused further deliveries. Fighting killed defenders and several inhabitants caught in burning store buildings. Avren ordered the execution of a surrendered officer whom he held responsible for his father's death. Caleth helped households return to the riverfront and arranged repairs to the landing. Their coalition then joined neighbouring settlements under a common defence and a ruling household based at the estuary.

Later tradition calls this the foundation of Caleran. The brothers brought a new political centre into being among existing settlements. They built on inhabited ground, employed local craftspeople and depended on grain raised by communities already established along the river. The relationship between this foundation and every earlier buried structure remains uncertain.

The first years exposed differences between the brothers. Avren regarded command of the fighting men as the basis of kingship and promised Ravessa a force to recover her lost basin. Caleth wanted the coalition's river and hill households to share decisions about war and expenditure. He had married **Sareth**, a Serathi potter whose family supplied the rebuilt settlement. Through her relatives he had become responsible for people who feared being drawn into a conflict inside the Faewild.

Caleth's objections were sharpened by a failed reconnaissance. Men sent along Ravessa's route returned carrying a dead companion whose body had been mauled by Nereth's hunters. Caleth refused to provide boats or provisions for the larger expedition Avren was preparing. He offered Ravessa continued shelter and supplies while insisting that the soldiers remain at home. She considered this a betrayal of the purpose for which she had protected them.

### Fratricide and its aftermath

The brothers met at an unfinished timber hall to settle command of the proposed expedition. Caleth arrived with household representatives who intended to resist the levy. Avren brought armed followers. During the argument, Caleth ordered the stored provisions released back to the households that had supplied them. Avren struck him with a spear and killed him. He had chosen to preserve his command by killing the brother whose supporters were preparing to withdraw it.

Avren's men dispersed the opposing households and killed several who resisted. Others escaped by boat. Sareth recovered her husband's body with help from his mother's relatives and returned to Thesra with their children. She continued her craft there and refused the king's later invitation to join his household. Meral remained in the river country, maintaining contact with both branches of the family while refusing to attend Avren's accession feast.

The death damaged Ravessa's project. The boat families withdrew provisions, Avren's followers were occupied with securing his rule, and the expedition she had expected became impossible at its intended scale. She obtained a smaller body of hired fighters, recovered one refuge and failed to dislodge Orsul from the contested basin. Several of those who went with her never returned. Her connection with the royal household continued uneasily, giving neither party the dependable alliance it had sought.

Avren ruled long enough to repair the landing's defences and establish a household that outlived him. His dependence on armed supporters enlarged their landholdings, while the families loyal to Caleth maintained a separate memory of the city's beginnings. Later rulers inherited the practical gains of the foundation and the quarrel within its ancestry. They could claim descent from the founding house, marry into its opponents or honour both brothers; each choice carried consequences among the living.

## The received tale: The Wolf's Two Sons

The following telling is proposed as a later Aurin household version. It preserves the rescue, the wolf's purpose and the murder, while compressing years of political struggle into a story told over one evening.

When the men who had killed their father came for them, Avren asked whether his mother was waiting in the boat. The men told him she was. He climbed in with his little brother, and they bound the children to the seat.

The water had begun to lift the boat when a grey wolf came down through the reeds. Avren pulled Caleth behind him and shouted until his throat hurt. The wolf put one forefoot on the gunwale.

“You will turn it over,” Avren said.

“Then hold still while I bite the rope.”

She carried Caleth by his coat. Avren followed, stumbling, with the loose rope still round his wrist. At the edge of the wood he stopped and looked back for his mother. The wolf waited until he could walk again.

In the wood lived Alvessa, who had once mended Meral's sail. She washed the boys, warmed milk and made beds for them beside the hearth. The wolf brought meat. In winter she lay across the doorway, and the children slept with their feet against her flank.

One day Avren asked why she had taken them.

“Nereth hunts in the hollow where my young were raised,” she said. “When you have a house of your own, I shall need men who will walk beside mine.”

Avren promised her a hundred spears. Caleth promised her a boat, because he had never seen a wolf in one and thought she would like it.

Their mother came back with a scar across her palm. Caleth hid behind Alvessa, for the woman at the door did not resemble the mother he had made from his brother's stories. Meral sat by the hearth and repaired his torn sleeve. By evening he had brought her the other things that needed mending.

The boys grew. Avren learned to take a ridge before his pursuer reached it. Caleth learned to bring a loaded boat through shallow water without losing the grain. Their mother took them downriver, and the people who had known their father found work for them. In time, those people found weapons too.

They fought for the landing and drove its masters from the river. They raised a hall where the families could eat together. Avren sat nearest the weapons; Caleth sat nearest the door, because somebody was always arriving with a broken fitting, a sick child or a complaint about the boats.

The wolf came to the feast. Everyone moved a little farther from the meat.

“Now,” she said, “you have your house.”

Avren rose to call for the spears. Caleth asked who would bring in the harvest and guard the river while the men were gone. The wolf laid her chin on his knee, as she had done when he was a child, and he stroked the grey hair between her ears.

“I can give you food,” he told her. “I can give you shelter. I cannot give you every man who trusted us.”

Avren said that a promise made in the wood was still a promise beside the river. Caleth said the people at the tables had made none. They argued until the food grew cold. Their mother took the children outside.

When the brothers met again, the hall still smelled of fresh timber. Caleth had come to send the grain home. Avren barred his way with a spear.

“Will you kill me for it?” Caleth asked.

Avren thrust before he had found an answer. Afterwards he called for water, cloth and the healer. Sareth held her husband while the healer tried to stop the bleeding. Caleth died on the floor of the house they had built together.

The wolf came when she heard the mourning. She smelled the spear and stood for a long time beside the body. In the telling, nobody hears what she says to Avren. When she leaves, she takes no food.

Sareth carried Caleth to the hill country. Meral walked beside the bier until her feet failed, then rode in the cart with her grandchildren. Avren sent a horse and an escort. Sareth sent the horse back.

The king held the landing. His men went with the wolf into the trees, and fewer came home. At the next planting feast, a child asked why there were two small bowls beside the hearth when only one founder sat in the hall. Meral filled both, and told him how the wolf had found them.

### Variants and customs

Royal tellings make Caleth the first to draw a weapon or attribute his death to a confused struggle among followers. Thesran versions give Sareth a larger part and describe her refusal to let Avren pay for the funeral. Stories told among hunters retain Ravessa's speech about the lost basin; later civic versions often turn her into an ordinary animal and leave out the purpose of her fosterage. Some temple poems call the boys sons of Orionis, a claim which sits uneasily beside traditions that continue to name Damaris and Meral.

The two small bowls belong chiefly to household commemorations. In the proposed survival of the custom, families set them beside a shared meal and give their contents to children before the adults eat. Some tellers emphasise the children rescued from the boat, others the brothers who failed to share their house. Potter families associated with Sareth preserve pairs whose decoration differs slightly, allowing both resemblance and separation to remain visible.

Wolf images belong to a wider range of later uses. Royal houses display her as a protector; hostile songs give her hungry eyes fixed on the boys. A few local traditions leave food at the edge of a wood, although the sites they identify cannot all be Ravessa's old approach. The survival of an image or custom supplies no complete account of the being whom it represents.

## Caleran's royal households

### The river kings after Avren

The founding household continued through several generations, intermarrying with the river families and hill-city houses upon whose supplies it depended. Later genealogists shortened this period by attributing works from several reigns to Avren. The more useful historical distinction is between the founder's military household and the descendants who turned its position into sustained government.

One of these descendants, **Esvan**, is associated with the extension of grain sheds and a common landing for upriver boats. The crown took a portion of cargo in return for maintaining the waterfront and protecting vessels within the settlement. Household heads met regularly to organise this work. Their council gained influence because the king needed their boats, labour and stored food, especially during shortages.

Esvan's authority reached unevenly beyond the city. He held some neighbouring farms directly and received service from others through marriage and protection. His soldiers took captives in disputes over the river approaches. Several captive households were settled beside the grain sheds, where their descendants became skilled in storage and transport while remaining dependent on the crown. Later royal accounts praised the expanded supply system and usually passed quickly over how its workers had arrived.

The dynasty's marriages also brought Sareth's wider kin back into Caleran. A later generation could cooperate without agreeing about Caleth's death. Craft households from Thesra worked on the royal buildings, maintaining their own meals and commemorations. The city acquired a larger population accustomed to living under one ruler while belonging to different religious and family associations.

### Ilaron and Tarsenna

**Ilaron** and **Tarsenna**, who ruled in a later generation around the turn of the tenth century BR, established the most influential royal religious settlement. Ilaron came from a maritime-derived household supplying ships and armed escorts. His mother's kin belonged to an estuary branch of the royal family and supported his claim during a disputed succession. Tarsenna belonged to a prominent Serathi house at Thesra, where she had learned astronomical observation, the ordering of festivals and the management of temple estates.

Their marriage joined substantial resources. Ilaron could bring shipping and soldiers to the city; Tarsenna could secure inland credit, skilled personnel and support from houses whose participation kept the river trade supplied. She controlled the resources she brought and held audiences concerning the temples and estates attached to her household. Their accession depended on the council accepting both sets of supporters.

The couple inherited a city whose public celebrations competed for labour, money and space. Maritime houses brought their own sacred stories, performers and observances. River households maintained crossing and harvest rites. Serathi families guarded forms of worship that gave their ancestors and teachers an honoured place. A royal ceremony could therefore signal whose past counted and whose contributions would be subordinated.

Ilaron wanted reliable contributions from these groups and a public language in which newcomers could acknowledge the ruling house. Tarsenna wanted inland religious learning to retain authority within the expanding city. She also wanted to prevent rival temple patrons from using incompatible dates to draw workers away from one another's festivals and building projects. Both saw advantages in supporting ceremonies that people could attend together.

Their government built a shared festival ground and improved the approach from the landing. Tarsenna brought teachers and instrument makers from Thesra to work with maritime observers. Ilaron provided transport for visiting delegations and employed crews during the building season. They attended performances together and developed an enthusiasm for a comic contest in which rival singers described the same voyage from the sailor's and the provisioner's points of view. Later moralists disapproved of some of the jokes preserved under the queen's name.

### The Common Sky settlement

The settlement remembered as the **Common Sky** developed over several years. It established a common calendar for royal ceremonies, recognised a group of Star cults as participants in public festivals and assigned support for their buildings and celebrations. The names familiar from maritime worship became the usual public names, while inland ritual specialists shaped much of the ceremonial practice.

Its central story placed humanity under the Star Children and represented the overthrow of Giant rule as a liberation in which the stars had favoured human ancestors. Maritime memories of the Prophet contributed the figure of **Astronax**, the Star Herald. Inland accounts contributed memories of royal observatories, servitude and surviving knowledge. Poets explained that the Giants had stolen celestial learning which properly belonged to humanity. Tarsenna's teachers retained useful observations while changing the rulers and ancestors praised during their recital.

The settlement altered public life in visible ways. Processions from different neighbourhoods met at the festival ground. Temple meals used grain from river estates and animals supplied through upland contacts. Travelling performers could enter royal competitions under Lyraxis's name without abandoning every local observance. Funerary specialists joined common commemorations, and route-keepers brought practices associated with Hecatara into ceremonies welcoming visiting households.

Resistance came from particular interests. Some maritime patrons objected to inland teachers deciding the order of ceremonies. A Thesran house refused to let its ancestral vessels leave the city for Caleran's festival. Several upland communities continued offerings to the Sun and land powers which the public Star story treated unfavourably or left at its edges. The couple accepted many domestic observances while using money, appointments and royal pressure to secure participation in the shared ceremonies.

The reign also retained blood sacrifice. Animals were killed at public feasts, and captured enemies were sacrificed during a campaign celebration approved by both rulers. Tarsenna selected the ceremonial occasion; Ilaron supplied the prisoners from a defeated river stronghold. Their relatives remembered the killings after the festival became a celebrated beginning of civic concord. The shared religion strengthened Caleran's government while preserving practices through which it displayed and exercised power over captives.

### The cults within the settlement

**Astaroth** received the dedication of weapons and the ceremonies attending the muster of fighting men. Iskaren traditions had connected inherited strength with royal warfare; maritime worship supplied stories of a terrifying patron of battle. The Common Sky brought these strands into a public cult serving the city's forces. Its later republican patrons included commanders competing for honour after victories. Astaroth's eventual elevation under Thalion belongs to the subsequent imperial history.

**Lyraxis** joined several kinds of performance. Palace entertainments, Serathi banquet songs and maritime tales of cunning all contributed to the cult. Under the couple, contests became an important part of shared festivals, providing livelihoods for musicians and amusement across household divisions. Republican sponsors enlarged them and used popular performers to attract support. Stories of inspired invention remained accompanied by tales of tricks which injured or humiliated their targets.

**Thanatos** brought together crossing rites, funerary practice and maritime stories about travel and theft. Enathi boat families had long described the dead as departing on a journey; Serathi houses equipped tombs with vessels and gifts. The common cult connected these practices to a Star Child who supposedly guided the dead. Its priests increasingly taught that appropriate payment and favour could improve a soul's passage. Republican patrons supported magnificent funerals and purchased promises which the priests could not fulfil.

**Orionis** had established followings among hunters and among households accustomed to dangerous woodland travel. Caleran's royal associations added the wolf-founders to this body of stories. The Common Sky gave their descendants a place in public celebration while different families continued to disagree about Ravessa. Republican hunters and military households later favoured versions in which discipline, pursuit or mastery explained the city's success.

**Hecatara** received practices concerning crossroads, unusual routes and the decisions made before a journey. Hill shrines and maritime experiences contributed different stories about reaching a place by an unexpected approach. Tarsenna's settlement supported a civic shrine where visitors could make offerings before departing. Later rulers and commanders employed specialists who claimed to find routes unavailable to their enemies; successful journeys encouraged much broader claims about fate and wisdom.

**Typhonius** inherited a particularly troubled place in the Aurin country. Velketh breeding houses had associated extraordinary bodies with royal power, while later farmers and stock-keepers preserved useful techniques alongside memories of coercion. Maritime monster stories enlarged the cult's imagery. Public ceremonies under the couple displayed remarkable animals and addressed fears of malformed birth and uncontrolled change. Republican owners of breeding estates sought advantage through the same worship, drawing criticism when their experiments harmed workers or animals.

**Arachnara** appeared in rites concerning vermin, concealed spaces and the small creatures found within large settlements. Palace servants had worked among ducts, stores and hidden passages whose upkeep mattered greatly to daily life. River and hill households developed offerings intended to avert infestations and misfortune. The Common Sky recognised these practices in the public religious order. As Caleran grew, household cults and specialist workers carried them into crowded districts, while hostile stories increasingly associated secrecy with corruption and disease.

The seven names followed here are those currently named in the established account of Astratheon. The Common Sky organised their public relationships alongside numerous household practices. Later theologians would continue to argue about the membership, ancestry and rivalries of the celestial family.

### Later kings and the weakening crown

Ilaron and Tarsenna's successors inherited festivals, temple estates and a council accustomed to managing substantial resources. Their daughter **Ilessa of the Festival House** married into an upriver royal branch. She maintained the festival endowments through a succession dispute and employed craftsmen whose descendants continued to serve the public temples. The marriage strengthened ties with grain-producing estates and gave her children supporters beyond the maritime houses that had backed her father.

Later kings expanded walls, store buildings and ship facilities with labour drawn from subject settlements and captives. Wealthy houses supplied advances for these works and expected influence in return. The crown could reward supporters with land or offices, but each reward increased the resources from which a household might later resist it. Councillors acquired direct relationships with soldiers, suppliers and temple personnel.

The last phase of Caleran's kingship centred on **Senavar's household**. Senavar tried to recover royal independence by extending levies and replacing established local commanders with his own dependants. A quarrel over grain deliveries became a confrontation when his soldiers seized boats at Nerava and imprisoned their crews. Caleran's merchants lost cargo, upriver households lost transport and several councillors feared that their own armed followers would be dispersed next.

A coalition of councillors, boat owners and neighbouring rulers forced the withdrawal of the royal garrison. Fighting and reprisals killed members of both the king's household and the coalition. Senavar's family retained support in some estates, making a single victorious replacement king difficult to establish. For a time, selected officers exercised the city's principal powers while settlements continued to negotiate their military and commercial relationships.

## The Celestial Compact and republican development, c. 800–350 BR

### Twelve cities and a shared meeting place

The twelve-city **Celestial Compact**, established around 800 BR, grew from repeated attempts to coordinate rival cities. It provided arrangements for a shared calendar, arbitration and military contributions. Caleran's river mouth, harbour connections and inland approaches made it a useful meeting place. The religious institutions developed under its kings supplied experienced personnel and familiar occasions on which delegations could assemble.

Participation did not give all inhabitants of the twelve cities an equal share in government. Rulers and leading houses negotiated obligations, while cultivators, workers and dependants supplied much of what was promised. Some member cities retained local kings or prominent hereditary families. The arrangements changed as common campaigns and the needs of transport increased the frequency with which representatives met.

Caleran's loss of a secure dynasty helped its councils acquire power, and the Compact provided a wider setting in which that power could grow. Over subsequent generations, assemblies, a senate of influential houses and annually chosen magistrates became enduring republican institutions. Offices once exercised within a king's household were redistributed among people who could claim authority through civic selection and family standing. Religious ceremonies continued to lend these offices dignity.

### Land, service and unequal membership

Republican expansion incorporated settlements through conquest, negotiated submission and alliance. The rewards were uneven. Some communities received substantial civic participation; others provided soldiers and taxes while their leading families negotiated privileges unavailable to their neighbours. Enathi, Serathi and Vardeni descendants entered these arrangements alongside maritime-derived households. Atherian identity increasingly described language, civic association and participation in a larger political world.

Military service drew men away during seasons when their farms needed labour. Wealthy neighbours lent seed or money, purchased distressed holdings and employed captives on enlarged estates. Successful campaigns brought more land and slaves into the same system. Farmers who lost their holdings sought work in towns or attached themselves to commanders promising a new settlement after the next war.

Around the middle republican centuries, a dispute over the **Nerava supply levy** exposed these tensions. Families whose boats and carts had served a long campaign demanded relief before another levy began. Prominent houses secured reductions for their own property while smaller suppliers continued to face seizure. Work stoppages and the refusal of some crews to embark forced a partial settlement. The episode gave river associations a durable place in republican bargaining, although its benefits varied sharply according to household influence.

Republican public life also offered genuine opportunities. New roads and more dependable transport enlarged markets for food and craft. Apprentices could move between towns; performers found larger audiences; returning soldiers brought unfamiliar games and tastes. Families celebrated admissions to civic bodies and took pride in public buildings to which they had contributed. Those gains helped sustain loyalty even among communities engaged in repeated disputes with Caleran.

### The conditions preceding Thalion

By the later republican period, provincial wealth had become central to competition within Caleran. Commanders distributed spoils and supported the households of their followers. Senators invested in estates, transport and military supply. Annual offices could change hands while the relationships created by repeated service endured for decades.

Disputes over settlement grants brought veterans into direct conflict with cultivators already occupying the promised land. Rival commanders supported different claimants and used festivals, funerals and temple patronage to advertise their standing. The Common Sky institutions survived within this competition. A public sacrifice or successful omen could become a claim that one faction alone had the stars' support.

The resulting civil struggles weakened the republic's ability to restrain its own military patrons. Cities sought protection from particular commanders, and families divided over which alliance offered safety or advancement. These are the conditions into which Aurelius Thalion would intervene around 350 BR. His seizure of power, unification and subsequent religious settlement belong to the next account.

## The wider inheritance

The Aurin history gives Caleran a foundation within an inhabited and changing country. Its kings drew upon river provisioning, hill-city religious learning, upland production and maritime connections. The republic expanded these relationships across a much larger world, making local identities part of an increasingly civic Atherian inheritance.

Communities farther into the highlands followed different paths. Some accepted Atherian speech and institutions while retaining valley affiliations and seasonal ways of life. Others entered the imperial world much later. Their descendants would contribute to the regional diversity of Thalmyria through further settlement, religious change and the upheavals surrounding the Fall and Reconquest. Those later developments require their own histories.

The proposed remains of this early account include Sarven's damaged observation terraces, abandoned grain facilities, changing forms of pottery and tools, paired household bowls, foundation songs and buildings repeatedly adapted by later users. They preserve different portions of the past. Exact surviving sites, ownership and the history of individual objects await the review of this foundation account and its later canonical development.
