# The Tarkhan Horse Centuries — World Egg Comparison and Canon Review

## Purpose and present canon

The Tarkhan already possess a strong outline. They are an Orphaned people of the northern dry belt. Western Well-Tarkhan developed wells, camel circuits and caravan brokerage long before the Fall. Eastern communities herded, hunted and travelled across grass country before Atherian remounts escaped after 905 AR. The First Catch transformed this eastern world between 905 and 920. Qarayel united the banners in 1261, dominated the northern roads during the Black Wind years and died in 1303. His coalition divided into four regional-dynastic Hordes in 1304.

The missing material lies between those dates. The Horse Centuries currently contain trade, raids and mercenary service, but little named political history. Qarayel has the shape of a conqueror without the relationships, institutions, cities, defeats and crimes which would make that conquest credible. The Four Hordes possess clear present functions but need a better account of what each inherited from the Black Wind.

The Inner Asian comparison provides that depth. It does not require a Tarkhan version of every Scythian, Turkic or Mongol people. The most useful architecture consists of several layers: an ancient open-country civilisation; a sudden horse and remount revolution; three centuries of unstable coalitions; one extraordinary imperial gathering; and four successor worlds which preserve different pieces of it.

## Historical architecture

### 1. The First Catch, 905–920 AR

The Atherian Fall releases horses, handlers, harness, breeding knowledge and abandoned frontier stores into an inhabited grassland. Eastern Tarkhan hunters and herders capture the animals. Some imperial handlers join voluntarily. Others are taken with the studs and forced to teach their captors. Several clans slaughter captured horses during the first winter because they cannot feed them. Grey Sister's remembered willingness belongs inside this disorder rather than replacing it.

The first winners are households with sheltered winter pasture, skilled herd labour and access to metal. A horse is initially expensive enough to deepen inequality. A poor family may possess riding skill yet lose its foals to a patron. Established camel houses finance breeding in exchange for future animals. Captive Atherian grooms become valuable property. Marriage joins handler families to Tarkhan houses, sometimes willingly and sometimes under coercion.

Within three generations, mounting a larger part of the camp changes hunting, courtship, seasonal range and war. Carts remain useful for households and heavy goods. Camels remain superior in the dry west. The transformation produces Grass-Tarkhan power without making either ecology obsolete.

### 2. The remount generations, c. 920–1030 AR

Horse wealth spreads through gifts, theft, bride-price, ransom and service. Eastern houses fight over winter pasture. A series of severe winters destroys several celebrated bloodlines and forces survivors into richer camps. Raiders reach villages and roads previously beyond their practical range. Captives can be moved farther before pursuit catches them.

Early mounted coalitions last for a campaign or season. Their leaders owe followers animals, food and loot. A successful chief distributes broodmares and captured property; a failing chief loses camps to another banner. Outsiders record each coalition name as a nation even when the same families appear beneath a different banner twenty years later.

The first attempts against walled settlements fail badly. Tarkhan riders can isolate a town and seize its herds, but gates and masonry defeat arrows. Captured masons, miners and Atherian artillery servants gradually alter this balance. Their work is coerced. Some negotiate freedom or protection for their families; others die building machines for the people who burned their homes.

### 3. The banner centuries, c. 1030–1180 AR

Several strong regional gatherings emerge and disappear. None unites the whole people. Western coalitions contest the Nine Wells and sell military service to oasis rulers. Central grass houses protect or extort seasonal markets. Northeastern clans bargain over the roads leading toward Castara. Sestaran and Velian authorities recruit some Tarkhan and punish others, often within the same extended family.

This period develops three institutions later used by Qarayel:

1. **Remount obligations.** A banner house promises a known number of sound horses at assembly. Failure can cost pasture, hostages or political standing.
2. **Road relays.** Allied camps maintain fresh mounts and shelters for messages. The network is irregular, privately controlled and frequently used for raids as well as diplomacy.
3. **Taken craft households.** Smiths, bowyers, leather workers, interpreters and siege labourers are attached to powerful camps. Some are respected clients. Others are slaves. A household's technical importance does not guarantee freedom.

Religious practice changes with movement. Falcon bonds and wind-speakers become important to scouting in particular eastern regions. Grey Sister's descent acquires ritual value. Rulers increasingly describe successful coalitions as favoured by Kokh. Since Kokh is a human conflation of several sky-aligned Menhir powers, one apparent answer may come from a lesser Orionis court person and another from a distinct atmospheric or road jurisdiction. A working bargain grants no right to conquer neighbours.

### 4. The generation before Qarayel, c. 1180–1260 AR

Longer campaigns produce wealthy banner houses and exhausted dependants. Some chiefs settle clients near markets and demand grain tax. Others regard settlement as a trap. Slave routes join the northern roads to Sestaran ports, Covenant markets and eastern buyers. Captive concubinage, child sale and coerced labour become ordinary sources of wealth in powerful camps.

The period should culminate in a failed gathering whose leaders divide the spoils before the war is won. Their quarrel strands families in a severe winter and permits a coalition of towns to kill Tarkhan hostages. Qarayel's minor house survives by moving abandoned people and herds out of the disaster. This provides a credible humble aperture without absolving his later conquests. He learns that loyalty follows food, horses and protection more reliably than ancestry alone.

### 5. Qarayel's gathering, 1261–1265 AR

Qarayel calls the Kurultai of the Long Grass after years spent building relationships among minor houses, dispossessed riders, slave-born specialists, hostage keepers and dissatisfied sons of greater clans. The assembly recognises his Nine-Mane Standard. Its support remains conditional: he must win, distribute wealth and prevent the great houses from consuming the lesser ones.

His reform should combine existing Tarkhan institutions rather than invent a new society. He breaks several hereditary commands, redistributes remount obligations and places personally loyal officers over mixed bodies of riders. He gives conquered specialists protection and status while continuing to hold many others in bondage. Interpreters and herd masters enter the governing circle. Hostages secure important houses. Some are educated and married into the coalition; others are executed when their relatives revolt.

Qarayel's oldest personal relationship should be a sworn companion who helped him survive the earlier winter and later refuses the new order. Their quarrel supplies the emotional centre of unification. The rival defends the freedom of old banners while accepting slavery and hereditary privilege within them. Qarayel offers advancement across clan lines while claiming the right to redistribute whole households. Each sees one real evil in the other and protects another in himself. The rivalry ends in war and an irreversible death; it does not map the complete Temüjin–Jamuqa narrative.

### 6. The Black Wind campaigns, 1265–1300 AR

The existing principle—extraction rather than permanent occupation—remains sound, but it requires concrete campaigns.

#### The war of the western fairs

Velian and Iqashari authorities refuse Qarayel's grass-toll and shelter rival banners. His riders isolate fair grounds, seize herds and cut caravan movement. One town surrenders, supplies grain and retains its local rulers under annual payment. Another kills his envoys and the families travelling with them. Qarayel storms it with captured engineers, massacres defenders and inhabitants after the breach, sells survivors and transfers smiths to his moving court. The destruction makes later towns surrender more quickly.

This episode gives terror its strategic and personal functions without making every settlement suffer the same fate. It also creates a surviving community of deported artisans whose descendants belong to Tarkhan history even if they reject Tarkhan identity.

#### The Nine Wells campaign

Qarayel uses the old Covenant seizure of 558 to recruit White and western houses. His army retakes several seasonal approaches but cannot hold the complete well system without settling garrisons. He accepts tribute, hostage access and limited grazing instead. White songs later call this the full restoration. Covenant histories reduce it to a raid. Families expelled in both 558 and Qarayel's campaign remember two conquerors claiming the same water.

#### The northern road war

Sestaran and other northern merchants attempt to bypass Tarkhan tolls. Qarayel attacks depots, forces guides into service and creates a relay route which moves news, tax demands and hostages faster than any single clan circuit. Security improves for merchants carrying his token. Those without it face confiscation, enslavement or death. Peace on the road is a purchased privilege within an extractive empire.

#### The eastern failure

One expedition beyond dependable grass collapses after weather, disease and a scorched-pasture defence destroy remounts. Qarayel abandons slow captives and orders the killing of horses which cannot continue. A subordinate refuses to leave wounded households and saves them at the cost of the campaign's treasure. Later court histories minimise the defeat. This failure establishes the material limits of the Black Wind and prevents Qarayel from becoming an undefeated tactical miracle merely because he dies undefeated in the formal record.

### 7. Government of a moving dominion

Qarayel does not administer every village. Local rulers collect grain, animals and silver under negotiated obligations. Camps maintain relay stages and remount registers. His court settles disputes among banner houses, receives foreign envoys, distributes captives and assigns winter pasture. The moving treasury consists partly of animals and movable wealth and partly of claims upon towns.

The system protects selected merchants, clergy, healers and artisans because Qarayel needs their work. Religious tolerance is practical and unequal. He permits Covenant, Processional and local rites in dependant communities when their leaders pray for his rule and meet obligations. He destroys or confiscates institutions that resist. Exemption proves political usefulness, not spiritual agreement.

Qarayel's public theology presents the Nine-Mane Standard as evidence that the open sky has judged settled rulers and chosen him to order the roads. Wind-speakers disagree over the claim. Actual Menhir encounters remain local bargains. Pride can feed upon the demand that victory reveal universal favour; Greed feeds upon tribute, slaves and ownership of movement; Wrath feeds upon punitive destruction. Their activity need not produce a new Archmask or Idol succession.

### 8. Household and dynasty

The conqueror's family must become part of the history. Wives and in-laws bring herds, routes and political relationships. A senior khatun manages the moving court during long campaigns and protects the inheritance of her children. Daughters marry client rulers or banner heirs. These marriages can secure peace and also hold women as instruments of policy. A daughter who governs her assigned households may wield real authority while remaining unable to choose the marriage that placed her there.

Qarayel's sons and nephews command different regions. Their rival courts attract officers and religious specialists. He refuses to designate an uncontested successor, perhaps because every choice would split the coalition before his death. Executions, inheritance disputes and the interests of senior women prevent agreement after 1303. This makes the Partition a consequence of the dominion's design rather than a sudden return to chaos.

The present canon requires male khanship and permanent formal war command. Historically specific women may still control appanages, direct court resources, organise defence and exercise regency. Sorqai's later office belongs to this tradition without creating universal female eligibility for the khanship.

### 9. The Partition, 1304–1360 AR

The Four Hordes develop from regional commands, dynastic households and attached populations. Their colour names do not describe four pure nations.

| Horde | Principal inheritance from the Black Wind | Human cost and internal contradiction |
|---|---|---|
| White | the Nine Wells grievance, northern raid circuits and the rhetoric of reclamation | families displaced by Tarkhan as well as Covenant armies disappear from heroic versions; captive sale finances resistance |
| Iron | siege specialists, settled contracts and western tax relationships | service and settlement create wealth while binding clans to the institutions they once raided |
| Moon | the Nine-Mane Standard, burial policy and strongest dynastic claim | succession remains dependent upon an ageing regent, rival male heirs and controlled memory of Qarayel |
| Ash | relay stages, eastern pasture and northern road diplomacy | road peace enriches the Horde while merchants and dependant camps carry its tribute burden |

The Partition should involve purges, household transfers and several local wars. It need not repeat the scale of the Black Wind. People also choose among successors for practical reasons: a widow takes her herd to the ruler who recognises her sons; an artisan family follows the Horde that protects its workshop; a slave flees across a new boundary and is returned under one compact but hidden by a rival camp.

## What the comparison changes

### Qarayel becomes a ruler rather than an atmospheric disaster

His rise begins in a minor household and a failed winter gathering. He succeeds through memory, redistribution, personal loyalty, violence and the disciplined use of existing institutions. His court contains wives, sons, rivals, interpreters, artisans, captives and client rulers. He makes choices which improve communication and restrain some elite predators while expanding slavery, tribute and massacre.

### The Horse Centuries become a history

Between 920 and 1260, horses alter inequality, marriage, hunting, raiding, settlement and relations with neighbouring states. Failed sieges lead to coerced technical learning. Short coalitions develop the obligations Qarayel later centralises. Winter mortality and pasture conflict remain as important as battle.

### The Hordes become successor societies

Each inherits a region, a dynastic claim, attached human communities and an institutional fragment. None is merely one temperament in coloured clothing. Their differences arise from what they control and whom they must govern.

## Hard edges which must remain

An eventual canon history should state directly that:

- Atherian handlers and their families were among the people captured with the remounts.
- Mounted raiding increased the range and volume of slave-taking.
- Captive women experienced forced marriage and concubinage; children could be sold, adopted without freeing their mothers or retained as hostages.
- Qarayel used threatened massacre to obtain surrender and ordered at least one actual massacre after a breach.
- Skilled prisoners were deported and compelled to build weapons, roads and court goods.
- Herds ruined crops and winter grazing; punitive grazing could starve settled families.
- Hostages sometimes entered ruling households and sometimes died for decisions they did not make.
- The successor struggle included executions, purges and forced transfers of households.

These facts do not require every Tarkhan to commit the same crimes. The victims and dissenters include Tarkhan as well as settled neighbours. A mounted society can condemn slavery while profiting from it, honour guest-right while killing people outside it and protect a road by threatening everyone who declines its protection.

## Ordinary life worth remembering

The hard history will become monotonous if every scene is a raid. Horse training requires patience and affection. Children learn balance on old mares. Families race short winter courses, argue over song verses, exchange fermented milk and repair felt together. A valued horse can be a household's livelihood rather than a warrior's ornament. Camel breeders mock riders whose animals cannot cross dry country; riders answer with jokes about camels refusing every command except the wrong one.

Seasonal assembly brings marriage negotiations, wrestling, archery, trade, mourning and music. Stories of famous chiefs sit beside tales of failed bridegrooms, stubborn animals and grandmothers who save camps through knowledge no warrior possessed. Dede Korkut's household-centred epic structure is more useful here than one unbroken national chronicle.

## Provisional historical and legendary leads

These are development leads, not Garden roots or canon events.

### The Winter That Ate Five Banners

A celebrated pre-Qarayel coalition divides winter pasture according to prestige rather than need. Ice seals the grass. Chiefs keep the sheltered valley for their war herds while dependant camps lose animals and children. A minor household breaks the boundary at night and is condemned for theft. Its survivors later form part of Qarayel's early following. The story changes a grazing trespass into the beginning of political allegiance.

### The Taken Engineer and the First Breach

A captured miner refuses to build against his own town. Qarayel's predecessor threatens his children. The miner constructs a machine whose first use breaches a different fortress, then sabotages it during the assault upon his home. Both armies kill civilians in the confusion. Later Iron-Horde siege houses claim him as a willing founder; his descendants preserve the tool with which he damaged the mechanism.

### The Mare Given Back

A wealthy horse house seizes a poor girl's mare as remount tax. The animal repeatedly returns to its first camp. The khan orders the girl adopted into his household to preserve his claim over both. She gains education and property while her mother remains enslaved. Their separation becomes the moral centre of the tale; the celebrated adoption does not repair it.

### Qarayel's Broken Brother

The future conqueror and his closest companion share hunger, danger and rescue before their political visions divide. Their last meeting ends without reconciliation. One of them orders the other's death. Songs disagree about who offered mercy and whether the form of execution honoured or mocked their old bond.

### The City of One Open Gate

A market town opens one gate after neighbouring rulers refuse aid. Its inhabitants survive and feed the Black Wind, while a second town kills Qarayel's envoys and is destroyed. Descendants of the spared town later celebrate prudence. Families who were handed over as part of the surrender call the day a betrayal.

### The Khatun's Three Winters

After Qarayel's death, a senior widow holds the moving court together through three winters. She rations grain, arranges marriages, executes two officers and hides one rival child. Her measures prevent immediate general war and ensure the later Partition. Each Horde remembers the child differently.

### The Relay Rider Who Arrived Too Soon

A young rider uses the new road to deliver an execution order before the condemned household's allies can intercede. Horrified by what speed has accomplished, he later carries a reprieve at the same pace and saves a rival family. Relay riders tell both halves when teaching that a road serves the hand which writes the message.

## Supernatural and theological rulings

1. **Kokh remains composite.** Tarkhan language may treat sky, wind and victorious fortune as one favour. The underlying Menhir and court persons remain distinct.
2. **Victory proves no divine mandate.** Qarayel's claim that the sky selected him is imperial theology. It is not a commission from the Lord.
3. **Falcon bonds remain bounded.** They depend upon lesser Orionis court persons, one bird and established sensory limits. They do not grant strategic omniscience.
4. **Idols exploit institutions they did not create.** Pride, Greed and Wrath may feed through imperial claims, tribute and retaliation. No new Mask, Archmask or succession is fixed by this dossier.
5. **Humans remain human.** Qarayel, his family, captives and every ordinary Tarkhan possess complete human souls. A legend, banner office, burial residue or counterfeit apparition is not the person.
6. **The grave remains protected.** No research lead explains Qarayel's undisclosed burial, the fate of the burial riders or the manifestations claimed in his name.

## Collision review

- The First Catch remains the post-905 horse revolution. Chariot houses and limited earlier horses do not become Grass-Tarkhan cavalry before it.
- The failed pre-Qarayel winter gathering is not the First Catch, Qarayel's 1261 kurultai or one of the Four Hordes.
- The city massacre does not replace the burning of Velian fairs already named in canon; integration should decide whether it deepens one fair war or becomes a separate campaign.
- The Nine Wells campaign cannot reverse the Covenant seizure or establish exclusive Tarkhan title in 1360.
- The relay network precedes and influences the Ash Road Peace but is not identical to the Castaran Long Road.
- The khatun-regency lead does not replace Sorqai. It establishes an earlier precedent for female regency under the existing male khanship rule.
- The sworn companion does not become a hidden founder of every anti-Qarayel faction or explain the Partition alone.
- The deported craft households do not make the Iron Horde a settled ethnic caste.

## Decisions required before integration

1. Should the resisting city destroyed by Qarayel be one of the already-burned Velian fairs, an Iqashari dependant town or a separate northern market?
2. Should Qarayel personally order the massacre after a rejected surrender, or permit it after the breach and then protect the officers responsible? Both make him culpable; the first presents deliberate terror, while the second presents authority compromised by the coalition he built.
3. Does his sworn companion die by Qarayel's order, kill himself after capture or fall in the last battle? The choice changes Qarayel's personal guilt and the later songs.
4. Which wife or senior female relative governs the last campaigns and first interregnum? This figure should remain separate from Sorqai.
5. Should the eastern failure be a well-known defeat, a suppressed disaster reconstructed from households or a victory in court histories and defeat in local memory?

## Recommended direction

Develop the Horse Centuries as a full history before expanding Qarayel's biography. Let the massacre occur at an Iqashari dependant market which killed envoys after receiving a surrender demand. Qarayel should personally approve exemplary destruction; his political intelligence and later road order must coexist with direct responsibility. His sworn companion should be captured and killed on Qarayel's order after refusing office, leaving an intimate crime which no institutional argument can absorb.

Give the interregnum to an unnamed senior khatun from a powerful western marriage house. Her three winters preserve the army, execute rivals and negotiate the Partition. Sorqai can later invoke her precedent without inheriting her identity or office. Treat the eastern expedition as a genuine, partially suppressed defeat remembered most clearly by the households abandoned during retreat.

This arrangement gives the Tarkhan a large history rather than a single conqueror. It also provides stories at several scales: the mother separated from an adopted daughter, the engineer coerced into siege work, the city deciding whether to open its gate, the companion refusing Qarayel and the khatun keeping four hostile inheritances alive through winter.
