# First Unknown Histories — Six Recoverable Lore Capsules

> [!warning] Nursery material
> These six histories are author-only exploratory truth. They do not enter chronology, canon, or [[The Historical Seed Garden]] until separately reviewed and promoted. Titles, personal names, exact locations, and surviving institutions remain provisional.

## Purpose and Reading Rule

These are the first histories built specifically to be **unknown in 1360 AR without being unknowable**. No living person, archive, supernatural witness, relic, or surviving polity possesses the complete account. Each central event can nevertheless be established by combining evidence that arose for different reasons and survived in different hands.

The capsules are deliberately independent. They are not six chapters of one secret war, do not share a hidden order, and do not explain the rise or fall of an age. Three deepen existing great-power ecologies; three are new plantings. The actor balance is two principally human histories, two mixed histories, and two histories in which the principal struggle belongs to greater powers.

# UH-01 — The Caravan That Cast Off Its Wards

- **Identity boundary:** this is not the court, petition tradition, population, ruin, ballast register, or present claim behind the [[The Historical Seed Garden#Late Chains — Petitions of the Cut-Loose Wards|Petitions of the Cut-Loose Wards]]. It concerns a different Giant court whose distinctive crime was using copied ancestors and three false evacuation processions to redirect pursuit. No document, claimant, route stone, or descendant is shared between the two roots.
- **Review disposition:** `revise` — preserve the escape, abandonment, copied ancestors, Hunt misdirection, gate killings, and recoverable court lie while replacing the Cut-Loose Wards' petition-and-ballast carriers with decoy itineraries, route carcasses, pursuit records, and counterfeit ancestry.

## What Actually Happened

In the last years of the Chains, a lesser Dynastic Giant court learned that its palace-anchor could be driven bodily into the Egg White. The court possessed enough stored mass, skilled labor, route knowledge, and captive names to move the palace and the estates immediately surrounding it. It did not possess enough to carry the outer wards: the human tenements, servant courts, workshops, burial terraces, and the households of Giant kin judged too weak or politically unreliable to preserve. The ruler's engineers reported this plainly. The ruling household publicly answered that no ward would be abandoned.

The court then conducted what its later histories call the **Equal Lightening**. Officials inventoried every district under the language of fair contribution, but protected the ruling estates before weighing anyone else's claim. They removed anchor plates from the outer streets, drained stored warmth and breathable shelter into the palace core, and transferred the ward-gates' true directions into portable route stones. Selected human artisans and giant-blooded human servants were moved inside because the court needed their labor. Most human tenants, debtors, elderly servants, rebellious Giant cousins, and hostage children remained outside. Every human among them retained a complete `human-soul`; every genuine Giant retained `giant-continuity`. Selection concerned political usefulness and bodily survival, never degrees of soul. Unlike the separately planted Cut-Loose Wards, nobody produced a surviving collective petition or later tenant-law archive from this court.

The court also released three processions of copied ancestors. These `constructed-pattern` decoys carried court memories and displayed the ruler's face, but none was the ruler or any dead ancestor. One was sent along the route promised to the human wards, one toward a pursuing Idol proxy, and one into the jurisdiction of a High Hunt whose natural claim the court had violated by taking a river-current out of the Material world. The copies contradicted one another because each had been given a different official account. The deception delayed the pursuers long enough for the palace to cross. It also caused the High Hunt to strike an abandoned refugee column after mistaking its stolen court banners for evidence that the ruler travelled among them.

When the anchor plates left, the outer wards did not vanish poetically. Floors tilted toward doorways that no longer opened onto streets. Roofs lost the stable pressure that kept them raised. Stored heat fled through exposed anchor channels. Several hundred people died in collapses, fires, fighting at the closed inner gate, or the first white cold. Others escaped into the Material countryside or followed fragments of the false routes. A group of tenants reached the palace gate during the final separation. Palace guards killed some and cut the bridge while families were still crossing. The cut hinge later travelled with a false procession, while the bodies and bridge itself were lost.

The palace survived as a moving Egg White caravan and eventually acquired Patron relationships with later tenants and clients. Its official memory now calls the abandoned wards a voluntary rear guard and insists that the copied processions were rescue missions. No current speaker for the caravan witnessed the departure. Its oldest apparent ancestors are the decoys or copies of later copies, and each consistently repeats the account assigned to its line without thereby possessing memory, personhood, or the original ancestor's continuity. The genuine ruler's subsequent fate is unknown: the same caravan may still carry the ruler, may have passed to an heir, or may now be governed by an office presenting the old face.

The abandoned people did not become one hidden nation. Some joined Exodus columns, some entered successor states, some died, and some were rewritten into later route laments. Their shared record is not a petition but a cluster of interrupted service itineraries: lists of people ordered to report to three evacuation processions which could not all have been genuine.

## What Later Traditions Say

- **The caravan account** remembers a perfectly proportioned evacuation in which every household chose one member to remain and guide the pursuing Hunt away.
- **Route-family laments** preserve an accusation that the palace assigned the poor to roads its own engineers knew were false after loading gold, archives, hunting beasts, and private gardens onto the genuine route.
- **Several Giant-descended houses** claim their ancestors refused escape out of loyalty to humanity. Two decoy itineraries instead assign those houses to politically expendable processions.
- **High Hunt folklore** calls the refugee dead oath-thieves because they carried court banners. This is true of the banners and false of the people beneath them.

## Source-Fidelity Ruling

- **Received source:** Exodus 14; European Wild Hunt families requiring later regional attribution; Æsir–Vanir hostage and settlement grammar; established World Egg Giant-flight material.
- **Borrowed element:** a ruling power pursues or sacrifices dependants during flight, while hostage, banner, and substitute identities confuse responsibility.
- **Original hard edge:** drowning or destruction in pursuit, hostage exchange, abandonment, doomed riders, and the death of people treated as expendable.
- **Current state:** `intact`.
- **Restoration:** none. Closed gates, route deaths, killed tenants, abandoned children, and culpable rulers remain explicit.
- **Historical layer:** exploratory authorial fact beneath Giant court history, service itineraries, copied testimony, route-family laments, and Hunt folklore.

## Human Coherence

- **Starting condition:** the palace can escape only if it carries less territory and fewer inhabitants than the court publicly promised.
- **Chosen action:** the ruling household exempts its own estates, strips the outer wards, selects useful servants, and assigns copied ancestors, stolen banners, and refugee processions to three false routes.
- **Supernatural trigger:** removing the wards' anchor plates and transferring their true directions into the palace route stones.
- **Mechanism in plain language:** the plates and stones are the built structures keeping the wards physically coherent along the crossing. Once removed, the palace retains stable floors, air, heat, and direction while the wards lose them.
- **Immediate observable effect:** buildings lean, heat drains from walls, doors open into white distance, the palace bridge separates, and the Hunt follows stolen banners down the wrong route.
- **Direct cost:** collapse, exposure, killing at the gate, separated families, abandoned Giant kin, and the destruction of a refugee column.
- **Later consequence:** mobile court propaganda, three incompatible evacuation laments, rival Giant genealogies, and a Patron caravan whose ownership remains disputed.
- **Limit and failed inference:** this proves one court's escape and betrayal. It does not prove that all escaped Giants acted alike, that every later palace is genuine, or that the High Hunt morally judged the victims.

## Discovery Interface

- **Knowledge state:** `unknown`, with parts also `suppressed` and `misclassified`.
- **Limited contemporary witnesses:** a human itinerary clerk assigned households to three processions but never learned which route was genuine; an excluded Giant cousin saw the protected loading order but died before learning which decoy survived; each copied ancestor reproduces only its assigned false mission; a Hunt token records the stolen banner and violated current but cannot identify innocent bearers.
- **Primary remainders:** three mutually exclusive service itineraries bearing the same departure hour, a bridge hinge cut from the palace side, the carcasses of two false procession-anchors, route stones carrying copied household seals, Hunt scoring marks, and ancestor-patterns with incompatible childhoods.
- **Independent evidence classes:** material, documentary, procedural, testimonial, and comparative.
- **Transmission damage:** court agents destroyed the genuine route roster; successor courts reused procession seals; later route families combined two laments; translations treated contradictory itineraries as alternative ritual orders; copies of copies harmonized their childhoods while retaining different departure commands.
- **1360 AR access point:** salvage from a newly wrecked palace decoy brings one functioning ancestor-pattern, a Hunt-marked route stone, and an itinerary fragment into the same Vortharan classification hearing.
- **Reproducible test:** investigators arrange recovered route stones according to the notch order on each service itinerary. Each complete false sequence opens for seconds onto a different dead procession-march bearing the same palace seal; mixing sequences opens nothing. The test establishes coordinated decoys, not the genuine palace's present route.
- **Plausible false reading:** the outer wards were detached to save them from an Idol attack, and later route families blamed rescuers for an unavoidable failure. The genuine pursuit damage makes this reading attractive.
- **Corroboration threshold:** the three contradictory itineraries, palace-side bridge cut, two route carcasses, Hunt scoring marks, and ancestor-pattern commands must agree before deliberate abandonment and pursuit fraud are treated as established.
- **What remains unknowable:** the ruler's name and present continuity, which decoy caused the refugee massacre, the final fate of most tenants, and the caravan's current location.
- **Protected Weird check:** it neither explains nor locates the Glass or Bird-Marked Sites and does not identify Anshara's final sovereign.

# UH-02 — The Peace Feast at Nine Hearths

- **Identity boundary:** this is an independent Scattering settlement and not an episode of the Crownless Company, Seven Who Took the Wrong Road, or any already planted founder genealogy. Its visible Genesis 34 inheritance remains source grammar rather than shared identity.
- **Review disposition:** `retain` — its refugee famine, guest-law assault, noncovenantal disarmament machinery, mixed survivor community, maternal legal reforms, and archaeological recovery change the required axes while preserving the source's rape, deceit, massacre, plunder, and coerced kinship.

## What Actually Happened

During the Scattering, a refugee lineage arrived at a nine-household grain settlement after losing most of its animals and winter seed. The settlement's ruling son assaulted a young woman of the refugee lineage while she was collecting water under the settlement's guest protection. He then demanded marriage and offered grain, land, and admission for her kin. His father treated the proposal as compensation capable of settling both the crime and the refugees' need. The woman refused the marriage. Neither ruling household allowed her refusal to control what followed.

Her brothers and maternal uncles proposed a **Peace Feast**. The host settlement would admit the refugees as kin after every fighting adult surrendered weapons for one ritual night and accepted a new hearth-mark cut into the forearm. The mark was not circumcision and did not carry the same covenantal meaning as Genesis 34; it was a local disarmament and admission procedure used to prevent bloodshed between newly joined households. The hosts accepted because they expected new labor, marriages, and an end to the accusation against the ruler's son.

The refugees attacked during the feast. They killed the assailant, his father, armed retainers, unarmed householders, servants, and several guests who had no part in the assault. They seized the granary, livestock, tools, and children. Surviving women and children were divided among the refugee households as dependants, servants, or coerced spouses. The assaulted woman survived the night and was taken away with her kin. One broken tablet preserves a refusal attributed to her—*no feast for me*—but no evidence establishes where she later lived, whether she was free to leave, or whether the words are hers.

The attackers did not simply replace the settlement. They needed the survivors' knowledge of seed, wells, winter roofs, and neighboring obligations. Within two generations, the victors and captives formed one community in law while remaining sharply divided in household memory. The new founders prohibited armed admission feasts, required women to testify separately in marriage disputes, and gave maternal households an unusual right to challenge land inheritance. These reforms did not undo the assault, killings, enslavement, or coerced marriages. They were built by descendants living inside their consequences.

Later rulers could not preserve both a peaceful founding and the community's distinctive laws. They recast the assault as an elopement, the disarmament as wedding preparation, and the mass killing as a plague that struck during the feast. Captive households became honored hosts who had voluntarily shared their grain. The founder lineage's possession of the granary became a bridal gift. Maternal laments preserved in neighboring settlements retained the accusation, but later singers no longer knew which woman, household, or town the verses concerned.

No supernatural power caused the crime or punishment. A later local Mask cultivated honor-killing and marriage coercion around the story, but it entered a structure humans had already built. The Year of Standing Stars does not occur in any primary layer and supplies no explanation for the lost date.

## What Later Traditions Say

- **The official founding song** describes nine hearths opening voluntarily to starving travelers through a marriage that united two peoples.
- **A neighboring lament family** remembers a woman whose attackers and avengers both bargained over her body and whose answer neither side recorded.
- **Founder law** calls the weapon surrender a precaution against plague delirium, although the bronze tokens fit weapon-rack locks rather than medical equipment.
- **Descendant households** dispute whether maternal challenge rights honor the founding bride or protect families descended from captives.

## Source-Fidelity Ruling

- **Received source:** Genesis 34 in the Orthodox Old Testament; dangerous-hospitality and violent-founder grammars used only at the structural level.
- **Borrowed element:** sexual violation followed by a proposed kinship settlement, deceptive disarmament, collective retaliatory killing, plunder, and the seizure of dependants.
- **Original hard edge:** rape, refusal denied political force, deceit, massacre beyond the guilty party, enslavement, and irreparable kin guilt.
- **Current state:** `intact`.
- **Restoration:** none. The assault, massacre, enslavement, coerced marriage, and survivor-built law remain causal rather than warning-note material.
- **Historical layer:** exploratory authorial fact beneath a founding song, maternal laments, inheritance law, and plague propaganda.

## Human Coherence

- **Starting condition:** starving refugees need admission and grain; the host ruler's son has violated a woman protected as a guest.
- **Chosen action:** host rulers try to settle the crime through marriage and property; the woman's kin pretend to accept and use the admission rite to disarm the settlement before attacking everyone.
- **Supernatural trigger:** none.
- **Mechanism in plain language:** bronze tokens lock surrendered weapons into communal racks for the feast night. Refugee attackers retain hidden tools and open the granary while the settlement is disarmed.
- **Immediate observable effect:** the assailant and many uninvolved people are killed, stores and children are seized, and captive households are distributed among the attackers.
- **Direct cost:** rape, murder, enslavement, coerced marriage, stolen land, famine risk, and a woman denied authority over decisions ostensibly made for her.
- **Later consequence:** one mixed community, a false marriage foundation, maternal inheritance protections, separate testimony rules, and households whose land claims descend from both victims and killers.
- **Limit and failed inference:** the initial assault does not justify collective massacre. The massacre does not make every descendant personally guilty or every later marriage coerced.

## Discovery Interface

- **Knowledge state:** `lost` and `misclassified`; no secret institution retains the whole account.
- **Limited contemporary witnesses:** a host granary apprentice survived the attack but knew nothing of the assault; a refugee ritual officiant recorded the disarmament but left before the killing ended; the alleged refusal tablet preserves one woman's voice or a near-contemporary attribution but not her later life; captive maternal songs name losses without describing the attackers' famine.
- **Primary remainders:** a concealed killing layer beneath the feast court, bodies bearing fresh hearth cuts, weapon-rack tokens in refugee graves, a granary seal later used as a bridal emblem, mixed household tools, and the refusal tablet.
- **Independent evidence classes:** material, documentary, procedural, comparative, and negative.
- **Transmission damage:** the first town burned; founder priests combined two rolls; maternal songs traveled without place names; rulers moved the feast court; later plague burials obscured the killing layer; captive descendants sometimes adopted the peaceful account to defend their land rights.
- **1360 AR access point:** a succession case turns on whether maternal challenge rights arise from a founding marriage or an imposed captive settlement. Repair of the old feast court exposes the earlier bodies.
- **Reproducible test:** surviving bronze "wedding tokens" fit the locking slots of excavated communal weapon racks and bear wear from repeated use. Replicas cut to the same measure lock and release the racks. The test establishes organized disarmament, not who planned the subsequent attack.
- **Plausible false reading:** a plague killed the hosts after a peaceful marriage, and frightened survivors buried bodies hurriedly while refugees inherited the empty settlement.
- **Corroboration threshold:** traumatic burial context, weapon-lock function, the granary apprentice fragment, maternal laments, and the legal anomaly must overlap before assault followed by planned massacre is accepted.
- **What remains unknowable:** the woman's name and later fate, the precise number killed or enslaved, which attackers objected, and whether the refusal tablet records her exact words.
- **Protected Weird check:** no primary or procedural evidence connects the event to the Year of Standing Stars.

# UH-03 — The Prisoners Who Became a Border

- **Identity boundary:** this is one bounded mortal summoning disaster around c. 830–790 BR, crossing the late City-State and early Star-age boundary. It is not the origin, decisive front, or final battle of the [[Great-Power Mythic Ecology and Planting Matrix#GP-II-02 — The War of the Last Map|War of the Last Map]], and it shares no prisoners, map, fortification, or office-bearer with another planted infernal history.
- **Review disposition:** `retain` — preserve the coerced boundary system, two hostile dominions, prisoner revolt, collapsed route, and forensic road test while making bearer, office, command token, body, and soul explicit.

## What Actually Happened

Around c. 830–790 BR, late City-State summoners opened one controlled route to an infernal road they believed belonged to a disciplined military dominion. The road's map-lord promised reliable intelligence against a rival city and protection from the "formless enemy" pressing against its frontier. The map-lord was a damned human person retaining `human-soul`; the map mantle it bore was a separately classified devil `infrastructure-office`. Neither bearer nor office was the Adversary, a god, or a necessary guardian of creation. Its dominion survived by assigning every prisoner, road, sentence, and gate a fixed place in a single enforceable map.

The map-lord had deliberately captured a bounded Abyssal wound and driven it toward a rival infernal jurisdiction. The wound was not an army or a people. It was a contradiction that caused mapped differences to fail: near and far exchanged places, prisoner and jailer ceased to remain separate in records, and an order could precede the act that produced it. To prevent the same failure from consuming its own road, the map-lord required living boundary markers. Mortal summoners supplied prisoners taken in debt raids, war, and criminal sentences. Infernal officers branded each body with a road-name and chained the person to one fixed station.

The rival dominion did not liberate them. Its Crowned Refusers and office-bearers captured boundary stations, mutilated surviving markers into rival signs, and sent Abyssal failures back along the same human chain. Both sides treated each captive as a replaceable coordinate. The prisoners remained human persons with `human-soul`; no brand divided, sold, or erased the Image. The copied road-names were command tokens inside the devil office, not persons, souls, or continuations of their bearers. Several prisoners died under torture, exposure, forced office-bearing, or the physical collapse of repeated roads. The dead routed according to the ordinary soul-road unless separately intercepted. Their bodies and command tokens continued to be used without becoming them.

The prisoners discovered that the road read the engraved station-name before it read the body. They began exchanging collar plates and secretly rotating stations. Infernal commands arrived at the wrong bodies, patrols marched in circles, and the directed Abyssal contradiction consumed the false path prepared for the rival instead of the prisoners' escape tunnel. During the final revolt they broke the central index, removed the named plates from the last seven stations, and collapsed the mortal summoning house that supplied replacements. Many prisoners and several mortal rescuers died. The local route folded shut around both hostile garrisons.

The act contained one weaponized wound by depriving it of the human chain through which it had been directed. It did not heal the Abyss, defeat Hell, redeem any map-lord, or end the War of the Last Map. Later summoners recovered the military maps but classified the scratched prisoner directions as errors made by uneducated captives. City historians recast the collapsed summoning house as a defensive fort destroyed by an enemy earthquake.

## What Later Traditions Say

- **Infernal manuals** describe the lost road as a heroic containment line betrayed by unreliable mortal components.
- **A city chronicle** claims condemned criminals volunteered to become wards protecting their families.
- **Prison songs** remember people escaping by exchanging names, which later occultists misread as literal soul exchange.
- **Rival map fragments** each place the collapse inside enemy territory, because both dominions redrew the boundary after losing it.

## Source-Fidelity Ruling

- **Received source:** Daniel 10; Revelation 12 and 20; familiar Gigantomachy; D&D's Blood War as structural inspiration only.
- **Borrowed element:** a conflict among hostile supernatural orders produces continuing siege, conscription, captivity, propaganda, and mortal proxy economies without making either side good.
- **Original hard edge:** enslavement, mutilation, coerced office-bearing, siege, and recurring destruction.
- **Current state:** `intact`.
- **Restoration:** none. Prisoners are bodily used as infrastructure and win only a bounded, costly revolt.
- **Historical layer:** exploratory authorial episode inside an older infernal ecology, later preserved in occult manuals, city propaganda, prison songs, and route remains.

## Human Coherence

- **Starting condition:** a map-lord wants to direct an Abyssal contradiction at a rival without losing its own roads.
- **Chosen action:** mortal summoners deliver prisoners; infernal officers brand and station them; the rival dominion reuses captured bodies; prisoners exchange the plates that tell the road where each body belongs.
- **Supernatural trigger:** a named plate is fixed to a branded station inside the mapped road.
- **Mechanism in plain language:** commands and distances follow the station-name engraved on the plate. Moving plates sends orders and patrols to the wrong physical locations.
- **Immediate observable effect:** patrols repeat the same distance, orders reach the wrong captives, fortress corridors overlap, and the prisoners reach the central index.
- **Direct cost:** torture, mutilation, deaths, destroyed bodies, collapsed roads, and survivors carrying unwanted office habits into ordinary life.
- **Later consequence:** corrupted infernal maps, veteran office-manifestations, prison songs about exchanged names, and a mortal road segment whose measured distance changes under named infernal markers.
- **Limit and failed inference:** exchanging plates does not exchange souls or identities. Destroying the index contains one directed wound and proves nothing about the wider war's beginning or end.

## Discovery Interface

- **Knowledge state:** `misclassified` and `lost`.
- **Limited contemporary witnesses:** a mortal summoner knew the supply contract but never saw the front; one prison scratch-roll records station exchanges but not the map-lord's plan; a surviving devil mandate names the military objective while treating people as numbers; later prisoner songs preserve the revolt but compress several leaders into one.
- **Primary remainders:** branded bodies at fixed intervals, detachable named plates on the wrong bodies, repeated fortress plans, a broken central index, a collapsed summoning cellar, and a road segment that responds differently to named and blank markers.
- **Independent evidence classes:** material, documentary, procedural, testimonial, comparative, and negative.
- **Transmission damage:** city courts destroyed evidence of unlawful prisoners; occult schools copied the maps without the scratches; infernal agents redrew the front; burial crews removed collars from bodies; later translators rendered road-names as moral ranks.
- **1360 AR access point:** quarrying beneath a Star-age city opens the collapsed summoning cellar and exposes a forty-pace road that surveyors cannot measure consistently.
- **Reproducible test:** observers fix a rope outside the affected segment and send weighted sleds through it. Blank markers traverse forty paces. A recovered named plate makes the sled repeat the plate's former station interval before emerging. No living bearer is required. The test establishes a procedural road command, not ownership by either infernal claimant.
- **Plausible false reading:** the site was a heavenly prison whose condemned volunteers held back the Abyss; the repeated road is therefore evidence that infernal discipline is cosmically necessary.
- **Corroboration threshold:** road behavior, mismatched plates and bodies, the supply contract, prisoner scratches, and rival mandates must agree before coerced human boundary use and revolt are established.
- **What remains unknowable:** the map-lord's personal identity, the number of prisoners, how many escaped, the present condition of either trapped garrison, and the wider war's first cause or final front.
- **Protected Weird check:** the episode neither causes nor explains the Year of Standing Stars.

# UH-04 — The Refuge at the Living Furnace

- **Identity boundary:** this failed single-generation furnace platform is not EL-R01, the **Living Brass Refuge**, and shares no owner, population, government, anchor, route, industry, or present settlement with that still-unplaced research lead. One is a small destroyed refuge-workshop; the other remains available as a living cosmopolitan Fire-littoral metropolis.
- **Review disposition:** `revise` — preserve real rescue, hereditary furnace slavery, revolt, gate violence, continuity classification, and the emergency-door test while reducing the polity from a metropolis to a failed fortified platform and closing any implication of shared survival with EL-R01.

## What Actually Happened

During the Long Frontier, several hundred people fled the destruction of a fortified river district. A route broker offered passage to a refuge where no frontier ruler could reach them. The destination was a small constructed littoral inside the Fire quadrant of the Elemental Shell: one fortified furnace platform of kilns, metal galleries, cooled barracks, and pressure conduits built around an Astral anchor and tethered to a great Material furnace. It was never a metropolis, free port, or capital and failed within its founding generation. The surrounding Shell was sterile state-of-matter pressure. It had no native people, king, will, or moral claim.

The refuge's owner was an Anchor-Lord with `astral-pattern`, acting as Patron through ownership of the anchor and route. Its human residents retained `human-soul`. Native Astral servants likewise had `astral-pattern`; later Driftkin workers had `drift-rewritten-life`; cooling frames and adaptive furnace systems used `shell-pattern` or `constructed-pattern` and were not persons. No one became an elemental by residence. The Patron initially provided real shelter, water condensed through imported apparatus, and a return route. It also required every admitted household to supply maintenance labor.

The first compact limited service to seven years. After a conduit failure killed experienced workers, the Patron's magistrates reclassified their outstanding shifts as household debt. Children inherited the missing labor. Criminal sentences, insolvency, and unpaid passage could all be converted into dangerous furnace duty. The government placed slaves, condemned prisoners, and debtors nearest the failure points because replacing them was cheaper than shutting the industrial galleries. People burned, suffocated, or died when cooling chambers failed. Refuge was genuine; so was the hereditary slavery built out of its upkeep.

The platform survived by releasing waste heat through the Material furnace that anchored its return. That furnace stood inside an old forge district whose workers still understood its ordinary mechanisms. Refugee families in the Material world received smuggled maintenance tallies and learned what the refuge had become. They did not cast a universal spell. They extinguished the furnace, shattered two heat-sink collars, and opened its emergency vents at the same hour that enslaved workers inside the refuge closed the industrial galleries and jammed the labor registers into the pressure valves.

With no Material heat sink, the platform began to melt from its outer galleries inward. The Patron could preserve its private hall and treasury only by releasing stored route pressure and reopening the return gate. It chose itself. Hundreds reached the gate, but guards and privileged households fought for passage. Some laborers killed magistrates; some guards killed refugees; several families were crushed or burned in the galleries. The Patron escaped with a smaller anchor fragment and some clients. The outer works failed completely. A few sealed service vaults may have remained habitable for days or months, but no continuing polity or present route is established.

Returnees concealed the full history for incompatible reasons. Frontier rulers did not want proof that they had driven subjects into extrarealm bondage. Merchant houses did not want hereditary labor contracts tested against their own passage debts. Some survivors defended the refuge because it had saved their families before enslaving them. Others feared that admitting any sealed vault had outlasted the revolt would invite another extraction campaign. The Patron's personal name, later anchor, and the fate of those who remained are not recoverable from known evidence.

## What Later Traditions Say

- **Frontier sermons** describe a false paradise destroyed when refugees worshipped its fiery ruler.
- **Merchant records** call the refuge a voluntary industrial colony whose workers rioted after refusing lawful debts.
- **Survivor households** disagree over whether the Patron was rescuer, slaver, or both at different stages.
- **Shell prospectors** claim the melted platform belonged to a native fire civilization. Its tools actually repeat human and Astral workshop measures.

## Source-Fidelity Ruling

- **Received source:** Exodus 1 forced labor; Daniel 3 and the furnace tradition; D&D and Pathfinder's living City-of-Brass structure, not the original *Arabian Nights* dead warning-city.
- **Borrowed element:** refugees or captives preserved inside a furnace installation whose engineered shelter depends upon coercive labor and lethal exposure.
- **Original hard edge:** slavery, inherited work burdens, punishment, execution or burning, and rulers treating workers as expendable infrastructure.
- **Current state:** `intact`.
- **Restoration:** none. The refuge saves lives, then converts maintenance into hereditary bondage; the revolt kills and does not rescue everyone.
- **Historical layer:** exploratory authorial fact beneath frontier sermons, survivor accounts, merchant contracts, and Shell-prospector error.

## Human Coherence

- **Starting condition:** refugees face death or enslavement on the frontier and accept passage to an engineered refuge requiring continual labor and a Material heat sink.
- **Chosen action:** the Patron's government converts emergency labor arrears into hereditary debt; workers and Material allies later disable the heat system to force the return gate open.
- **Supernatural trigger:** the Material furnace and Astral anchor are run as one cooling circuit. Closing the furnace while blocking internal pressure valves removes the platform's ability to discharge heat.
- **Mechanism in plain language:** heat accumulates in the outer galleries. The Patron must spend stored route power either preserving the whole refuge temporarily or opening an exit while saving its own anchor.
- **Immediate observable effect:** metalwork softens, cooling walls crack, the outer galleries fill with lethal heat, and the return doorway reappears inside the Material furnace hall.
- **Direct cost:** years of slavery, burned and suffocated workers, fighting at the gate, abandoned residents, destroyed homes, and a smaller Patron court escaping with portable wealth.
- **Later consequence:** hidden survivor lines, disputed passage debts, a forbidden platform diagram, heat-altered tools, and merchant law separating rescue obligations from inherited service.
- **Limit and failed inference:** the platform's existence does not make the Shell inhabited by native elemental nations. Its collapse does not prove every inhabitant died, establish a surviving city, or show that every Patron refuge is fraudulent.

- **Primary remainders:** heat-altered human tools, multilingual labor tallies, household debt contracts, death lists, shattered Material heat-sink collars, one melted platform, and one emergency doorway mechanism.
- **Independent evidence classes:** material, documentary, procedural, testimonial, comparative, and negative.
- **Transmission damage:** merchant houses split contracts by language; frontier governments confiscated survivor testimony; prospectors looted the galleries; later copies converted labor categories into moral castes; the Patron's proxies removed its personal name from every portable instrument.
- **1360 AR access point:** repair of the old Material forge uncovers the emergency collars and a sealed register naming living descendant households as still owing furnace shifts.
- **Reproducible test:** when the two surviving collars are mounted in their original order and the furnace reaches the recorded temperature, the emergency doorway appears for less than a minute onto the same dead outer gallery. Reversing either collar prevents the opening. The test proves a working historical correspondence, not a safe route or the Patron's survival.
- **Plausible false reading:** the refugees willingly colonized a native elemental fortress and destroyed it through religious rebellion; present descendants therefore inherit the refuge's unpaid debt.
- **Corroboration threshold:** the seven-year admission compact, inherited tallies, death distribution, Material sabotage record, human workshop measures, and doorway test must agree before refuge-turned-slavery is established.
- **What remains unknowable:** the Patron's name and later fate, the exact population, how long the sealed service vaults remained habitable, the fate of those left behind, and whether any individuals escaped by another route. No current Shell settlement is presumed to descend from the platform.

# UH-05 — The Returners' Second Sack

- **Identity boundary:** this is not the [[The Historical Seed Garden#Fall and Reconquest — The Unreturned Ledger|Unreturned Ledger]], its refugee treasury, its clerks, its service-credit tradition, or any parcel associated with the Deed in the Bread Jar. It is an unrelated Material town, municipal water-court register, mass execution, and title-tablet fraud. Resembling legal problems do not imply documentary descent.
- **Review disposition:** `revise` — preserve the mixed occupation society, executions, winter expulsions, destroyed register, backdated grants, and present forensic lawsuit while fixing the episode as a separate municipal crime with no Patron, Egg White treasury, carried property ledger, or protected household deed.

## What Actually Happened

During the Fall, one provincial town surrendered after its garrison fled and its refugee elite escaped with the army. The occupiers appointed local stewards, imported officials, and occupation-born families to keep the wells, mills, hospital, grain court, and tax rolls operating. Some stewards enriched themselves, informed on resistance households, seized abandoned property, or assisted forced requisitions. Others kept people alive, hid fugitives, paid ransoms, and treated the property of absent refugees as a trust rather than spoil. Over two generations these categories became entangled through marriage, debt, coercion, and ordinary survival.

Before the reconquest, the town's water court wrote a maintenance register recording who had repaired each property, which refugee claims remained valid, which estates had been confiscated, and which occupants had earned a hearing before removal. The register did not grant permanent ownership automatically. It made return conditional upon distinguishing collaboration, necessity, service, purchase, coercion, and theft. Copies went to a merchant house, a frontier monastery, and the occupation tax office.

A returning claimant army retook the town after a short siege. Its leaders needed pay for soldiers and estates for political supporters. They also wanted a clean story in which every person found inside the walls was an occupier or willing collaborator. The commander ordered the steward council, mixed households named by refugee claimants, and several occupation-born clergy arrested before the water register could be heard. Some prisoners had committed murder or confiscation. Others were guilty only of birth, marriage, or maintaining civic works. The army executed them together outside the hospital wall.

The victors then burned the principal maintenance register and issued replacement titles dated as though the refugee houses had retained uninterrupted possession. They granted estates to supporters who had never owned them and converted repairs made by resident families into debts supposedly owed to the returning claimants. Soldiers killed people resisting eviction, and dependants were expelled during winter. This was the town's second sack: smaller than the original conquest, committed in the name of restoration, and omitted from the victorious chronicle.

The deception never became perfect. The returning administration needed the occupiers' tax figures, water schedules, and repair knowledge, so clerks scraped older tablets and wrote the new titles over them. A hostile merchant retained paired entries because it was still owed money by both sides. The frontier monastery preserved a partial copy while removing names of families it had secretly sheltered. Later descendants inherited real refugee losses, real occupation crimes, real service claims, and fraudulent titles simultaneously.

The event does not make the Reconquest illegitimate or its opponents innocent. It fixes one entirely Material town in which a just cause did not prevent victorious people from committing murder, dispossession, and documentary fraud. No Patron, Egg White refuge, portable treasury, carried-out property register, or bread-jar deed participates. Repentance by individual soldiers or later restitution cannot resurrect the executed or make the first generation of false grants true.

## What Later Traditions Say

- **The victory chronicle** says occupation troops massacred their own stewards before fleeing and that returning claimants restored the town without reprisal.
- **Current noble houses** possess apparently continuous titles beginning before the Fall, several of which were actually created after the reconquest.
- **Occupation-descended families** remember every executed steward as a civic martyr, including documented informers and confiscators.
- **Water workers** preserve the phrase *the well served the hands that kept it*, without remembering the hearing procedure from which it came.

## Source-Fidelity Ruling

- **Received source:** 1–2 Maccabees as resistance, political succession, retaliation, and competing sacred history; Baruch, Lamentations, Ezra, and 1 Esdras as exile-and-return grammars.
- **Borrowed element:** liberation becomes government, and restoration produces contested succession, purges, property claims, competing chronicles, and violence under sacred language.
- **Original hard edge:** siege, execution, reprisal killing, confiscation, civil division, and the inability of later sacred memory to make every victorious act righteous.
- **Current state:** `intact`.
- **Restoration:** none. The reconquerors execute guilty and innocent people together, dispossess dependants, and falsify titles.
- **Historical layer:** exploratory authorial fact beneath victorious chronicle, occupation apologetic, title law, water custom, and merchant accounts.

## Human Coherence

- **Starting condition:** a retaken town contains collaborators, coerced officials, rescuers, ordinary families, and valuable property claimed by refugees and soldiers.
- **Chosen action:** the returning commander refuses individual hearings, executes the mixed steward class, burns the maintenance register, and rewards supporters with replacement titles.
- **Supernatural trigger:** none.
- **Mechanism in plain language:** clerks scrape occupation tablets, backdate new deeds, reuse older seals, and copy necessary tax figures while removing the names of resident claimants.
- **Immediate observable effect:** prisoners die outside the hospital, families are expelled, soldiers occupy granted estates, and the water court loses the record required to hear competing claims.
- **Direct cost:** executions, winter deaths, stolen property, rewarded informers, ruined refugee claims, and descendants forced to defend identities built from both service and wrongdoing.
- **Later consequence:** noble titles with concealed breaks, occupation-descended grievance, distorted martyr lists, and present litigation over water and repair rights.
- **Limit and failed inference:** one criminal reconquest episode does not invalidate the wider liberation, make every occupier innocent, or assign hereditary guilt to returning families.

## Discovery Interface

- **Knowledge state:** `suppressed` at first, later `lost` and `misclassified`.
- **Limited contemporary witnesses:** the returning chaplain recorded executions but believed all prisoners were collaborators; a water clerk knew the service classifications but fled before the second sack; a merchant copyist preserved paired debts without understanding the killings; a soldier's confession names forged grants but not every beneficiary.
- **Primary remainders:** the hospital-wall grave, reconstruction-era weapons among the dead, scraped tax tablets beneath current titles, repair marks predating reconquest grants, merchant double entries, and the monastery's redacted register.
- **Independent evidence classes:** material, documentary, testimonial, comparative, and negative.
- **Transmission damage:** victorious archives destroyed the main register; the monastery erased protected names; families split documents during inheritance; occupation apologists canonized criminals; later title courts treated reused seals as proof of continuity.
- **1360 AR access point:** expansion of the hospital uncovers the grave while a water-right lawsuit forces three current houses to produce their oldest title tablets.
- **Reproducible test:** warming and re-waxing the title tablets causes the deeper occupation-era impressions to take ink before the later writing. Independent tablets reveal the same removed service categories and different original holders. The process establishes rewriting but does not by itself determine rightful present ownership.
- **Plausible false reading:** occupation troops killed the stewards before retreating, and returning clerks merely reused enemy tablets during an emergency restoration.
- **Corroboration threshold:** grave dating and weapon context, under-impressions, merchant entries, monastery copy, and repair chronology must align before the second sack and deliberate title fraud are established.
- **What remains unknowable:** every prisoner's individual guilt, the commander's private motive, the number who died after eviction, which present heirs knew of the fraud, and the final equitable distribution of current property.
- **Protected Weird check:** the event neither involves nor explains the Thirty Sisters and does not predetermine present property judgements.

# UH-06 — The Saint Who Guarded the Enemy Gate

- **Identity boundary:** this landlocked fortress episode is not SB-P04, the **Saint of the Two Ferries**, and shares no saint, city, ferry, river, hospice, relic, icon, cult, witness family, or manifestation with that retained placement. It is a separate use of the broader common-saint and wrong-gate grammars at an inland military prison.
- **Review disposition:** `revise` — preserve partisan invocation, Patron and Mask counterfeits, executions, desecration, bounded rescue, and forensic gate response while replacing the paired ferry cities with one mountain fortress, rival military cults, a different saint, prison architecture, and an arsenal hearing.

## What Actually Happened

In the violent decades after the Great Split, two rival armies met at a landlocked mountain fortress controlling a grain road and three winter passes. Both armies venerated the same early military saint: a former army physician remembered for refusing to certify tortured prisoners as fit for execution. The saint was not the holy person of the Two Ferries and possessed no river, ferry, or hospice cult. One army's chaplains called the saint the Physician of the Western Host; the other remembered the same person as the Keeper of Captive Soldiers. Each demanded a sign proving that its military communion alone inherited the saint's protection.

Several impressive signs followed, but most were not the saint. A Patron had leased a familiar armored apparition to the attacking army in exchange for captured winter stores and future passage rights. The apparition revealed an old supply postern because the contract named the commander's seal, not because the saint endorsed the assault. Inside the fortress, an Envy Mask cultivated the garrison cult's humiliation. Officers stole badges and finger bones from enemy graves, named rival captains before a sweating campaign icon, and received dreams showing enemies begging beneath the fortress standard. Human commanders then beat prisoners, mutilated messengers, desecrated bodies, and killed suspected collaborators. The counterfeits exploited chosen acts; they did not commit the crimes in human hands.

Prisoners from both armies, local porters, and the families of suspected collaborators were confined beside the fortress's refuse gate, the gate facing away from the contested road and therefore called the **enemy gate** by both garrisons. During the final withdrawal, the fortress commander executed several captives and ordered the rest sealed inside a granary already burning from siege fire. The genuine saint manifested there under a bounded commission to preserve the prisoners and noncombatants. Witnesses described the saint in different uniforms but agreed on the politically useless acts: the saint broke the refuse-gate windlass, dropped its iron screen between the captives and a revenge company, carried wounded children through the ash passage, and named three tortured enemy messengers omitted from both armies' rolls.

The saint did not destroy either army, crown a bishop, settle the schism, or declare which military cult possessed exclusive custody. When soldiers carried the enemy-gate key toward the command tower, it became ordinary iron. The manifestation ended after the captives crossed the ash passage into an abandoned shepherd enclosure and the dead received burial custody. A handful of witness angels may have been present according to later Lives, but no surviving earthly evidence can distinguish angelic attendants from iconographic expansion. No angelic action is required to establish the rescue.

Each army preserved the part least damaging to itself. The attackers recorded the supply postern opened by the Patron and omitted the prisoners rescued behind their advance. The former garrison celebrated the sweating icon while suppressing grave theft and torture. Local porters were dismissed as camp followers confusing a physician, jailer, and common saint. Military clerks destroyed prison rolls to hide extrajudicial killings. The two traditions therefore possess genuine relics, genuine victims, real supernatural events, and false conclusions at the same time.

The iron enemy-gate key later developed a narrow procedural response connected to the historical custody charge. The response is an `infrastructure-office` fixed in the rescued gate, not the saint's soul, a Patron, or a continuing manifestation. When a lawful custodian names a specific prisoner facing an unlawful military killing inside the fortress's old command boundary, the key releases the surviving gate windlass even when its brake is pinned. It does not open other gates, identify the saint, compel a manifestation, prove a church, or protect someone from lawful judgement. The effect is evidence only when combined with hostile records and the physical gate sequence.

## What Later Traditions Say

- **The attacking army's successor order** treats the Patron-opened supply postern as the saint's victory and calls the prisoner rescue a garrison interpolation.
- **The old garrison cult** preserves the sweating campaign icon as proof of the saint's anger while omitting the grave theft and torture that fed the Envy Mask.
- **Mountain porter songs** remember that the saint came to the enemy gate because prisoners, enemies, and children were there.
- **Relic courts** accept that both military cults preserve authentic objects from the siege but dispute whether either bodily relic belongs to the manifested saint.

## Source-Fidelity Ruling

- **Received source:** Acts 15; 1 Corinthians 1–3; Orthodox common-saint reception; Saint Nicholas's interruption of unlawful execution and rescue traditions; military-apparition traditions used at their received source layer.
- **Borrowed element:** divided communities claim one holy witness; spectacular claims compete with a bounded intervention protecting prisoners and correcting the saint's own partisans.
- **Original hard edge:** schism violence, imprisonment, beating, killing, corpse or relic desecration, and political misuse of holy memory.
- **Current state:** `intact`.
- **Restoration:** none. Named human institutions commit the violence; the real intervention saves actual prisoners without sanitizing the executions or reprisals.
- **Historical layer:** exploratory authorial fact beneath rival military Lives, Patron contract, Mask cult, porter songs, prison suppression, and later iconography.

## Human Coherence

- **Starting condition:** rival armies share an early military saint but seek exclusive legitimacy during the siege of a landlocked fortress.
- **Chosen action:** one command signs a Patron contract; rival officers feed an Envy Mask through grave theft and named revenge; the fortress commander orders executions and abandonment of prisoners in a burning granary.
- **Supernatural triggers:** the Patron responds to the contracted command seal and promised stores; the Mask responds to named enemies and stolen grave remains; the saint manifests only under the Lord's commission at the endangered prisoners.
- **Mechanism in plain language:** the Patron reveals the contracted supply postern; the Mask produces sweatlike stains and revenge dreams; the saint physically breaks the refuse-gate windlass, lowers its screen, carries victims, and ends the manifestation after custody is secured.
- **Immediate observable effect:** an army gains one entrance, reprisals intensify, prisoners are killed, and surviving prisoners and children cross the ash passage behind the lowered screen.
- **Direct cost:** beatings, executions, mob killing, shrine seizure, desecrated graves, burned property, and testimony suppressed by both cities.
- **Later consequence:** incompatible military Lives, a hidden Patron claim on winter stores, an Envy-fed campaign cult, hostile standard corrections, and a narrow gate-key procedure.
- **Limit and failed inference:** the real saintly rescue validates only the custody commission. It proves neither church's primacy, military claim, relic exclusivity, nor general innocence.

## Discovery Interface

- **Knowledge state:** `suppressed`, `misclassified`, and still `unknown` in combination.
- **Limited contemporary witnesses:** attacking clergy saw the supply postern but not the full contract; garrison officers saw the icon but concealed grave theft; prisoners and mountain porters saw the rescue but not the rival command councils; a Patron factor recorded terms without knowing whether a true saint appeared; the manifested saint named victims but supplied no general history.
- **Primary remainders:** the Patron contract under a saint's title, stores ledgers, desecrated graves, prison lists, the broken windlass, hostile corrections sewn beneath two campaign standards, porter testimony, and the enemy-gate key.
- **Independent evidence classes:** material, documentary, procedural, testimonial, comparative, and negative.
- **Transmission damage:** both military orders edited Lives independently; army courts destroyed execution orders; porter songs changed language and patronage; standard-makers copied hidden corrections without their explanation; the Patron sold later versions of the armored apparition to other commands.
- **1360 AR access point:** restoration of the abandoned fortress arsenal exposes the enemy-gate mechanism and three murdered names beneath an execution board while a military relic hearing brings the rival Lives, standards, contract, and key together.
- **Reproducible test:** with proper judicial witnesses, the key releases only the original enemy-gate windlass after a specifically named prisoner inside the fort's old command boundary has received an unlawful military death order. A false name, lawful sentence, distant prisoner, or partisan battle petition produces no response. The test never summons the saint.
- **Plausible false reading:** the saint led the attacking army through the supply postern, cursed the garrison through the sweating icon, and rescued only prisoners who converted; later rivals divided one coherent military miracle into hostile fragments.
- **Corroboration threshold:** the gate-key's bounded response, Patron contract, stolen-grave inventory, physical windlass damage, hidden standard corrections, and testimony from prisoners of both armies must agree before one genuine nonpartisan intervention and two counterfeits are treated as established.
- **What remains unknowable:** the saint's definitive military title, whether witness angels were present, which bodily relic—if either—belongs to the saint, the private motives of suppressing commanders and bishops, and whether a future commission will ever occur.
- **Protected Weird check:** the event does not cause or explain the Bells of the Split. The bells are absent from every primary layer.
