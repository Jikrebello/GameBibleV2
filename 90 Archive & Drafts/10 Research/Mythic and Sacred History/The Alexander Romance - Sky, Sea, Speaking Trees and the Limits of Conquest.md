# The Alexander Romance — Sky, Sea, Speaking Trees and the Limits of Conquest

Alexander's literary life became much larger than his empire. Greek and Roman readers inherited accounts of a Macedonian conqueror who reached India and died at Babylon. Egyptian, Syriac, Armenian, Persian, Arabic, Hebrew and European writers made him the son of an Egyptian king, a Persian prince, a seeker of immortal water, a visitor to the heavens and the sea floor, a builder of the last gate and, in some traditions, a sage or prophet. Other Iranian traditions remembered him as an accursed destroyer. These portraits disagree because communities used Alexander to think about their own rulers, enemies, sacred histories and limits of knowledge.

The World Egg can preserve that abundance without granting equal historical truth to every story. The recommended arrangement gives several famous adventures a real incident beneath them. Each incident occurs during **Eryndor III of Ortheia's** eastern campaign of c. 702–685 BR. No second world tour is required. Later cultures separate the incidents from their original military setting, combine them with local heroes and move them to the edges of their own maps.

The resulting cycle concerns a king who repeatedly tries to extend royal power into a realm it cannot possess. Eryndor descends below the sea during the siege of Terys, rises into the upper air to inspect an enemy position, demands knowledge of his death from a speaking grove, seeks water that might preserve life and closes a mountain road against a surviving horror. Each marvel is real in a limited sense. None makes him a prophet, saint, Menhir child or divinely appointed ruler.

## The growth of the Romance

The work conventionally called the *Alexander Romance* is a family of texts rather than one stable book. Its oldest surviving Greek form is attributed to Pseudo-Callisthenes, a name created after the traditional attribution to Alexander's contemporary Callisthenes proved impossible. The collection took shape in the Hellenistic and Roman worlds, probably in Alexandria, and reached something like its surviving form around the third century AD. Earlier letters, local tales and court traditions were incorporated into it.

The Romance then travelled through translation and retelling. Greek recensions fed Latin and European traditions. Syriac and Armenian versions carried eastern material and developed new religious settings. Persian poets and storytellers made Eskandar a relative or legitimate successor of Darius, a philosopher-king, a comic adventurer or a prophet according to the work and audience. Arabic and Islamic traditions associated him in varying degrees with Dhu al-Qarnayn. Jewish writers placed him before sages, the gates of paradise and the moral problem of limitless desire. Medieval European artists favoured the ascent by griffins and the descent in a glass vessel because both gave the universal king a visible world to survey.

This transmission was creative. The iron gate against Gog and Magog acquired its great apocalyptic importance in later Syriac material and related traditions; it is not securely part of the earliest Greek historical memory. Persian texts could praise Alexander as a legitimate world ruler while Zoroastrian literature called him the accursed destroyer of temples and learning. An Egyptian strand made him the son of Nectanebo, the last native pharaoh, through Nectanebo's magical deception of Olympias. The story gave the foreign conqueror Egyptian ancestry at the price of a sexual fraud against his mother.

The Eastern Orthodox scriptural tradition supplies another controlling judgement. First Maccabees begins with Alexander's conquests, self-exaltation, death and the division of his kingdom among his servants. Its interest lies in the violent political inheritance produced by conquest. The king's greatness is historically consequential and morally dangerous. No later romance can turn power, marvel or favourable prophecy into evidence of divine approval.

## The ascent into the sky

### Received story

In the best-known medieval form, Alexander sits in a basket or cage borne aloft by great birds, commonly eagles or griffins. He holds meat above them on poles so that they climb towards it. From the height he sees the inhabited earth reduced to a threshing floor surrounded by a coiling sea. Illustrated manuscripts made the ascent one of the most recognisable scenes in the cycle.

The episode gives the world-conqueror the viewpoint his title promises. It also exposes the absurdity of the promise. A king can be lifted above the world without acquiring the right or ability to rule everything he sees. Men and animals bear the danger created by his curiosity.

### Historical kernel

Eryndor makes a genuine aerial ascent during the fortress campaigns of c. 688–687 BR. His army already depends upon engineers, animal handlers and knowledge gathered from conquered peoples. Captured or trained [[Sky-Lions and Crown-Beasts|Sky-Lions]] lift a light, tethered observation frame from a high ridge. The king insists upon taking the observer's place so that he can inspect a fortress, river bend or road hidden beyond the mountain shoulder.

The ascent works. It also disturbs a local Zephyraxis correspondence. An [[Aerials and Deep Envoys|Aerial]] strips shouted instructions from the handlers' mouths, pulls the frame into a cross-current and forces a violent descent beyond the prepared landing ground. One handler dies or is permanently injured; at least one Sky-Lion is killed after becoming tangled in the harness. The king returns with useful knowledge and rewards the surviving craftworkers, but later images remove the handlers, ropes and dead animal. They show him commanding obedient griffins by will alone.

This is biological flight assisted by engineered tackle. It is not an ascent into Heaven, a prophetic vision or a reusable royal power.

## The descent beneath the sea

### Received story

Later Romance traditions place Alexander in a glass or metal vessel lowered into the sea. Manuscript images show a transparent bell, an iron cage or a barrel-like chamber. Some versions give him lamps, animals or companions. A great fish seizes the device and throws it back towards land. Alexander observes that powerful creatures devour the weak in the sea just as powerful men do on land, and recognises the folly of entering a domain he cannot master.

The descent joins curiosity to conquest. Alexander wishes to see what lies below; several tellings sharpen the motive into a desire to subdue the sea. Terror supplies the correction.

### Historical kernel

The descent belongs inside the great harbour siege of c. 697–695 BR. The defenders occupy an island, reef fortress or headland protected by submerged foundations, chains and difficult currents. Engineers drawn from Ubar workshops and Atherian ports construct a weighted observation bell with thick glass panels and an air line. Its immediate purpose is military: locate the chain anchors, measure the foundation and choose where the causeway can be extended.

The king descends because he distrusts conflicting reports and wants personal possession of the decisive knowledge. A sapient Deep Envoy, a large Thalassor-shaped predator or both attack the intrusion. The hauling ship is pulled off station; men on the lines are dragged overboard; one diver or engineer is crushed when the bell strikes the foundation. The vessel is recovered and the observed weakness helps the siege works. The conqueror's victory therefore remains connected to the experiment, as does the death of the people who made it possible.

Later harbour communities disagree about the attacker. One tradition remembers a queen below the reef who refused his demand for submission. Another remembers only a vast fish. A third says the king saw drowned soldiers seated around a table and never again ate before his men after a battle. The first two may preserve separate actors. The third is a moral tale unless independent evidence is later established.

The incident reveals one local underwater ecology. It establishes no universal sea kingdom and no conquest of Thalassor's domain.

## The Trees of the Sun and Moon

### Received story

Alexander reaches a sacred grove whose two trees speak at appointed hours. The Tree of the Sun and the Tree of the Moon answer his questions concerning victory, death and return. They tell him that he will die at Babylon and will not see his mother or homeland again. In one Greek-derived telling, the explanation connects his fate with the mothers, cities, countries and friends deprived by his conquests. The king threatens interpreters who hesitate to translate the answer.

The prophecy gives the homeward half of the Romance its momentum. The audience knows the conqueror will die. The remaining interest lies in how he receives the knowledge and whether knowledge changes him.

### Historical kernel

The army encounters a connected grove in the foothills east of the Ubar basin around c. 687–686 BR. Its [[Root-Minds, Water-Wives, and Stone-Voices|Root-Minds]] possess territorial memory under Sylvorn pressure. Sunlight and moonlight pass through different living canopies, activating separate parts of the fungal and root network. The grove can answer through leaves, cracking trunks, several human mouths or a voice carried from the soil. Its knowledge concerns land, graves, travellers and the return routes passing through its domain.

The king removes iron and enters with a small party because weapons silence the older roots. He asks whether the eastern road will make him master of the whole inhabited world. The grove answers with the place and season of his death, though not necessarily its medical cause. It also says he will never see his upland home again. An interpreter first softens the answer. The king discovers the alteration and orders his execution, or stops the execution only after a companion intervenes. Either choice preserves the source tradition's coercion: the ruler who demands truth punishes the person required to speak it.

The prediction need not make the grove omniscient. It knows that the king's return route, existing wounds, local disease and the season of the Ubar capital form a fatal pattern. Later cultures identify the speakers as solar and lunar trees because two phases of the same network answered at different hours. Qahuri tellers make them servants of Father Sun and Mother Moon. Jirahar versions call them witnesses who refused a king's bribe. Atherian royal art turns them into conquered oracles bowing to him.

## The Water of Life and the Dark Road

### Received story

The search for immortal water became one of the most fertile eastern parts of the cycle. A dried or salted fish revives when washed in a hidden spring. Alexander's cook, companion or guide discovers the water, while Alexander misses it. In Persian traditions Khidr enters the Land of Darkness, drinks and receives unending life; Alexander searches in vain. The failure survives even in versions that otherwise make him a wise or prophetic king.

The tale turns world conquest inward. The king can inherit capitals and treasure, yet the least prestigious member of his party recognises the thing he most desires. Immortality goes to the guide, cook or companion rather than the ruler.

### Historical kernel

The Dark Road can be a short, disastrous expedition during c. 686–685 BR, after the speaking grove and before the army's final refusal. Guides lead a selected party through a lightless ravine, buried road or Egg White-adjacent fault where distance and time do not behave consistently. The route promises access behind an enemy position and rumours of a spring that restores exhausted animals.

A camp cook or local guide washes a dried fish in a seep. The flesh moves and the fish escapes into the stone-dark water. A mortally wounded porter recovers enough strength to stand. The king orders the spring collected and enters the fault himself. Every sealed vessel returns filled with ordinary bitter water. One companion disappears beyond the local correspondence after drinking directly; whether that person survives bodily in a small Egg White refuge remains open.

The water can restore damaged embodied life within its place and conditions. It cannot erase the human soul, cancel the Boatman's road or place a person beyond the Lord's judgement. A bodily crossing may delay an ordinary death while preserving a complete human soul. Such a survivor would resemble a self-bound human Patron in location and dependence without ceasing to be human. No later cult can reproduce the crossing by drinking water sold under the conqueror's name.

The king's failure supplies the fixed historical centre. Later traditions may disagree over who found the spring, who drank and whether the missing companion still lives.

## The gate and the enclosed threat

### Received story

Alexander's barrier against Gog and Magog became a major Syriac, Persian, Arabic, Jewish and Christian image. The conquering king reaches a northern or eastern pass and seals destructive peoples behind a gate of metal and stone. Apocalyptic traditions connect their eventual release with the end of an age. The famous form is a late development within the Alexander complex and should not be projected wholesale into the historical king's campaign.

The story nevertheless preserves a strong political contradiction. A ruler famous for opening countries and displacing peoples receives honour for closing one road and containing another people. Later empires could identify their feared neighbours with the enclosed nations and present frontier violence as the defence of the world.

### Historical kernel

Eryndor closes one strategic pass around c. 686 BR. His engineers repair an older Giant barrier while retreating from a fortress district. The enemy may be a surviving Giant war-brood, a recurrence system producing malformed soldiers, or an army gathered around a violent Idol Mask. Its identity remains unresolved until the farther eastern geography is developed.

The gate stops a particular pursuit. Its construction requires the destruction of villages in the approach, forced labour from prisoners and the abandonment of people who remain on the far side. Soldiers kill those who attempt to force entry before the final closure. The decision saves the returning army and exposed settlements west of the pass. It also creates descendants who remember the king as the man who shut their ancestors among the monsters.

Later rulers enlarge the story. Each claims that its own northern enemy descends from the enclosed host. Preachers make the gate an apocalyptic seal. Court romances fill the far side with every creature excluded from ordinary maps. The historical barrier does not imprison all demons, explain a protected Weird or promise a final release.

## The queen who recognises the king

Persian and Syriac-derived romances give Alexander an important encounter with Candace, Qeydafa or Nushaba. He enters a queen's court in disguise; she recognises him from a portrait and prevents his deception from becoming conquest. Persian versions develop her as a formidable ruler whose negotiation forces Alexander to accept another kind of sovereignty.

This episode can become a real encounter around c. 688 BR. A settled eastern queen controls food, passes and mounted guides needed for the fortress war. Alexander enters under another name to inspect her strength or recover captured officers. Her portrait archive identifies him. She keeps the discovery private, brings him before the families harmed by his troops and bargains for hostages, restitution and recognised boundaries. She neither seduces him nor becomes a reward for his campaign.

The settlement explains why her country survives the conquest and why later Atherian biographies omit the humiliation. Her own tradition can exaggerate the meeting into complete submission by the world king. A Persian-shaped successor tradition can make her his moral equal. This incident also gives the later Samyra romances an older queenly model without making the two women relatives.

## The naked philosophers and the rebuke of conquest

Greek historical and Romance traditions place Alexander before gymnosophists or Brahman sages. Their answers expose the brevity of life, the poverty of royal desire and the violence concealed by claims to glory. Some episodes end in admiration; others retain threats against people whose wisdom displeases him.

The World Egg equivalent is a real community of human ascetics or philosophical households with its own language, property arrangements and political disputes. They live near the eastern campaign rather than outside history. Eryndor questions several leaders after they support resistance or refuse tribute. One asks how much earth a dead king needs and marks the answer with the length of a grave. Another identifies the king's generosity as the redistribution of wealth taken from cities he destroyed.

The king spares some speakers, imprisons another and executes the organiser whose community supplied the enemy. Later royal biographies remember only the witty exchange and clemency. The community's descendants remember the names of those killed. The episode keeps admiration, coercion and intellectual seriousness together.

## Creatures at the edge of the campaign

The Romance contains peoples with monstrous bodies, strange animals, giant crabs, serpents and lands where ordinary categories fail. World Egg history can support a portion of this material through existing ecology:

- Sky-Lions account for the ascent without creating a new race of obedient griffins.
- Deep Envoys and Thalassor-shaped predators account for incompatible undersea witnesses.
- Root-Minds account for the grove's attention and speech.
- surviving High Giants, giant-blooded human dynasts and engineered war-beasts can supply several monster-fighting episodes;
- court persons may be remembered as animal-headed peoples where a brief encounter was misunderstood;
- copied soldiers, malformed recurrence bodies and Idol court-beasts may account for creatures near the sealed pass.

Human communities with unfamiliar appearance or custom must never be converted into bestiary entries. The one-eyed, headless and dog-headed nations of later maps remain folklore unless a separate creaturely identity is established.

## A historical sequence that fits the short reign

| Date | Existing campaign | Recommended Romance kernel |
|---:|---|---|
| 706 BR | accession after the father's assassination | no Romance addition |
| 705–703 BR | revolt and destruction of an Atherian city | later romances conceal the king's first great atrocity |
| 702–698 BR | sea crossing and western Arqeshite victories | marvel stories begin among multilingual troops and ports |
| 697–695 BR | prolonged harbour siege | real undersea observation bell and lethal attack |
| 695 BR | Qahur and the royal oracle | bounded sign; later starry-paternity tradition |
| 694–690 BR | alliance with Arezan and fall of Arqesh | philosophers, translators and royal archives enter the court |
| 689–688 BR | pursuit of Kassur III | queen recognises the disguised Eryndor and compels a settlement |
| 688–687 BR | eastern fortress campaigns | real Sky-Lion ascent during reconnaissance |
| 687–686 BR | exposed roads beyond the basin | speaking grove and local prediction of death |
| 686 BR | withdrawal through a threatened pass | one engineered gate closes a particular surviving threat |
| 686–685 BR | attempted advance and growing refusal | Dark Road and failed search for life-giving water |
| 685 BR | army refuses further march | no supernatural power overrides the soldiers' decision |
| 684 BR | death in an Ubar river capital | prophecy remembered; succession destroys the household |

The episodes occupy approximately twelve years of campaigning because they arise from problems the army already faces. The bell serves a siege. The ascent serves reconnaissance. The queen controls a supply route. The grove stands beside an eastern road. The gate covers a retreat. The Dark Road is one final attempt to move farther. None requires a hidden extra reign.

## Five limits and one human refusal

The strongest received cycle groups the incidents by what the king cannot own:

| Incident | What he seeks | What he receives | Who pays |
|---|---|---|---|
| descent | command of the harbour and knowledge beneath water | one military observation and bodily terror | divers, haulers, engineers and defenders |
| ascent | the view of a world ruler | one useful glimpse and an uncontrolled fall | handlers, Sky-Lions and attendants |
| grove | certain knowledge of victory and return | knowledge of death which he cannot punish away | interpreter and companions forced to hear it |
| water | preservation beyond mortality | proof that restoration exists, followed by personal failure | porter, cook or guide, and the missing drinker |
| gate | safety through absolute enclosure | temporary defence and a divided frontier memory | prisoners, villagers and those abandoned beyond it |
| army refusal | obedience without limit | an open refusal by exhausted human beings | veterans, dependants and eastern recruits in the retreat |

This pattern makes the wonders serve the historical biography. His curiosity, courage and appetite are real. So are his generosity, fascination with other peoples and willingness to share danger. The same king treats people as instruments when they block his desired horizon. The marvels do not absolve him; they reveal him.

## Cultural romance families in the World Egg

The existence of the following traditions can become canonical even when their literal details remain disputed:

### Upland royal cycle

The conqueror rises on two crown-beasts, receives the horizon as his inheritance and descends because his dead father calls him home. This courtly version omits the handlers and the human city he destroyed after accession.

### Qahuri river cycle

Father Sun permits the king one view above the world and Mother Moon shows him the water below it. The river priests interpret his failure to drink as proof that even a ruler who bears a Qahuri title must enter death. Rival recensions make him either the secret son of a Qahuri magician-king or the foreign dupe of one.

### Arqeshite lament

The trees name the cities he burned and the families deported. The king kills the interpreter, then hears the same sentence from every tree along the return road. This version makes the conqueror's death a consequence of imperial violence without requiring the grove to administer divine justice.

### Vashari book of roads

The queen who recognised him and the ascetics who rebuked him become the true guardians of the east. Arezan receives less prominence than in official Vasharan history because popular storytellers prefer the contest between a proud king and people who cannot be impressed.

### Jirahar traveller cycle

The discoverer of the Water is a cook, drover or anonymous guide. The king cannot find the spring because he abandons the slowest travellers. Several versions attach the story to a route-house custom of feeding the last arrival before the master of the house.

### Later Atherian imperial cycle

Imperial romances turn the sealed pass and harbour works into precedents for Atherian engineering. The conqueror becomes an early civiliser of the east. Dissident versions restore the forced labour, destroyed villages and drowned workers.

### Redeemer-era and church reception

Later Christian readers may treat the ruler as an example of pagan magnificence brought to the grave. A popular Life may falsely convert him, place a saint beside the Water or turn the gate into a Christian apocalyptic monument. Such received stories remain theologically bounded: a pre-Redeemer conqueror does not become a saint through retrospective admiration.

## Identity and metaphysical controls

- Eryndor is a complete `human-soul` person. He has no Menhir, Giant or Idol parentage.
- Sky-Lions are biological creatures. The Aerial is a separate court-law intelligence. Neither becomes an angelic mount.
- A Deep Envoy, predator, sea-place intelligence and Thalassor remain distinct possible actors.
- The speaking grove consists of one or more Root-Minds under Sylvorn pressure. Sylvorn does not personally pronounce divine judgement.
- The spring restores or preserves embodied life under local conditions. It cannot destroy the Image, cancel judgement or manufacture sainthood.
- A bodily Egg White survivor retains a complete human soul. Patron describes a later anchor relationship, not a new species.
- The gate contains one historical danger. The imprisoned actors must receive separate classifications before canon promotion.
- The king's corpse, human soul, royal office, legendary persona, copied image and the Crowned Countenance's attention remain separate.
- A fulfilled prediction proves the grove's bounded knowledge. It does not authenticate every later word attributed to it.

## Source-fidelity ruling

The adapted cycle retains the Romance's principal hard edges. Alexander's ascent and descent are reckless uses of servants, animals and technical specialists. The speaking-tree episode retains the ruler's threat against interpreters. The Water of Life goes to a socially lesser figure or guide while the conqueror fails. The gate protects some people by abandoning and killing others. Philosophical rebuke does not erase executions. The later legend grows around conquest, enslavement, destroyed cities and the early death of a man who caused many other deaths.

The pass should not combine every source's cruelties into every episode. Each incident carries the cost belonging to its borrowed element and its military setting.

## Canon options

### Recommended historical core

Promote the undersea bell, Sky-Lion ascent, speaking grove, failed life-water expedition, local gate, eastern queen and ascetic rebuke as separate incidents in the c. 697–685 BR campaign. Preserve the precise identities of the sea attacker, missing drinker and enclosed threat until their regional homes are developed.

### Recommended legendary core

Make the existence of at least six culturally distinct Romance families canonical. Their agreement establishes that unusual events surrounded the campaign. Their incompatible details prevent any one literary version from functioning as a complete chronicle.

### Material to retain only as romance

- bodily ascent into Heaven;
- conquest of a universal undersea empire;
- prophetic or saintly status;
- literal dominion over all animals or elemental states;
- imprisonment of every monster or an apocalyptic nation;
- a drink that automatically abolishes death and judgement;
- monstrous human races created from descriptions of foreign peoples;
- complete secret Qahuri or Persian biological parentage.
