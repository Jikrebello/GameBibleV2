# Palaces, Sea Migrations and the Long Fall — Mycenaean Research and World Egg Comparison

The Mycenaean world offers a history of households gaining wealth, royal centres concentrating it, and communities surviving the destruction of those centres. Its usefulness for Atheria extends across all three periods. Warrior burials suggest competition among emerging elites; palace remains reveal organised production and unequal dependence; post-palatial settlements show people rebuilding under altered conditions. Later Greek poetry supplies a further history of remembrance, in which distant wars and ruined houses acquired meanings suited to subsequent audiences.

This study separates that evidence from the proposed World Egg arrangement. The historical comparison does not move the Glass descendants into the Prophet’s liberation campaign. Their southern settlement begins generations after Arsuvan’s collapse, among people who have already buried their dead, raised children and established new ways of living.

## The emerging warrior households

The comparison uses three broad archaeological periods. Their boundaries vary between regions, especially where Cretan and mainland sequences intersect.

| Historical period | Approximate orientation | Principal evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Emerging warrior households | Seventeenth–fifteenth centuries BC | Rich burials, weapons, prestige objects and changing interregional connections |
| Mature mainland palace societies | Especially fourteenth–thirteenth centuries BC | Monumental centres, organised production, infrastructure and Linear B administrations |
| Post-palatial societies | Twelfth–eleventh centuries BC, with subsequent Early Iron Age developments | Continued or relocated settlement, changed burials, surviving crafts and new political arrangements |

The Greek heroic works discussed below were composed or transmitted much later. Their remembered Bronze Age settings do not make them contemporary accounts. The [Cambridge overview of economy and administration](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-companion-to-the-aegean-bronze-age/economy-and-administration/78B9F1C2DDA5BB5CC83D06F150EA468B) also stresses that palace-centred states were never universal across Mycenaean Greece.

The earlier Mycenaean elites grew within an Aegean already connected by shipping, skilled work and competing centres of wealth. The rich shaft graves at Mycenae, particularly those of the sixteenth century BC, belong to this emerging world. Weapons, elaborate ornaments and imported or foreign-influenced objects made particular households conspicuous. Such deposits reveal concentrated resources and claims to distinction. They cannot by themselves identify the deceased as the rulers, adventurers or named ancestors of later epic.

Connections with Crete were important to mainland elites. Techniques, images and forms of display travelled with objects and craftspeople; mainland households adapted them to their own circumstances. The evidence supports both local development and sustained exchange. The palatial system of later centuries should therefore be treated as a historical achievement requiring explanation, rather than the natural starting condition of every Greek-speaking community. [Metropolitan Museum, “Mycenaean Civilization”](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/mycenaean-civilization).

For worldbuilding, the emerging-household period provides relationships often lost when ancient history begins with an established empire. A family can become important because it commands a landing place, marries into a neighbouring household, equips followers or attracts a craft specialist. Its descendants may subsequently describe these practical advantages as ancestral superiority. Other families remember the same rise through lost grazing, a marriage bargain or service in an ambitious neighbour’s household.

That pattern can inform Giant antiquity without requiring a newly invented universal Giant monarchy. Regional houses may consolidate authority around estates, workshops and mountain approaches before acquiring elaborate royal titles. Their human dependants also have histories: some households become indispensable boat-builders or interpreters, while others are displaced or enslaved by the expanding court. A palace’s later grandeur rests upon these earlier relationships.

## The mature palace societies

By the later Bronze Age, major mainland centres included Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos and Thebes. Their importance depended upon surrounding settlement, cultivation and movement. Fortifications protected selected centres; roads, bridges, storage and water arrangements connected royal ambition with practical work. Knossos and other Cretan evidence belong within this wider account, although the chronology of political change on Crete has its own problems. Mainland and island histories should not be reduced to the succession of one capital.

Linear B records are especially valuable because they come from the administrations themselves. The script was deciphered as Greek in the twentieth century. The surviving tablets chiefly record particular transactions, allocations, personnel and obligations; they were not composed as comprehensive descriptions of society. Many survived because destructive fires hardened their clay. An archive can illuminate a palace’s final working period while leaving most neighbouring households almost invisible. The Cambridge Mycenaean Epigraphy Group and its CaLiBRA catalogue provide the appropriate starting points for identifying tablets and checking readings. [Cambridge project](https://www.classics.cam.ac.uk/research/projects/mycep), [CaLiBRA catalogue](https://calibra.classics.cam.ac.uk/Tablet/Search).

The palace’s reach varied by activity and region. It could organise specialist production, receive agricultural goods and allocate materials or rations without owning every field or directing every exchange. That distinction matters when imagining collapse: the disappearance of a royal administration need not eliminate every market, craft tradition or household holding. Equally, a family’s ability to cultivate independently would provide little protection if warfare destroyed its seed, draught animals or access to land.

The vocabulary of the tablets distinguishes offices, ranks and forms of dependency. The *wanax*, conventionally understood as the king, belongs to a hierarchy whose precise distribution of authority must be reconstructed from incomplete evidence. It would be misleading to translate the whole arrangement into a later European feudal system. Production, religious offerings, military resources and household authority overlapped in ways particular to these societies.

Feasting helps connect these activities. Food supplied to a royal gathering sustained people while displaying the ruler’s ability to assemble and provide for them. Palace demand also supported specialist goods such as cloth, perfumed oil, weapons and chariot equipment. Local communities retained interests in land and production which could conflict with royal demands. This combination of concentrated display and negotiated access to resources is examined in the accessible text of [“Mycenaean Glocalism: Greek Political Economies and International Trade”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/trade-and-civilisation/mycenaean-glocalism-greek-political-economies-and-international-trade/707867BD61CAD048D99A3CEAB3E5D19C).

Labour included women and children as well as adult men. The National Archaeological Museum’s tablet PY Aa 85 records women working in textile production together with girls and boys; the museum relates it to wider evidence for children within the palace workforce. Such records make dependent families visible, although individual legal conditions cannot always be recovered. Enslaved and otherwise dependent workers should appear in an account of royal prosperity, with careful attention to what the evidence can actually distinguish. [National Archaeological Museum, PY Aa 85](https://www.namuseum.gr/en/monthly_artefact/work-first-then-play-2/).

Religion likewise belonged to everyday allocation and authority. Offerings, sanctuaries and religious personnel appear in administrative evidence, while later familiar divine names sometimes have earlier attestations. Continuity of a name does not establish an unchanged theology across the intervening centuries. A Bronze Age offering and a Classical festival can belong to related traditions whose social setting has substantially altered.

For Atheria, this encourages a materially grounded account of Giant sacred kingship. Royal astronomy can organise agricultural demands, festivals and claims to inherited power. A workshop may produce ceremonial objects and military equipment for the same household. A human family may inherit expertise in both activities. After liberation, its descendants can preserve the craft while disputing the ruler’s sacred interpretation of it.

## Shipping and the wider Mediterranean

Mycenaean vessels, cargoes and objects belonged to networks reaching beyond the mainland. Pottery travelled widely; its distribution records several kinds of movement and does not establish a colony wherever a vessel was found. Containers could carry wine, oil or other products, and foreign designs could be adopted locally. Merchants, crews, envoys and craftspeople connected societies whose political institutions remained distinct.

Crete, Cyprus, the Levant and Egypt each require their own historical account. Their participation in exchange supplied materials, knowledge and prestige, while wars or succession crises could alter the conditions of access. A route depended upon people willing to receive a ship, provide water, exchange goods and allow its departure. Control of a coastal stronghold could consequently affect households far inland.

For the World Egg, such relationships are particularly useful in linking palace agriculture with maritime exchange. Grain from a river basin, ore from an upland district, timber from a forest margin and specialist work from a hill settlement can meet at a port without forming one culturally uniform population. The later loss of cooperation then has recognisable consequences: a smith lacks fuel or metal, a garrison requisitions food, and a returning vessel finds that its previous host has been overthrown.

These are different experiences of the same political deterioration. A complete account should follow both the commanders competing for the harbour and the households deciding whether to remain beside it.

## Destruction and the limits of a single explanation

The end of the palace world involved several destruction sequences, episodes of rebuilding and eventual abandonment. Sigrid Deger-Jalkotzy’s overview distinguishes earlier Late Helladic IIIB destructions from the later destruction of palatial centres around the end of that phase. Proposed earthquakes, warfare and wider instability must be assessed against individual sites. The accessible chapter summary explicitly records continuing disagreement over cause. [“Decline, Destruction, Aftermath”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-companion-to-the-aegean-bronze-age/decline-destruction-aftermath/6F5022A91B80E31A3B6CB086909FE1BD).

A burned building establishes a fire. Its deposits may help establish whether it was occupied, abandoned, looted or rebuilt. Identifying an attacker, a rebellion or a political motive requires additional evidence. Similar destruction dates across a wide region do not automatically demonstrate the passage of one army, while differences of a generation can become obscured by broad archaeological date ranges.

Recent scholarship continues to examine social change across the twelfth to early tenth centuries, including settlement, burial and ritual. This perspective gives the aftermath its own historical substance. The later population is more than an interval between the last palace and the eventual rise of the city-state. [Antonis Kotsonas, *Social Change across the End of the Aegean Bronze Age*, publisher’s overview](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/abs/social-change-across-the-end-of-the-aegean-bronze-age/569D1C020E1E820EFE533A4DCF0D9146).

Economic explanation also requires attention to direction of causation. Reduced imports might reflect disrupted supply, diminished demand, population change or several of these together. Evidence for shrinking exchange cannot simply be made the cause of every other change. [*The Collapse of the Mycenaean Economy*, chapter on demographic and domestic economic change, accessible summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/collapse-of-the-mycenaean-economy/demographic-and-domestic-economic-change-in-early-greece-factors-of-supply-and-demand/9AA296081882FB44BCBC905BA0F51DD3).

The strongest World Egg application is therefore a sequence of connected failures with different local outcomes. The Unbinding already supplies a controlling historical crisis. Research should help explain how that crisis acts upon grain deliveries, royal marriages, garrisons and labour, rather than replace it with another universal cause.

## Sea movements and Egyptian victory accounts

“Sea Peoples” is a modern collective designation applied to groups named in eastern Mediterranean evidence. Egyptian royal accounts, particularly the programme at Medinet Habu associated with Ramesses III, are important evidence for conflict and the king’s presentation of victory. The inscriptions and reliefs require reading within their monumental purpose: they proclaim royal mastery, protection and defeat of enemies. They do not provide a neutral census of every moving population.

The Epigraphic Survey’s publications preserve the record used for studying this programme. This dossier uses the publication information and accessible indexed textual extracts; it does not claim a fresh inspection of every relief or hieroglyphic text. [Medinet Habu, volume I](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/oip8.pdf), [historical records and translations](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/saoc12.pdf).

Movement by sea could involve families seeking settlement, hired fighters, raiders, traders and groups whose role changed over time. A displaced household might initially travel under military protection and later cultivate captured land. A commander might alternate service, piracy and settlement. Migration studies consequently test combinations of archaeological evidence rather than treating foreign-looking pottery as a complete ethnic identity. [Assaf Yasur-Landau, *The Philistines and Aegean Migration at the End of the Late Bronze Age*, introductory summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/philistines-and-aegean-migration-at-the-end-of-the-late-bronze-age/introduction/519528E70158C44D0F2B68ECB62863D6).

In the proposed Giant aftermath, sea movements begin among existing populations during and after the Unbinding. Their members need not be Glass descendants. Some carry former palace soldiers; others transport escaped workers, household tools, seed or stolen cargo. Later Atherian fleets enter a maritime world already reshaped by these movements. An imperial chronicle may eventually credit its own ancestors with every famous arrival, while a shore community remembers the names of earlier strangers.

## What survives the palace

The loss of central government can destroy specialised work dependent upon concentrated demand. A workshop producing elaborate royal equipment may cease to function when its patrons disappear, while a potter or carpenter continues serving nearby households. Families may salvage stone and metal from a ruined centre, convert rooms to new uses or abandon an unhealthy and exposed site.

Recovery also alters authority. A person able to organise food, defence or a voyage may acquire influence previously held by royal officials. Former dependants may preserve knowledge while rejecting the house that employed them. Elsewhere, an armed heir can survive by making a smaller population support the pretensions of a much larger vanished kingdom.

Those possibilities give the Orphaned interval its proper length. Between c. 2260 and 2165 BR, children grow up who have never seen Arsuvan united. They learn skills from relatives, build different settlements and inherit arguments over abandoned land. Their descendants encounter the southern Glass communities during another generation. The newcomers possess their own shared law, memory and practical knowledge; they also depend upon local pilots, cultivators and negotiators.

The survival of skills should be described at an appropriate scale. One district may maintain boats and drainage while losing monumental construction. Another preserves fine metalwork but has little safe maritime access. Uneven recovery produces reasons to exchange, marry, raid and migrate. It also gives heroic stories something tangible to protect.

## Heroic remembrance: five useful traditions

### Perseus: protection, violent return and unintended inheritance

In the *Library* attributed to Apollodorus, Danaë and Perseus are cast away and received on Seriphos. Polydectes’ pursuit of Danaë provides the household danger behind the impossible demand placed upon her son. Perseus’ victories ultimately enable a violent return against the ruler. The later accidental killing of Acrisius complicates the successful hero’s inheritance: the threatened prophecy is fulfilled through an event that differs from a deliberate act of vengeance. [*Library* 2.4](https://www.theoi.com/Text/Apollodorus2.html).

The adaptable structure combines dependence upon a host, coercion directed at a family member, dangerous travel and an outcome that success cannot make wholly fortunate. Its World Egg descendant could belong to an Orphaned coastal community or a mixed settlement facing a surviving dynast. The Gorgon, divine parentage and complete itinerary need not be transferred. A replacement must retain the concrete family danger and the irreversible consequence of the return.

### Heracles: service after a household catastrophe

Heracles’ traditions vary in both the timing and victims of his madness. Apollodorus places the killing of his children and children of Iphicles before service to Eurystheus; Megara survives that account. Euripides’ *Heracles* places the destruction of his household after the labours and includes Megara among the dead. A composite retelling should announce its choices rather than claim a single ancient sequence.

The existing World Egg Sleeper already carries the central wound of a champion killing loved members of his household under supernatural distortion and subsequently undertaking exploitative labours. That history should remain the principal destination of this grammar. New palace-aftermath material can examine service, exile or the reception of his legend without manufacturing another identical guilty strongman. Other Heraclean episodes, such as the murder of Iphitus and subsequent servitude, also require their own source choice before use. [Apollodorus, book 2](https://www.theoi.com/Text/Apollodorus2.html); the reception of the household tragedy is also visible in the surviving [*Megara* poem](https://www.theoi.com/Text/Megara.html).

### Thebes: a house’s conflict becomes a city’s disaster

The Theban traditions connect exposure, mistaken identity, kinship, contested inheritance and war between brothers. Different works rearrange the emphasis. Their significance lies partly in the movement between household and city: the struggles of a ruling family draw other households into siege and mourning. Antigone’s burial of her brother makes the consequences of victory intimate and physically dangerous.

For the Giant aftermath, a detached royal house might split while dependent human settlements choose sides, flee or attempt neutrality. The protagonists need not repeat Oedipus’ entire life. If the chosen element is a fratricidal succession war, its deaths and the refusal of burial must remain consequential. The fall of that one house must also leave people beyond its genealogy with decisions of their own. The *Library*’s [Theban account](https://www.theoi.com/Text/Apollodorus3.html) supplies a mythographic sequence, not an excavated Bronze Age biography.

### Agamemnon: the victory brought home

Aeschylus’ *Agamemnon*, performed in 458 BC, presents the victorious commander’s return within a history of family violence. Iphigenia’s sacrifice, Cassandra’s enslavement and death, Clytemnestra’s actions and the older crimes of the house give the homecoming its substance. Military success has enlarged the household’s suffering and brought a captive woman into its final catastrophe. [Aeschylus, *Agamemnon*](https://classics.mit.edu/Aeschylus/agamemnon.html).

A World Egg homecoming could follow a campaign against a surviving Giant stronghold. Its human victor may have sacrificed companions, betrayed allies or brought captives home. The subsequent conflict would arise from those decisions. The audience need not discover that the Giant ruler was secretly innocent for the human victor to bear guilt.

### Odysseus: restored possession and the cost of restoration

The *Odyssey* makes recognition, endurance and return compelling through relationships with household members, hosts and companions. Book 22 also contains the slaughter of the suitors, the hanging of enslaved women and the mutilation of Melanthius. The poem’s restoration of household authority includes lethal judgements against people whose positions and freedom were unequal. These actions cannot disappear if that particular restoration episode is borrowed. [*Odyssey* 22](https://classics.mit.edu/Homer/odyssey.22.xxii.html).

A translated homecoming should preserve the experiences of those who lived through the absence. The returning captain’s claim is one part of the history; the people who kept children alive, accommodated occupying forces or suffered coercion supply others. This creates a different story from a straightforward voyage of discovery and avoids assigning every maritime legend to the existing wandering duo.

## The Trojan war and the homecomings as a connected heroic cycle

The Trojan traditions offer a sustained history of a coalition at war, a city approaching destruction and households waiting for people who may never return. Gods enter these stories as participants with preferences, attachments and quarrels. They send disease, strengthen a fighter, conceal someone in danger, deceive an opponent and alter the sea through which the victors must travel.

This material deserves a principal place in the Atherian comparison. The earlier account gave individual return stories more attention than the war joining them. The revised proposal develops one regional siege and several connected homecomings, with Menhir manifestations, court persons and Idol Masks acting throughout. The palace aftermath provides its setting; it remains separate from the much later Punic-inspired capital.

### What belongs to which source

The *Iliad* narrates a short period late in the war and ends with Hector’s funeral. The wooden horse, Troy’s final sack and most Greek returns belong to other parts of the epic tradition, later summaries and tragedies. The *Odyssey* develops one extended homecoming while recalling the fates of other commanders. Treating the entire cycle as one contemporary war chronicle would obscure both its literary development and the alternatives preserved by its tellers.

The *Library* epitome preserves a particularly useful sequence beyond the *Iliad*: the struggle over Achilles’ arms, Ajax’s death, the horse, the sack, violations of sanctuary and the departure of the victors. It also records versions in which captive women burn ships to prevent their transportation into slavery. Those women alter where settlement occurs; they have more to contribute than lamentation beside a victorious captain. [Apollodorus, *Epitome* 5–6](https://www.theoi.com/Text/ApollodorusE.html).

A World Egg cycle can similarly contain an established military event, conflicting local histories and a developed body of poetry. Its prose account should say which supernatural acts are proposed as actual events and which belong to later telling. Calling the whole cycle legendary must not become a way of removing all supernatural action from the history.

### The quarrel in the camp

The *Iliad* opens with Apollo’s plague after Agamemnon refuses to release Chryseis and insults her father, the priest Chryses. Agamemnon explicitly intends sexual and domestic possession of the captive. When he finally yields, he demands another captive woman as compensation. The ensuing quarrel concerns prestige and command conducted through the ownership of women whose own wishes carry little authority.

Athena’s intervention in Achilles’ anger is physically intimate: she restrains him as he reaches towards violence. Thetis subsequently seeks support for her son, extending the quarrel into relations among the gods. The gods’ involvement changes what people do, while the human dispute retains its own responsibilities. [*Iliad* 1](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerIliad1.html).

The applicable Atherian pattern is a coalition damaged by the conduct of its own leaders, with a real supernatural response affecting its ability to remain in the field. The captain protesting another man’s conduct need not be innocent himself. A captive woman’s history should continue beyond her transfer between powerful men.

### The powers take sides

In *Iliad* 20, the gods enter the fighting in opposed groups. Their alignments involve relationships and earlier commitments. In book 5, Athena helps Diomedes distinguish divine participants; Aphrodite and Ares are wounded, and Apollo protects Aeneas. The spectacle includes human courage and vulnerability in direct contact with powers greater than the combatants. [*Iliad* 20](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerIliad20.html), [book 5](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerIliad5.html).

The World Egg translation should give the powers observable acts. A mailed presence can turn an impact against a siege engine. A tide can enter a channel against the expected current. A Mask wearing the likeness of a trusted person can lead someone into a fatal encounter. Such events are materially different from a priest merely describing a favourable omen.

The underlying identities must nevertheless remain World Egg identities. A Menhir’s local manifestation can be driven out; its entire sovereignty does not bleed to death beneath a spear. An Idol can speak and fight through a Mask without placing its Throne on the battlefield. A court person may support particular humans out of attachment, rivalry or ambition and possess a moral understanding different from the associated Menhir.

### A landscape joins the battle

Achilles’ violence in *Iliad* 21 provokes the river Scamander, whose channel is obstructed by bodies. The river attacks him, and the conflict brings in other divine assistance. The setting becomes an active participant: battle changes the river, and the river changes the battle. [*Iliad* 21](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerIliad21.html).

For Atheria, the strongest equivalent is a harbour or tidal channel whose existing supernatural relationships are disturbed by siege works, bodies and wreckage. A contest between water, wind and resistance can occur within the same bay. Sailors and soldiers then have practical choices: cut an obstructing boom, abandon a machine, retrieve a body or escape while a particular path remains open.

This keeps the scale heroic without making the conflict universal. The war belongs to one coast; several sovereignties have reasons to contest what happens there. Their interaction leaves a broken quay, altered landing or customary refusal whose meaning comes from the event.

### The body returned and the city destroyed

Priam’s visit to Achilles in *Iliad* 24 depends upon Hermes’ guidance and protection. Their meeting brings the dead Hector’s household and his killer into the same tent. Shared grief permits a burial without reversing the killing or ending the war. The supernatural assistance makes an intimate encounter possible. [*Iliad* 24](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerIliad24.html).

The later sack traditions are considerably harsher than a victory procession. They include the rape of Cassandra in sanctuary, Astyanax’s killing, Polyxena’s sacrifice, enslavement and the destruction of the city. Apollodorus also records anger against Ajax for his violation of sanctuary and the victors’ difficulty deciding what to do about him. The sources preserve a victory followed by obligations and dangers that the victors cannot settle simply by sailing away. [*Epitome* 5.22–6.6](https://www.theoi.com/Text/ApollodorusE.html).

An adaptation need not repeat every death or insert a wooden horse. If it borrows the violated sanctuary and endangered return, it must retain the assault itself and the decisions that protect its perpetrator. The family seeking burial, the captive trying to escape and the commander calculating his departure each supply a different part of the aftermath.

### The sea becomes an extended theatre of action

The *Odyssey* connects extraordinary encounters through the traveller’s effort to get home and the losses that alter that hope. In book 9, Polyphemus kills and consumes companions. Odysseus’ escape demonstrates ingenuity, while his declaration of his identity allows the wounded Cyclops to call upon Poseidon against him. The boast affects people still dependent upon his judgement. [*Odyssey* 9](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerOdyssey9.html).

Book 10 places Aeolus’ gift and the companions’ disastrous suspicion beside Circe’s transformation of men into animals. The episode preserves their awareness beneath altered bodies. Book 5 presents Calypso’s detention of Odysseus and his preference for home over the future she offers. These stories concern authority, mistrust, appetite, enforced intimacy and a life that can be made comfortable while remaining captive. [*Odyssey* 10](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerOdyssey10.html), [book 5](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HomerOdyssey5.html).

Their World Egg counterparts can involve sea courts, altered coasts and Typhonius-related bodily change. Humans transformed by a host remain human persons. An offer of an enduring body cannot become an offer of godhood or a different soul. A seeming conversation with a dead companion requires a separate explanation; recollections carried by matter, living impostors and copied voices are not that companion’s routed soul.

The homecoming itself must retain the severity of the element selected. The earlier discussion of *Odyssey* 22 remains controlling for a restored household built through slaughter and executions. Other returners can establish new households, accept that an old relationship has changed or fail to reach home. A shared war thus produces several histories instead of a single captain collecting every famous adventure.

### Direction for the World Egg draft

The comparison now develops **The Siege and the Returnings**, a provisional cycle placed within c. 1980–1930 BR. Its war concerns a surviving Giant-held harbour and a coalition of local and Glass-descended communities; its journeys continue after the city falls. The proposed period belongs to the long aftermath, leaving the Prophet’s generation and Arsuvan’s collapse intact.

The cycle provides room for memorable champions, captives, spouses, pilots and supernatural adversaries. Full names and complete biographies remain for review. The important commitment at this stage is that Menhir and Idols actually intervene: they alter battles, take opposing interests and complicate the return. Their actions operate through the established limits of the World Egg rather than through a newly imported Olympian council.

## Proposed historical arrangement

| World Egg period | Proposed emphasis | Boundary retained |
|---|---|---|
| Before and during c. 2300–2260 BR | Giant palace households, extraction, rival houses, revolt and destruction during the Unbinding | The established liberation sequence controls the major crisis. |
| c. 2260–2165 BR | Orphaned cultivation, workshop survival, new leadership, detached Giant strongholds and predatory heirs | Arsuvan is disintegrated; the Glass people remain sheltered through their own formation. |
| c. 2130–2050 BR | Southern separation and early encounters between different recovering societies | The Prophet is dead; no successor inherits his commission. |
| c. 2050–1900 BR | Settler conquest, alliances, mixed households and local defeats of surviving rulers | Established populations possess their own history; conquest has no divine commission. |
| c. 1900–1400 BR | Mixed maritime communities, armed households and recovery networks | Atherian identity develops through sustained contact. |
| c. 1400–800 BR | Cities, regional royal houses, Caleran and the approach to the Compact | Existing founders and the Common Sky remain in their established places. |

Arsuvan supplies the strongest local anchor. Arveth’s seizure of seed grain, Lethra’s organisation of escape and resistance, the deaths among her crews and the king’s bodily death already give its collapse specific participants. The new research should develop consequences around that account. A surviving ruler encountered two centuries later belongs to a detached house or another stronghold. Neither Arveth nor a united Arsuvan returns to furnish a second fall.

The later disruptions of c. 1600–1400 BR remain failures within the newer maritime world. The Kassilon grain seizure at Erenost retains its existing c. 1550–1500 placement and consequences. It can illustrate the vulnerability of recovery networks to war and coercion without becoming a disguised repetition of the Unbinding.

The earliest builders of the Deep Hill City remain unknown. Palace analogies do not establish ownership of every buried structure. Likewise, a later Atherian claim to an early champion must be tested individually. A story can be inherited through a conquered city, a marriage or a singer’s patron without making its hero a Glass descendant.

## Research controls and next use

The historical evidence supports a long, uneven aftermath; the exact regional history remains a World Egg proposal. Giant houses, human communities and later political claims require separate treatment. High Giants can die in mortal combat where their established circumstances permit it. A Titan-Firstborn cannot be permanently destroyed by an ordinary heroic weapon, and a giant-blooded human retains a complete human soul.

The proposed expansion should first deepen the interval between Arsuvan’s collapse and southern settlement, then add selected regional encounters to the maritime account. It should retain mundane achievements alongside heroic violence: reopening a landing, preserving a craft, planting an abandoned orchard or finding relatives across a disrupted route. These provide both the substance of recovery and the reasons later people remember a particular loss.

The revised comparison gives the Trojan war and connected homecomings a principal regional cycle. It does not create another permanent heroic company or assign every traveller to the existing wandering duo. The precise uses of the Persean and Theban outlines remain subject to collision review; the Heraclean household catastrophe already has a destination. Source adaptations retain the particular harm that drives the selected episode without accumulating every atrocity in a cycle into one invented life.

### Source access and limitations

The museum entries and Cambridge project pages were consulted as accessible texts. Cambridge book and chapter pages were used at the level of their public summaries; the full books are not represented as read. The Medinet Habu material was consulted through publication records and accessible textual extracts, without a new visual analysis. The Greek myths were consulted in online translations of ancient literary and mythographic works. Their composition and transmission are later than the societies to which many of their events are assigned.

This distinction is essential to the proposed two heroic layers. Archaeology supplies material conditions and sequences; later literature supplies received stories and their moral wounds. The World Egg arrangement is an openly identified invention informed by both.
