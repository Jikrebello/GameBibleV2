# Grobi before Atheria — Hearths, Hosts and Winter Roads

## A living history after Kharvos

The Grobi are a self-sustaining lineage of Wrath-rewritten life. Their first population arose at Kharvos during the collapse of [[Nhaldur - The Penal Crownland and the First Rewriting|Nhaldur]], when Wrath deliberately remade 2,014 human bodies as the beginning of a self-reproducing Material army. The victims' human souls separated and entered the proper judgement road. Furor made the vacated flesh grow through food, repair itself by consuming bodily reserves and seek mastery through violence. The lineage then reproduced and spread through the southern cold world.

Grobi are intelligent monsters, not injured humans, hereditary damned persons or dependent Wrath Masks. They remember, speak, plan, suffer, form packs, rear young and devise tactics. They do not possess a human soul or a moral conscience. Domination, fighting and consumption are not temptations which an otherwise moral Grobi may reject; they are the ends around which Wrath constructed Grobi life. An individual can choose a chief, route, victim, bargain or method of attack. It cannot become a moral neighbour by discovering that mercy and another creature's dignity are goods. Pack attachment resembles the loyalty of a social predator: real, sometimes intense and always bounded by hierarchy, appetite and survival.

Ancient Grobi history is difficult to reconstruct because many records are carried in drums, scars, weapon forms, repeated camp names and stories translated by enemies. A host could be temporary without being misunderstood, and the absence of writing did not make its commanders stupid. Kharvos archaeology, food middens, breeding chambers, migration traces and barter marks provide independent evidence.

## Birth, childhood and the five stages

Grobi reproduce sexually and bear live young. Pregnancy, birth and early growth require warmth, prepared food and protection from rival predators. Packs which fail to provide these things disappear. Most young mature into the goblin stage. Some well-fed lines produce young called **born-tall**, whose growth stabilises as hobgoblin without a remembered later transition. This remains variation within one lineage rather than a second species or caste.

Food after childhood can move a body through goblin, hobgoblin, orc, ogre and troll stages. Scarcity can reverse the lower transitions. Memory and learned skill continue across ordinary stage change, but rank normally follows the strength a body can still enforce. A shrinking orc remains cunning and may keep followers through fear; if it cannot, the pack tears it down.

The stages create political pressure:

- adult goblins are approximately the size and strength of human children of ten or twelve; they scout, steal, poison food, set traps and harass from concealment because a fair fight wastes them;
- hobgoblins approach human size and sustain most signalling, rough craft, organised hunting and warband discipline;
- orcs stand roughly seven to nine feet tall and possess at least the strength of five human men; concentrated food, victory and visible power make them the usual war chiefs;
- ogres consume stores at a rate few settled communities can sustain and may lose complex judgement as growth continues;
- trolls are rare regenerative apex bodies whose hunger makes ordinary household life extremely difficult.

Every stage shares the same predatory nature. Size changes the tactics available to it. Goblins prefer poison, traps, swarming attacks and false retreats. Hobgoblins organise those methods into campaigns. Orcs break human formations and gates with crude weapons which their strength makes devastating. Ogres and trolls are pointed towards walls, livestock and dense concentrations of prey by smaller Grobi clever enough to stay behind them.

## The first generations, c. 2284–2180 BR

The first Grobi woke inside sealed barracks and bodily workshops. They possessed fragments of trained movement, work sounds and altered material memory without continuing the human persons whose souls had departed. Hunger, dominance and violence governed the first days because Wrath had made those drives the organising principles of the new bodies.

After breaking the lower barracks, packs fought over the main grain lift, cistern and warm rooms. Larger bodies obtained food first and consumed rivals which would not submit. Some reproduced the prison's ration hierarchy because it gave them a ready method of control. Others fled with whatever they could carry. The first births within several years demonstrated that the Rewriting had created a lineage rather than a finite transformed cohort.

The early population divided along practical roads. Mine-route bands entered Molkar galleries. Coastal groups reached Avarn. Eastern companies followed Veyrun's road. Kharvos remained valuable because its breached ring contained shelter, tools and grain chambers. It also remained dangerous: damaged workshops, Wrath feeding marks and fighting over rooms killed successive occupants.

Many early packs failed. Leaders fed the largest fighters in anticipation of attack, then lacked food for pregnant Grobi and young. Ogres consumed the brood when stores collapsed. Bands which reproduced Nhalduran punishment without its external grain supply exhausted themselves.

## Hearth and share

The durable Grobi **hearth** was a breeding and food-defence pack rather than a moral household. It included related breeders, their young, subordinate hunters and stronger protectors able to hold the same fire. A large camp contained several hearths, rough workshops and a dominance council. A member could leave if it escaped pursuit. Human captives did not enter the hearth as adopted kin; they remained stored food.

Young normally received a hearth name after surviving their first winter. Before that point, the pack used descriptions or brood marks. Public naming identified a body worth feeding, training and avenging because it now represented an investment in the pack. A hearth could take Grobi young from a defeated rival and raise them as subordinate members. No comparable adoption existed for humans.

The **Narrow-Bowl Hearths** established the oldest well-attested brood share. Their custom required the breeding chamber and young pot to receive food before the adult war-feast. The bowl was deliberately narrow so that no hand larger than a goblin's could reach inside. A chief which stole from it advertised that it could win food today only by eating the pack's future strength.

The practice developed into the **Eighth Share**. Seven portions could be assigned to hunters, fighters, rough smiths, leaders, barter, reserve and Wrath rites; the eighth maintained breeders and young. The division was not always literally one eighth. It was enforced when enough pack members believed that preserving future numbers outweighed enlarging the current chief.

The Ash-Marrow Hearths rejected it. Their priests taught that the brood existed to replace losses after the strongest bodies had won more food. They concentrated stores in orc and ogre fighters and fed the young only from conquest. This produced spectacular growth during expansion and cannibal collapse after defeat.

## Pairing, household power and dependants

Grobi mating followed rank, possession and opportunity. A powerful body claimed access until challenged, evaded or killed. Some packs maintained several breeders around one orc; others formed temporary pairs or allowed goblins and hobgoblins to mate beyond the chief's control. Jealousy, brood ownership and the seizure of rivals' young caused continual internal violence. Human captives were not mates or future members of the lineage. They were prey.

Pregnant Grobi worked until bodily condition made the breeding chamber more useful. Stable hearths assigned several goblins or hobgoblins to feed and move the young so that one death did not waste the whole brood. War-hosts which carried breeding hearths needed slower routes, more fuel and protected sledges. Leaders abandoned or consumed them when speed or hunger mattered more than future numbers.

Feeding the young was not restricted to mothers. Older brood-mates, subordinate goblins and hearth-appointed **pot keepers** guarded the Eighth Share. A pot keeper could accuse an orc before the dominance council of weakening its own pack. The office survived only while enough fighters considered the accusation materially true.

## Language, drums and memory

The earliest Grobi speech drew from altered Nhalduran prison languages, new coinages and sounds adapted to changed mouths and chests. Regional dialects separated quickly. Kharvos, coast, mine-road and eastern bands did not share one complete language.

Large hosts developed a common war speech because commands, food tallies and warnings had to cross dialects. A speaker could know a hearth language, a host jargon and enough human words to threaten a village or deceive a sentry. Captive interpreters were forced to translate until their usefulness ended, after which the pack ate them with its other prisoners.

Drums carried signals farther and more reliably than shouted speech. Codes announced route, stage composition, food need, truce, challenge and planned attack. A host normally identified itself by a distinctive opening measure. Copying an enemy's measure could lure allies or disgrace the copied group, and drum law developed around authorised patterns.

Drum keepers memorised victories, leaders and routes as sequences joined to recited names. Tempo and material supplied context: hide, wood, bone and metal answered differently. Hosts commonly stretched the skin of a defeated enemy over a victory drum. The object advertised that the pack had consumed a rival and retained its voice as a command signal.

## Craft and the body

Grobi technology remained lower than that of the human and Ättar societies they preyed upon. Stone, bone, antler, hide, wood and stolen metal supplied most tools. Grobi smiths could heat, hammer and roughly weld scraps into heavy cleavers, spear heads, hooks and crude armour, but rarely produced a balanced blade, fitted mail or complex mechanism. Weapons were ugly, thick and wastefully made. Orc strength allowed a poorly balanced iron club to break a human shield; regeneration allowed Grobi to accept splinters, recoil and exposed limbs which would disable a human fighter.

No craft knowledge was genetically installed. Young Grobi learnt by watching, imitation, blows and repeated failure. A band which killed or lost its smiths could possess metal it could not repair. Captive human specialists might be forced to mend a gate, blade or cart before being eaten. Few taught a complete craft because Grobi chiefs valued the immediate object more than an apprenticeship and seldom preserved a captive after the present need.

Low technology shaped tactics by stage. Goblins used concealed pits, poisoned points, snares, stolen bows and hit-and-run attacks because they possessed the size and strength of human children around ten or twelve years old. Hobgoblins managed signals, shields and ambush lines. Orcs, standing roughly seven to nine feet tall and stronger than at least five human men, carried oversized axes, mauls and hooked poles which required little refinement to kill. Ogres and trolls attacked walls and dense formations as living siege engines.

Stage change produced a rough bodily craft. Grobi bodies developed **stage-glands** whose secretions regulated appetite, growth, tissue repair and reversion. Experienced cutters called **red-knucklers** learnt to recognise dangerous swelling, bind tears, drain fever and keep a valuable fighter alive through food loss. They could not safely create a new stage without the body's own nourishment.

Gland extraction from a living Grobi could cause pain, uncontrolled transition or death. Leaders used it to keep fighters large or force growth before an assault. Red-knucklers used smaller preparations to close wounds. The killer ordinarily claimed the useful organs of a defeated Grobi unless a stronger leader seized them. These practices later attracted foreign alchemists without changing Grobi creaturely continuity.

## Captives, prey and temporary truce

Grobi attacks on human settlements were feeding events. Fighters consumed people and animals during the assault, stripped the stores and gnawed or boiled leather, bone and other organic material afterwards. They did not maintain human slaves as permanent household dependants. Captives survived only when the host wanted fresh meat for later, a living guide, an interpreter or a specialist for one immediate task. Once that use ended, the pack ate them. Starvation, Grobi food and captivity could not turn a human into Grobi.

Grobi taken by humans likewise remained Grobi. Captors killed them, displayed them, forced short labour or sold them for alchemy. A bound Grobi could understand commands and feign obedience, but it possessed no moral conversion towards its captors. It waited for weakness, escape or a chance to feed.

Grobi did recognise temporary restraint when immediate attack threatened a better meal. A visitor which surrendered its weapons and set out a bowl of food could speak until the bowl was overturned. The custom did not make the visitor a guest in the human sense. It marked a pause enforced by appetite, reputation and the threat of collective retaliation.

Most recognised brokers were goblins or hobgoblins whose low food need and speed made travel practical. Human intermediaries remained outside the weapon line whenever possible. Their safety depended upon guards and on offering more food or metal than their bodies were worth.

## The White Pot truce

Around c. 1460 BR, Kharvos hearths, Kreseni metal carriers and Wolf-affiliated hall brokers established the **White Pot exchange** outside the damaged ring. Each party filled one pale clay pot with food and set it beyond its own weapon line. Trade continued while all pots stood. Every Grobi pack knew that attacking early would unite the other armed participants against it and forfeit the food already displayed. Overturning a pot ended the calculation after enough time for each side to withdraw.

The exchange moved hides, bone work, metal scrap, salt, preserved food, animals, captives sold as meat and route information. Grobi offered stolen goods for metal they could not make and livestock which could enlarge their bodies. Humans sometimes bought prisoners before the Grobi ate them. The value of the meeting lay in a predictable pause, not peace.

The White Pot was broken several times. One human chief overturned the Grobi pot before his hidden archers attacked. Grobi retaliation consumed his landing and nearby households. Later meetings placed all pots where the smallest recognised broker could reach them, preventing one large leader from ending the pause without moving through the other traders.

The practice spread to seasonal crossings. It never created peace among Grobi and humans. A hearth could trade in autumn and consume the same neighbours in winter after the marker ended.

## Religion and Wrath

Grobi knew that mass violence produced effects beyond bodily excitement. Large gatherings, repeated victory, public humiliation and anticipated attack strengthened local Wrath architectures. Drums aligned movement and appetite. Fetishes made from weapons, food vessels, enemy bones and stage-glands could become responsive. Rage speakers received visions or commands whose accuracy varied.

The **Ash-Marrow Hearths** cultivated these effects. Their priests taught that increase in body proved acceptance by the Red Appetite and that anything too weak to hold its food had already consented to be eaten. Leaders fed victory sites, staged combats and kept enemy remains near ration grounds. A dependent Wrath Mask or effect could grant fear, force or appetite and direct the host towards violence which fed Furor better than a quick victory would.

The Narrow-Bowl Hearths did not refuse Wrath. They taught that the **Table Under Stone** proved the first law of predation: the first Grobi survived because they ate, broke the doors and reproduced. Their dispute with Ash-Marrow concerned timing. A pack which fed every victory to its largest body might win one battle and leave no brood for the next. Narrow-Bowl priests therefore reserved young, stopped a feast before the breeding share vanished and regarded a chief eaten by its own followers as one which had failed to dominate.

Some packs treated Kharvos itself as an origin ground. Others avoided the lower barracks because Wrath manifestations there consumed Grobi as readily as humans. Grobi could bargain with local powers or serve foreign cults when doing so yielded prey, weather or victory. Similar red marks did not prove that every rite addressed Wrath, and no species-wide psychic field united them.

## Settlements, migration and the winter frontier

Grobi settlement followed warmth, food and defensible routes. Kharvos and lesser Nhalduran ruins supplied stone shelter. Taiga camps followed hunting and grazing. River camps combined fishing, smoke pits, hide work and opportunistic gathering. Some hearths maintained year-round lairs while sending hunting and war companies outward.

The yearly cycle created the frontier war season. Packs which survived winter bred and fed through spring, summer and autumn. Their growing numbers and stages gathered into larger companies as the cold returned. Scouts identified herds, human granaries and weak villages; smoke discipline concealed the approach; drums announced strength when terror promised an easier feeding. By winter, dozens of local warbands could be moving north and west at once. Human chroniclers called each major convergence a new Grobi horde, but it was the recurring result of the same bodily cycle.

Success compounded. Captured food allowed goblins to grow, hobgoblins to maintain organisation and orcs to attract followers. Ogres and trolls could breach strongholds. The same concentration enlarged daily consumption. Destroying or diverting food could cause reversion, desertion and internal conflict without automatically making every Grobi helpless.

Human defences changed the route. Beacons, emptied outer stores, defended crossings and refuge halls could turn a host towards weaker prey. Scorched food also threatened human civilians and future recovery. Denying food remained the most reliable way to force reversion, cannibalism and the collapse of a Grobi host.

## Hosts and councils

A **war-host** formed when several hearths and companies submitted to one leader for a route or campaign. The leader promised food, plunder and stronger prey. Large hosts included pot keepers, rough smiths, red-knucklers, drum keepers, scouts, cooks, animal handlers, breeding hearths and human captives kept as provisions.

Orc leaders were common because their seven-to-nine-foot bodies made command visible and their strength outclassed human champions. Rule lasted only while it could be enforced. Goblin scouts could desert with route knowledge. Hobgoblin quartermasters could redirect shares. An ogre could seize a feast and still fail to organise the next day's march. A chief which weakened, shrank or lost its food was commonly challenged and eaten by its own followers.

The largest political dispute concerned who could hold captured food. **First Mouth** custom fed the leader and large fighters before distribution. **Eighth Share** preserved breeders and young before the victory feast. **Taking Hand** rewarded the company which physically seized the store. These were dominance rules backed by force and expectation, not moral laws. Hosts often combined the three and fought over the result.

## The Nine-Drum Host and the Empty Halls

The greatest ancient Grobi movement was the **Nine-Drum Host** of c. 1608–1596 BR. It grew out of the winter pattern rather than beginning it. Several abundant summers supported many large-stage fighters. Early freezes then threatened reversion and death. **Gharuk Eight-Bowls**, an orc between seven and nine feet tall and stronger than at least five human men, proposed seizure of the northern hall country and a permanent Warm Road.

Gharuk joined nine companies by defeating or consuming their leaders. **Varka Red-Ladle** organised crude sledges, breeding hearths, rough repair and human prisoners forced to interpret until eaten. Her promise that the brood would share the first hall stores made permanent occupation possible. Ash-Marrow Wrath speakers treated each captured hall and its population as a feeding station.

The Narrow-Bowl leader **Urzha** betrayed Gharuk after he conquered two of her camps, ate their leaders and began feeding their young to his ogres. She gave the host's ration route to the Ättar coalitions so that the humans would break the stronger predator above her. In return, the halls marked an eastern corridor and withheld attack while her surviving pack escaped. Urzha neither befriended the Ättar nor acted for their good. She used one enemy to destroy another and preserve her own breeding strength. Other Grobi called the act treason because it denied them the captured halls.

The early campaign succeeded because human halls divided their response and because crude Grobi arms were carried by bodies far stronger and harder to kill than human soldiers. The host consumed villages, livestock and stores. It failed after human boats kept defenders supplied, scouts cut the sled road and the four newly transformed pact chiefs broke separated companies. Gharuk then opened brood sledges to maintain ogres. The loss of future fighters drove companies away.

Gharuk was wounded near Hall Arven and began to shrink. His own orc guard dragged him down and ate him alive, each competitor trying to claim the old chief's strength through his flesh. Varka withdrew several hearths. Urzha recovered part of her brood and used the marked corridor to escape. Human victors killed Grobi wounded and young at captured camps; a later company attacked stragglers after the temporary marker had fallen. The war did not end the Grobi lineage. It altered routes, strengthened Eighth Share strategy and left the Ättar with their four lunar pacts.

## After the Nine Drums, c. 1550–800 BR

Defeated companies returned with different lessons. Ash-Marrow singers blamed insufficient violence and Urzha's betrayal. Varka's surviving followers claimed that Gharuk had destroyed the host by eating its future. Several packs gave the quartermaster control of the brood ration until a chief defeated that officer publicly and took it back.

Kharvos became a regional gathering and feeding place. Narrow-Bowl hearths maintained breeding rooms near the outer ring. Rough smith packs occupied former Nemek workshops without understanding most of their mechanisms. Wrath cults held the lower barracks until repeated manifestations consumed too many Grobi and several chiefs sealed the chambers. No unified Kharvos kingdom resulted.

Human chiefs sometimes paid Grobi packs with livestock, prisoners and route knowledge to attack a rival. Such bargains redirected one hunt and never made the Grobi mercenaries bound by human honour. Kreseni carriers bartered rough iron for hides and stolen goods at guarded exchanges. Ättar halls bought prisoners or route warnings during temporary truces. Every transaction could feed the next winter attack.

The Breaking of the Bronze Roads reduced access to imported metal and grain. Grobi became still more dependent upon scavenged weapons and badly worked local iron. Bands seized abandoned cargo and forced captured craftspeople to repair its most valuable objects before eating them. Local food determined which hearths survived the disruption.

## Atherian centuries, c. 800–350 BR

Atherian expansion created new granaries, herds and military roads along the north. Grobi leaders raided these concentrations, sold captives as meat or accepted payment to attack a rival settlement first. Atherian commanders sometimes bought route intelligence from a goblin broker and killed it as soon as the information had been tested.

Foreign demand for stage-glands encouraged live capture, corpse theft and predatory barter. Red-knucklers sold preparations when the food or metal offered outweighed the gland's use to their own pack. Human alchemists exaggerated the substances into promises of instant strength. No preparation reproduced the five-stage continuity in a human body.

Casvilar's campaigns altered some northern migration corridors by building posts, requisitioning food and damaging local winter stores. Grobi hosts exploited weakened settlements and attacked military supply parties. Casvilar never campaigned against a single Grobi state, and his conquests did not reach Kharvos.

In 350 BR, ancient Grobi packs remained divided:

- Kharvos contained rival dominance councils, crude workshops, Wrath sites and the largest regular White Pot exchange;
- Narrow-Bowl packs preserved brood shares and eastern routes while disputing whether Urzha's betrayal proved cunning or weakness;
- Ash-Marrow hosts cultivated Wrath-fed victory and sought leaders able to maintain large stages;
- mine-road and coastal hearths specialised in scavenged metal, hide, fishing and ambush;
- every winter, warbands gathered and broke according to food, leadership, weather and available human prey.

No ruler commanded the entire lineage. Common biology and fragments of war speech made temporary combination possible. They did not create one mind or prevent Grobi packs from consuming one another.

## Exultation and dominance

Grobi pleasure centred on eating, pursuit, physical competition and the public humiliation of rivals. Cooks challenged one another to make bark, old hide or bitter roots edible, then forced the loser to name the hidden ingredients. Drum contests copied an enemy's measure while turning its leader's death into rhythm. Rough smiths boasted that a repaired blade had survived more owners than its current bearer would.

Young played stage games in which the smallest participant hid the feast from the largest and won by escaping. Adults repeated the game as a cruel test of a weakened chief. Animal handlers named stubborn beasts after defeated orcs. Pack attachment to brood-mates, animals, tools and drums could be fierce, but it did not produce moral regard for prey or a general capacity for mercy.

## Proposed historical rulings

- All Grobi belong to one true-breeding `wrath-rewritten-life` lineage.
- Sexual reproduction, live birth and dependent childhood are proposed as its ordinary biology.
- Most children mature through goblin stage; born-tall hobgoblin development remains a variation within the lineage.
- Stage change preserves individual continuity and does not establish five species or castes.
- The hearth and food share are pack institutions which preserve breeding strength; they do not express moral rights held by weak members.
- Human captives remain human and are kept by Grobi only as food or for immediate use before slaughter; Grobi captives remain Grobi.
- Grobi technology is crude and materially poor. Craft, bodily treatment, language and drum codes are learnt rather than biologically installed.
- Adult goblins match human children of roughly ten or twelve in size and strength and depend upon poison, traps, ambush and evasion.
- Orcs stand roughly seven to nine feet tall and possess at least the strength of five human men; larger stages increase this physical advantage.
- Wrath resonance is local and dependent upon feeding architecture, not a universal Grobi mind.
- Grobi possess intelligence, memory, suffering and tactical choice without human moral conscience. Domination, fighting and consumption define their creaturely ends.
- Each winter produces a new northern war season as surviving bands grow through the warmer months. The Nine-Drum Host was an unusually large attempt to turn that cycle into permanent conquest.
- Gharuk's host consumed human settlements and kept captives only as marching food or for immediate forced tasks.
