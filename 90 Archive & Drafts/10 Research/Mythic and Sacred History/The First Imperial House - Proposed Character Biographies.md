# The First Imperial House Proposed Character Biographies

Thalion and his first three successors governed Atheria from 350 to 247 BR. Across their reigns, the military settlement of the founding generation became a larger empire sustained by provincial farms, towns, roads and fleets. Their family history followed the same transformation: people who remembered defeated relatives and precarious claims gave way to rulers brought up inside a court whose authority appeared permanent.

These four connected biographies develop the opening century of the selected southern expansion route. The naval network supports every reign. Southern communities retain their own interests, and the territorial gains leave substantial further expansion for the Veloran and later houses.

## Aurelius Thalion

Aurelius Thalion (390–318 BR; emperor from 350) founded the Atherian Empire after the wars following Teren Casvilar's assassination. His later government regularised military service, taxation and provincial appointments while protecting many of the settlements and fortunes created by his victory. The chief concern of his final decades was that the arrangements holding the empire together should survive his death.

### Family and formation

Thalion was six when his father, Aurel, died. His mother, Lysara Casvilar, maintained the household through property, kinship and the obligations of people whom her family had assisted. He grew up seeing visitors arrive with requests which could rarely be granted without disappointing someone else. His elder sister Evara learnt the same work and later used it to protect dependants across hostile political camps.

Casvilar's designation of Thalion as heir in 370 BR brought an army's expectations to a young man without the money or command experience to satisfy them. Borrowing, sales and personal persuasion kept his claim alive. He retained a close interest in the practical terms of an agreement: who would pay, which store would supply the grain and whose relatives would guarantee delivery. He could be patient with an awkward explanation and abrupt with an impressive promise that concealed those answers.

His friendship with Istran Melvo made that habit useful in war. Melvo understood vessels, crews and repair; Thalion could secure appointments and money. Thalion relied upon people whose abilities exceeded his own in particular fields, although his willingness to depend upon them diminished when their following threatened his command.

Ilvara Deneth, whom he married in 365, gave the household a durable centre during his absences and illnesses. Their intimacy included work: she knew which petitioners he would avoid, which promises he had forgotten and which families had already received more than their public standing suggested. They enjoyed evenings with a small company after formal audiences, when Thalion could listen to the disputes of familiar people without having to pronounce a public judgement. Such access remained valuable, and their guests understood it.

### The choices that secured the throne

Thalion accepted the murder of Belisar Venn, who had helped him rise, when agreement with Darion Velsar became more useful. He confirmed veteran grants at the expense of occupied farms and suppressed the resistance around Darsil. After the final conquest of Sarqen in 350, he ordered the execution of Amesar, Casvilar's acknowledged son and a potential rival.

These decisions shaped his understanding of stability. He had seen promises raise armies and abandoned promises turn dependants into enemies. As emperor, he tried to reduce the occasions on which soldiers needed a new claimant to obtain their pay. He also resisted restitution that would threaten the loyalty of the men who had established him.

Evara's household exposed the limits of the reconciliation celebrated at court. Her son Meren was Velsar's child; Talven and Lethia were the surviving children of Velsar and Samyra, whose kingdom Thalion had conquered. Thalion could support their education and arrange useful futures while retaining authority over whether those futures were permitted at all. Evara continued to seek protections that a public celebration of family unity did not provide.

### Making the settlement last

During the first part of his imperial reign, Thalion concentrated on the Aurin core, Sarqen and the routes inherited from earlier conquest. Melvo's demobilisation arrangements left selected vessels, trained crews and repair stations in continuing service. Governors and harbour officers could call upon these resources without rebuilding a fleet for every emergency. Local merchants gained dependable customers, while assessed ports supplied labour and stores even in poor seasons.

In the southern approaches, Thalion confirmed several existing garrisons and required their officers to submit more regular accounts of provisions. Around 334–329 BR, petitions from cultivators and discharged soldiers led to a limited settlement of disputed holdings in districts behind the frontier. Some families received money or access to other land. Grants regarded as necessary to military loyalty remained intact. The settlement improved the government's knowledge of occupied farms and established further imperial claims over their produce.

His foreign relationships favoured arrangements that could be maintained at an affordable cost. Ortheian rulers and independent harbour councils received requests for passage, stores and the return of fugitives. Qahuri crowns continued to govern their own river country. Sarqen's officials dealt with their merchants and envoys, giving Atheria information and influence without transferring the crowns' sovereignty. Vashar remained a power whose responses had to enter every eastern calculation.

Thalion gave Astaroth's priesthood precedence in ceremonies of victory and command. He accepted honours associating his government with celestial order and took satisfaction in occasions when cities appeared together under his patronage. Public religion gave permanence to a settlement he knew had depended upon fragile bargains between living people.

### Caelor and Meren

His son Caelor had long been the expected successor. The injuries Caelor suffered during a building inspection in 326, followed by his death in 324, changed both the family and its political arrangements. Thalion had time to watch an anticipated future become impossible. He became more insistent that decisions about command should be completed while he could still enforce them.

He adopted Meren, Evara and Velsar's son, whose position joined the founder's family to another military inheritance. The choice disappointed supporters of Thalion's daughter Lysara and her children. It also gave the army an experienced adult whom many officers already knew. During Thalion's final illness, Meren increasingly handled reports and appointments.

Thalion died in 318 after army and senate recognition had made the succession practicable. He left a functioning government and an effective naval system, together with disputed land, favoured households and conquered communities whose obedience depended on continuing negotiation. His imperial honours did not alter his human identity or absolve his decisions.

## Meren I

Meren I (358–286 BR; emperor from 318) was Thalion's nephew and adopted son, and the biological son of Evara Thalion and Darion Velsar. His reign extended Atherian government across additional southern districts through local alliances, repeated campaigns and the conversion of military obligations into regular provincial dues. He sought a reputation for keeping agreements, but increasingly treated the emperor's interpretation of an agreement as binding upon everyone concerned.

### A childhood among survivors

Meren was eight when his father died and Thalion conquered Sarqen. Talven and Lethia entered Evara's household as children of the defeated royal alliance. Familiarity between the children grew within arrangements that reminded their attendants of rank, captivity and the dangers attached to particular names.

Meren learnt to hear two accounts of the same person. Velsar could be an abandoned husband, a generous patron, an unsuccessful commander and a threat whose defeat had supposedly rescued the state. His mother continued helping people whose histories complicated the court's version. Meren became cautious about public humiliation and preferred meetings with a small number of prepared speakers, where disagreement could be managed before it became a spectacle.

His proposed wife, Alessa Corven, came from a municipal household involved in supplying the Aurin towns. She knew the difference between a levy written in an office and grain gathered from farms with different harvests. Their daughter Cassara was born around 310 BR, eight years into Meren's reign. Alessa supervised much of her early education and introduced her to people responsible for storage and delivery. Meren welcomed Cassara's interest but sometimes found her questions about failed promises uncomfortable.

He enjoyed races and took a close interest in the fortunes of provincial teams. At such gatherings he could be relaxed, funny and visibly partisan. Sponsorship brought provincial visitors to Caleran and gave local households another route to favour. When supporters fought, he punished organisers while preserving the circuit and its public appeal.

### Extending the southern settlement

Meren confirmed army pay and existing appointments at his accession. In Sarqen, Talven's supervised office allowed part of the conquered royal family's standing to serve provincial government. The arrangement suited Meren's preference for obligations that preserved a person's public dignity while limiting their independent power.

The southern campaigns began from a similar calculation. Around 312–307 BR, rival communities in the Rauven-facing approaches sought imperial recognition of crossing dues, pasture claims and the obligations left by Casvilar's conquests. Meren's general Tavren Arel offered protection to recognised partners in return for stores, auxiliary service and access through their territory. Communities outside those agreements faced a stronger neighbour supported by imperial soldiers.

Tavren's campaigns took additional defended settlements and extended control over cultivated country between existing posts. The army used river transport where the season allowed and gathered supplies at established landings. Fleets carried bulk cargo to suitable coastal depots; local boat crews and carriers moved it inland. Southern guides determined which routes could bear a loaded column and which apparent shortcuts would strand it.

Brenna of Kellovar, a widow responsible for a substantial grain store and its dependent households, supported an early agreement that limited repeated requisitions. Her brother presented the undertaking before the recognised male assembly speakers. Brenna's purpose was to preserve seed and labour for the next harvest. When Tavren demanded additional deliveries, she withheld the store's boats and persuaded neighbouring suppliers to do the same.

Meren ordered the boats restored and required Tavren to reckon deliveries already made against the disputed demand. He retained the district's obligation to supply the army. Brenna recovered a workable harvest allowance; the imperial officer acquired a more precise account of what her households possessed. Their settlement encouraged some neighbours to seek terms and others to hide stores beyond the places officials could inspect.

### A campaign that failed

Around 305 BR, Tavren attempted to compel several Ossen route communities to provide passage and supplies beyond their existing agreements. Guides withdrew and thaw damaged the raised paths. The expedition exhausted its dry fuel and fodder before it could secure a dependable chain of deliveries. Soldiers abandoned equipment, killed transport animals for food and withdrew with injured companions whom the remaining boats could carry.

Meren retained Tavren, whose officers and local allies were difficult to replace. He restricted the next campaign to routes that could be supplied and demanded recovery of prisoners through negotiated exchanges. Several captive soldiers returned; people seized and sold by imperial auxiliaries during the advance were much harder to recover. Meren declined to unsettle every allied household implicated in those sales.

The following advances, around 303–296 BR, were slower and more durable. Garrisoned towns acquired assessed agricultural districts, regular markets and officials able to hear revenue disputes. Selected northern Belathi districts entered separate agreements or suffered conquest where their leaders opposed the new toll and supply arrangements. The territorial result included farms, woodland rights and working communities across several connected districts. Independent assemblies and rulers remained beyond the expanding provincial belt.

### Cassara's apprenticeship

Meren's later government increasingly depended upon municipal officers who could maintain the resources his campaigns had acquired. Cassara worked in the Aurin stores during the final years of his reign and represented him during illness. He valued her accuracy and persistence, although she could make an audience uncomfortable by asking how a celebrated military achievement would be maintained through winter.

He named her successor and confirmed the appointments of commanders who accepted her authority. Provincial festivals and public vows placed the succession within the religious life of the dynasty. Cassara's sex made her accession unprecedented in the imperial office; her years of visible work gave officers and town officials practical reasons to recognise her.

Meren died in 286, aged seventy-two. The government he left could extract revenue from a larger southern territory than the one he had inherited. Its roads, stores and local arrangements varied greatly in quality. Cassara had spent enough time among their accounts to know where the next failures were likely to occur.

## Cassara I

Cassara I (proposed birth c. 310 BR; empress 286–263 BR) was Meren's daughter and the first woman to hold the imperial command in her own name. Her government enlarged water storage, harbour granaries and provincial roads. These works helped Atheria occupy and govern a broader southern territory, while their demands upon land and labour produced conflicts which Cassara's own decisions sometimes intensified.

### Learning to govern

Cassara grew up during Meren's southern campaigns. Household conversations brought together victories, missing deliveries, provincial visitors and the cost of another season's service. Her mother, Alessa Corven, encouraged her to follow an account back to the people responsible for filling a store. Meren gave her duties that made her answer for shortages in public.

She liked explanations that could be demonstrated. A watercourse, loaded cart or damaged retaining wall gave her something against which to test an official's assurance. She could spend an afternoon listening to a craftsperson and later lose patience with a councillor who described customary rights without quantities or boundaries. People who knew her learnt to bring both a petition and a practical account of what its acceptance would require.

Her proposed husband, Valeran Soreth, belonged to a family experienced in harbour administration. He was comfortable among shipping households whose cooperation depended upon reputation and personal introductions. Their son Olarion was born around 283 BR, three years after her accession. Valeran encouraged the child's fascination with moving toys and visiting performers. Cassara shared some of that interest, especially when a mechanism revealed an ingenious solution to a practical problem.

### Roads and occupied country

From approximately 285 to 276 BR, Cassara directed money and labour towards the districts acquired or brought under firmer control during Meren's reign. Raised approaches, bridges, granaries and repaired river landings allowed garrisons to remain supplied for longer periods. Town councils undertook specified works in return for clearer assessments and the confirmation of market privileges.

The programme also enabled further advances. Commanders could reach cultivated districts beyond the earlier supply limit, and neighbouring leaders faced a choice between negotiating obligations and meeting troops that could remain after harvest. Some local households sought provincial status because an imperial judgement offered protection against a powerful neighbour. Others lost the ability to withdraw a seasonal contribution once it had become an annual assessment.

Cassara supported administrative work in newly governed towns and brought provincial artists and teachers to Caleran. Families whose children learnt the court's language and public conventions gained access to appointments. Local assemblies continued to handle many disputes, while imperial officers increasingly decided matters involving tax, garrisons and the land assigned to their support.

### The quarry revolt

In 274 BR, managers extended compulsory quarry service through the harvest to maintain an aqueduct programme. Workers refused to continue. Troops killed strikers, and families were expelled from housing attached to the works. Cassara dismissed two managers but upheld the compulsory programme and completed the aqueduct.

Her response followed a judgement she was willing to defend: the works served towns whose water supply could not be postponed indefinitely, and abandoning the assessment would encourage resistance elsewhere. She approved closer supervision of work schedules and treated the managers' conduct as a punishable failure within a necessary system. The affected households had already lost relatives, housing and part of their harvest. Replacing managers did not restore those losses.

The decision exposed a limit to her celebrated accessibility. She listened carefully to people explaining how to complete work and was much less receptive when they argued that the state had no right to demand it. Her ability to understand a problem could make her more determined to impose the solution she preferred.

### Harbours and the court

Around 272–266 BR, Maerdis, the head of a Dovren shipping household, negotiated a group of repair and transport agreements with Cassara's officers. Her partners wanted predictable payment, access to protected markets and clear limits upon the seizure of working boats. The government wanted crews and vessels available when southern campaigns required them.

The resulting agreements secured particular services and landings. Authority over neighbouring farms remained a separate matter. When officers tried to extend a harbour obligation to those farms, Maerdis supported the landholders' objection: involuntary inland levies threatened the markets that supplied her crews. Cassara accepted the limit where the transport agreement remained useful and demanded new terms where commanders sought a permanent base.

Her patronage gave musicians, astronomers and comic performers opportunities at court. She enjoyed skilled imitation, including performances in which officials' elaborate explanations became the joke. Astronomers connected observation, calendars and the dynasty's public religious ceremonies. Cassara regarded orderly worship and useful knowledge as compatible parts of imperial government.

Olarion grew up among these visitors. His mother brought him into audiences and expected increasing competence, but often corrected him before people whose respect he wanted. He became more confident hosting entertainments in which he controlled the invitations and the programme. Cassara continued to regard experience as something he would acquire through office, as she had done.

She died in 263, leaving her son a larger and better connected empire. Provincial commanders could now sustain armies with resources organised far from Caleran. Their recognition made Olarion emperor, and their continued cooperation remained necessary to his reign.

## Olarion

Olarion (proposed birth c. 283 BR; emperor 263–247 BR) inherited the throne from Cassara at about twenty. His court supported ingenious crafts and elaborate entertainments, while executions, confiscations and denunciations made political service increasingly dangerous. By his death, several military and provincial networks could support rival claims to the government his family had enlarged.

### Growing up in an accomplished court

Olarion's childhood gave him access to performers, engineers and visitors from distant provincial towns. He acquired a good memory for faces and enjoyed arranging occasions at which people with different talents could surprise one another. Mechanical birds and moving scenery fascinated him. He could notice an improvement in a device which a senior official dismissed as a toy.

His mother's public competence made his own apprenticeship difficult. Cassara's questions were designed to expose a weak explanation, and he experienced some of those audiences as humiliation. In private gatherings he could reward wit, settle the order of performance and decide whose work deserved attention. At his accession he wanted the court to become the place where the empire's varied accomplishments received recognition from him.

Mirsa Tel, a proposed maker of small mechanisms and stage devices, became one of the craftspeople he particularly favoured. She gained a workshop and the means to train apprentices. Olarion could be generous about failed experiments when he understood what the maker had attempted. The same patience rarely extended to a governor whose explanation challenged the emperor's political judgement.

### Patronage and command

Olarion's early reign continued existing administration and frontier commitments. Public festivals rewarded craftsmen, performers and provincial delegations. He expanded night processions and private performances, sometimes requiring costly alterations at short notice. Officers responsible for transport found themselves balancing the movement of court materials against stores already promised to garrisons.

Around 260 BR, he required more decisions about rewards and senior appointments to return to Caleran. The measure answered a real concern: provincial commanders could distribute advantages that made a town's prosperity depend upon them personally. It also delayed settlements that had previously been reached near the people affected.

In the proposed development of the canon execution of an eastern commander, Avel Darsen returned to Caleran without permission in 258 BR. He brought officers and petitions from harbours seeking payment of arrears. Darsen expected his service and the standing of the petitioners to force an audience. Olarion treated the arrival as an attempt to compel the imperial household and ordered his execution.

The charges did not resolve the underlying debts. Harbour officers continued supplying forces for which payment was late. Some began seeking promises from commanders who could protect their claims. Olarion's effort to assert control made a personal military patron more attractive to people waiting upon the court.

### The household under suspicion

Olarion later ordered two relatives killed after accusations that household rites concealed a succession gathering. The victims' identities remain open in this draft. Their observances took place in a world where public religious standing, family remembrance and political connection often met. The emperor chose to treat the accusations as grounds for execution.

Confiscations supplied money and rewarded people willing to denounce others. They also changed the conduct of ordinary audiences. A governor had reason to minimise a shortage; an attendant had reason to report a private complaint; a family threatened by an informer had reason to send children and portable wealth towards a friendly army.

Olarion remained capable of affection and enjoyment within his selected company. Mirsa's workshop received materials while other public accounts went unpaid. That unequal security made her dependants loyal to the patron who protected them and anxious about what would happen when his favour moved elsewhere. Palace pleasure relied upon many people who had little freedom to refuse its demands.

### The shortages at Caleran

In 252 BR, food shortages brought demonstrations. Olarion released grain, and soldiers killed people trying to enter a closed store. Subsequent games quieted much of the city while the families of the dead continued seeking information and restitution.

The shortage revealed weaknesses in a system that still possessed farms, ships and substantial stores. Arrears made carriers reluctant to accept another compulsory voyage. Officers reserved supplies for units whose support they required. Officials feared the consequences of admitting how badly their own accounts had fallen behind. Court expenditure aggravated these pressures, but several failures of authority and delivery stood between available grain and the people waiting for it.

The empire continued to govern substantial southern provinces throughout these years. Town officers collected dues, repaired roads and handled disputes. The same capacity allowed military patrons to gather supplies when confidence in the court failed. Olarion inherited resources distributed across a wide territory and progressively lost the cooperation needed to direct them together.

### Death and the four claims

Olarion died suddenly in 247. Illness, poison and an attendant's violence remained competing explanations. No version is selected here. His death left no successor whom all the principal commands were willing to recognise.

Avaren Mott drew authority from the senate, capital and part of the guard. Daran Thalion held western estates and veteran support. Aster Halven commanded ships and eastern garrisons, with backing from ports seeking dependable payment. Samor Veloran controlled the central river army and its grain corridor. Each claim rested upon institutions and relationships developed under the first dynasty.

The ensuing war disrupted provincial supply and brought requisitions, executions and new military settlements. Samor's victory in 246 restored a single command. He inherited a much larger and more capable state than Thalion had founded, together with communities that had learned how much their obligations could change when a different army reached the capital.

## What the first house leaves behind

By 247 BR, the proposed expansion has turned parts of the inherited southern frontier into substantial governed districts containing cultivated land, markets, woodland resources and settled populations. Fleets and allied shipping connect their approaches to the imperial core. The next dynasty can extend that provincial body farther into the southern and eastern country whose later history contributes to Eclessia.

Qahur still has its crowns. Vashar remains an independent eastern power. Ortheia and the Elmaric harbours maintain varied relationships with Caleran, and the great eastern transfers and later Qahuri annexation remain ahead. The Four Eagles exposes the political cost of the first century's achievement: several men can now assemble the money, transport and soldiers required to claim an empire.
