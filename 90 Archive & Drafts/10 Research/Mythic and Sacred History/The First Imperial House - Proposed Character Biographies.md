# The First Imperial House Proposed Character Biographies

Thalion and his first three successors governed Atheria from 350 to 247 BR. The structural proposal of this page is simple: good government and the southern occupation built the stores, roads, fleets and paid commands that later funded the Painted Reign. By 247 several men could assemble the money, transport and soldiers needed to claim an empire. Canon facts are in [[The House of Thalion]], [[The Later Reign of Aurelius Thalion]] and [[Age of Glory - Authorial History and Integration Review]]; [[The First Imperial House - Sources and Development Review|the development review]] lists what here is proposal.

## Aurelius Thalion, 350–318 BR

Thalion's chief concern in his last decades was that the settlement should survive his death. He kept selected vessels, trained crews and repair stations in continuing service after Melvo's demobilisation, so that governors could call on a fleet without rebuilding one. In the southern approaches he confirmed existing garrisons and required regular accounts of provisions. Around 334–329 BR petitions from cultivators and discharged soldiers produced a limited settlement of disputed holdings: some families received money or other land, grants needed for army loyalty stayed intact, and the government learned what the occupied farms produced.

Caelor's death under the untested treasury hall (injured 326, died 324) made Thalion insist that decisions about command be completed while he could enforce them. He adopted Meren, disappointing the supporters of his daughter Lysara, who was named for his mother. The wardens' test he imposed after the fall made every later public work dearer in stone and labour.

## Meren I, 318–286 BR

Meren extended government across additional southern districts through local alliances, repeated short campaigns and the conversion of military obligations into regular provincial dues. Around 312–307 BR, rival communities in the Rauven-facing approaches sought imperial recognition of crossing dues and pasture claims. His general (unnamed) offered protection to recognised partners in return for stores, auxiliary service and access. Brenna of Kellovar, a widow managing a grain store and its dependent households, withheld her store's boats when the army demanded deliveries beyond an agreed limit; Meren restored the boats but had deliveries already made reckoned against the demand. Neighbours learned both to seek terms and to hide stores.

**The Ossen failure, c. 305 BR.** An attempt to compel the Ossen route communities to provide passage beyond their agreements failed when guides withdrew and thaw broke the raised paths. The column exhausted its dry fuel and fodder, killed transport animals for food and withdrew with the wounded the boats could carry. Prisoners came back by exchange; people seized and sold by imperial auxiliaries mostly did not. Thereafter campaigns stayed on routes that could feed a garrison, and the slower advances of c. 303–296 BR turned garrisoned towns into assessed districts.

## Varelan I and Cassara, 286–263 BR

Varelan, a harbour officer adopted as Meren's heir, and Cassara, Meren's daughter, who knew where the Aurin accounts showed shortages first, spent c. 285–276 BR joining the districts Meren had taken: raised approaches, bridges, granaries and river landings that let garrisons stay supplied through winter. Dovren shipping households sold carriage and repair on terms they could enforce at their own ports and refused to let harbour obligations spread to neighbouring farms.

Every new work carried the Star's share, and the quarries could not cut the extra stone in the working season. The strike of 274 BR over "stone for the Star to break" was put down by troops. Cassara pressed for the removal of two managers; Varelan dismissed them, kept the labour assessment and kept the test. Cassara's own judgement was that the works served towns whose water could not wait. Her death date is left open; she may have outlived Varelan.

## Olarion, 263–247 BR

Olarion succeeded his father at about twenty. Around 260 BR he pulled decisions on rewards and senior appointments back to Caleran, answering a real danger of provincial patrons while delaying settlements that had once been made near the people affected. The execution of an eastern commander who came to Caleran with harbour officers and their petitions for arrears left the debts unpaid and made a personal military patron more attractive to the harbours. Confiscations rewarded informers, who swore before the accession portrait. By the shortage of 252, arrears made carriers reluctant to sail, officers reserved supplies for units they needed, and officials hid how far their accounts had fallen behind.

## What the first house leaves behind

By 247 the inherited southern frontier had become governed districts with cultivated land, markets and settled people; fleets and allied shipping joined them to the core; and the capital, the western veterans, the eastern fleet and the river army each commanded enough of the empire's resources to make a claim. Qahur kept its crowns, and Vashar remained an independent eastern power.
