# The Child Collections and the Destruction of Qarzeth

## The civic sanctuary

The sanctuary of the Bronze-Faced Provider stood within Qarzeth's upper district. Its estate owned workshops, gardens, storehouses and dwellings. Priests distributed food during festivals and financed public works. Leading households gained prestige by supporting it. These services made the institution part of ordinary civic life long before its last and most violent decades.

The cult taught that security required an irreplaceable gift. Human sacrifice occurred across the Republic's history with changing frequency. Animals could serve as substitutes in some rites. Particular families vowed children in moments of fear. The civic government expanded the practice by maintaining collectors, holding rooms and official quotas.

Children taken for sacrifice were killed. Some were infants offered by their families. Others were older captives, dependants or children purchased from poor households. Wealthy patrons sometimes surrendered their own and sometimes paid for substitutes. Dependent towns faced demands enforced by soldiers.

## The Years of Three Collections

Military restriction, border loss and public anxiety gave the priesthood greater influence after 580 BR. The Provider's interpreters promised restored safety if the city paid without evasion. Three exceptional collections between about 548 and 540 reached into towns which had previously offered animals or money.

Collectors travelled with civic lists and guards. Families sent children into orchards, concealed them in cargo or placed them with relatives under different household names. Some neighbours informed for payment or to protect their own children. Punishments included confiscation, imprisonment and the taking of another household member.

Ilya worked in the sanctuary stores and saw children held before the third collection. She copied names onto packing strips and left Qarzeth in a grain cart. Her list contained errors and omissions; it also identified rooms, officials and collection districts. She found protection at Esharat.

Sapha of Esharat organised carriers, midwives and rural households into a rescue route. They moved children beneath produce, through irrigation channels and aboard small fishing vessels. The network saved dozens and failed others. Collectors killed Sapha's husband during a search. Her daughter Neral was taken and sacrificed in Qarzeth.

The survivors did not agree upon what should follow. Ilya wanted the sanctuary exposed and dismantled. Sapha wanted the capital's power broken. Qarzeth exiles demanded restoration under new judges. Belqara leaders wanted independence. Their testimony nevertheless established a shared fact: the killings belonged to a public institution protected by the Republic.

## The Mask and its human servants

The Bronze-Faced Provider was a dependent Gluttony Mask. Its local pattern gathered around the belief that the city consumed precious lives to preserve the plenty of its households. It appeared in smoke, dreams and moments of collective fear. Periods of good harvest following sacrifice strengthened the cult, even when weather and ordinary labour explained the food.

The Mask did not invent every practice or compel every participant beyond choice. Priests developed doctrine, magistrates approved collectors, patrons paid and guards held children. The Mask exploited and rewarded the feeding architecture they maintained. Its promises always required another offering because no finite death could satisfy the Hunger behind it.

Opponents sometimes received help from shore powers, human allies or fortunate weather. None of that aid created a universal holy war. The destruction of the sanctuary later broke this particular feeding system. Gluttony's guilty sovereignty continued elsewhere.

## Caleran debate

Escaped families reached Atherian ports during the decades after the second war. Their stories circulated among sailors, temples and political patrons. Orsen Valeor made Ilya's list and testimony from former captives central to his speeches. His repeated demand that the child-taking city fall became the phrase by which the final policy was remembered.

The demand contained two truths. Qarzeth's government was murdering children, and the cult possessed defenders among its ruling houses. Caleran also wanted land, security, shipping and the permanent removal of a recovered rival. Some senators cared chiefly about the children. Others used their deaths to win support for conquest.

Mera Castren proposed inspectors, hostages and compulsory closure of the sanctuary. Gateway opponents of sacrifice supported parts of her policy. They feared that annihilation would punish the families who had resisted beside the officials who took children. Valeor argued that Qarzeth would reopen the rooms as soon as Caleran withdrew.

## Siege and rescue

After Qarzeth surrendered weapons and hostages, the Caleran demand expanded to evacuation. The inhabitants refused. Opponents of sacrifice fought in defence of their homes beside cult patrons. Some tried to overthrow the priesthood during the siege and were killed by civic guards.

The blockade reduced food. Children, elderly people and enslaved labourers died first in crowded districts. Priests continued selected sacrifices and claimed that surrender would make every prior death meaningless. Ilya guided an Atherian detachment towards the holding rooms after the wall was breached.

Soldiers killed guards and opened the chambers. They found living children and cremated remains. Parents recovered some captives. Others identified clothes or tokens belonging to the dead. The image was broken and the sanctuary serving rooms dismantled.

Elsewhere in the city, the victorious army murdered inhabitants, raped captives and organised people for sale. Some rescued children were separated again during the distribution of prisoners. Officers intervened unevenly. The army which ended the civic sacrifices also committed crimes under its own command.

## The salting

Caleran's senate had chosen to prevent Qarzeth from returning as a capital. After the sack, engineers identified the upper sanctuary district, civic squares, elite gardens, store compounds and selected waterworks. Salt arrived through the harbour and was spread heavily across those areas. Cisterns were fouled, channels broken and building stone removed.

The salting damaged a defined urban area rather than the whole country. Its cost and organisation made the act a statement of power as well as material destruction. Villages, fields and lesser towns remained outside the treated ground. Qarzeth's outer agricultural settlements continued under new authorities.

The centre never recovered. Scavengers entered the ruins, exiles attempted illegal burials and later cultists searched for fragments of the Provider's image. Sarqen prohibited the use of Qarzeth stone in royal buildings, partly from fear and partly to deny claims that the old capital had been restored elsewhere.

## People after the event

Ilya lived at Esharat and spent her remaining years matching rescued people with relatives. Later Atherian accounts made her a grateful witness to Caleran virtue. Her own fragments include the names of children lost after the breach and a curse against soldiers who sold them.

Sapha survived her daughter by many years. She refused office in the spared-town league and trained carriers to recognise collection marks used by traffickers. Esharat's later rescue festival honoured her family while omitting the households she had threatened into cooperation.

Orsen Valeor died before the final assault. Caleran erected a statue showing him with a rescued infant. Gateway survivors broke its face during a later disturbance. Mera Castren helped establish ransoming funds after the sack and defended the senate's right to rule the conquered country.

The dead children acquired no collective supernatural personhood. Their souls remained human souls. Names, ashes, family memories and later cult images were distinct. No relic or apparition should be accepted as one of them without the ordinary evidence required by the setting's soul rules.

## Historical inheritance

The spared towns built a public prohibition against human sacrifice into their later agreements. The Nerethan kings repeated the prohibition at accession. This was a real moral inheritance and a source of royal legitimacy.

The prohibition could also hide other violence. The new country retained slavery, compulsory labour and harsh punishments. Royal speakers invoked rescued children against political rivals whose families had opposed the collections before the crown existed. The memory remained powerful because the killings were real, not because every later use of them was honest.
