# Sacred Basin Houses, Cities and Ordinary Life

## Principal towns

### Tenaret

Tenaret stood on the middle river where several cultivated districts could exchange produce without passing through the lower fan or southern narrows. The Senareth kings enlarged its store courts and quays. Later dynasties valued its royal tombs, medical houses and claim to the joined-reed coronation.

The town's prosperity depended upon villages beyond its walls. Grain boats arrived in fleets after harvest, and temple estates employed brewers, linen workers, stonecutters, gardeners and healers. The tomb-workers' stoppage of 903 BR began in an artisan settlement outside Tenaret and became the best-known early dispute over withheld rations.

The largest White Measure complex near the city was the Copper Orchard of Tamekha. Its workshops improved mineral embalming and treated wounded labourers while using prisoners in lethal experiments. After Tamekha's death, demands from her Second House contributed directly to the grain diversion which provoked the stoppage.

### Qemaret

Qemaret occupied the channels of the lower fan. Barges unloaded into store basins connected with cart roads towards the gateway coast. Foreign merchants and soldiers settled there in substantial numbers, helping the Yasharet house acquire power.

The city endured the massacre of 1076 BR and recovered because its canals and workshops remained essential. Later poetry calls it the Foreign Queen's Nest or the Gate of Plenty according to the teller's politics. Families whose ancestors survived the fall preserve both traditions.

Qemaret became the commercial centre of the White Measure under the Yasharet house. Khaseret's funerary harbour and later House of the Returning Cargo joined warehouses in the city to rooms within her Egg White estate. Merchants used its storage and route knowledge without necessarily accepting the movement's complete teaching.

### Sakhret

Sakhret guarded the southern narrows, quarry approaches and roads towards pasture and mines. Its rulers drew strength from garrison households and from communities able to move animals and supplies through the dry margins. Southern restorers repeatedly used the town as a base.

Sakhret festivals included wrestling, boat-hauling contests and an evening meal for returning quarry crews. Royal accounts celebrated the stone they brought home; household songs more often remember crushed hands, jokes played on overseers and the names of companions who died on the road.

### Aseret

Aseret began as a garrison and craft settlement. Ithren III made it the capital of the Single Radiance and diverted labour and temple property towards its ceremonial way. After his dynasty fell, the great sanctuary was dismantled. Workshops, houses and a military post continued around the abandoned foundations.

### Isethar

Isethar lay between the strongest royal centres. Its boats and storehouses allowed the town to feed one crown while negotiating with another. Talmera's steering board, bearing rival seals, became an emblem of double-dealing in royal histories and of practical courage among boat households.

Meresha, the earliest named teacher of the White Measure, belonged to an Isethar funerary-linen household. Her Court of Unspent Lamps stood beyond the principal drying grounds. Later followers turned the town into a place of pilgrimage, while local boat families remembered that Meresha acquired part of her wealth from neighbours unable to repay famine loans.

These houses intermarried and adopted children from allied families. Later genealogies simplified disputed parentage and removed foreign spouses. The dynasty names express political memory more reliably than biological purity.

## Work and the flood year

The agricultural year began with the approach of high water. Crews cleared channels, repaired banks and moved animals from low ground. Flood measurers recorded water at fixed stones, while experienced cultivators judged local soil by colour and smell. A crown could collect their figures; it could not replace their knowledge.

As water withdrew, households sowed grain and tended lentils, flax, onions, dates and orchard crops according to district. Fishing and fowling supplemented the harvest. Grain moved through household bins, village stores, temple estates and royal collections. Each level provided security in a bad year and an opportunity for seizure.

Women owned and inherited fields, boats, workshops and household goods under several basin customs. They appeared as temple musicians, healers, brewers, merchants and royal patrons. The strongest marriage houses used maternal descent to connect rival crowns. These powers existed within an unequal society in which male soldiers and estate officials could still coerce labour, marriage or property.

## Learning, craft and recreation

Temple and palace schools taught writing, calculation, medicine, ritual speech and the administration of stores. Apprentices copied instructions praising restraint while enduring beatings for errors. Scribes mocked one another's handwriting, composed love songs and wrote complaints about food, heat and tedious travel.

The Basin's workshops produced linen, painted pottery, metal fittings, river craft, carved furniture and funerary goods. Large commissions brought specialists together for years. Workers formed friendships and marriage networks across estates whose patrons were enemies.

White Measure commissions joined embalming, metalwork, architecture, medicine and dream craft. A completed Second House might occupy a generation of work. Its builders fashioned corresponding objects for Material and Egg White rooms, prepared the founder's body and trained the witnesses responsible for closing the soul-binding. Failed preparations killed practitioners and attendants while leaving workshops with debts that survived them.

Public recreation included boat races, wrestling, music, board games and processions timed to the river year. Beer and sweet cakes accompanied funerals as well as festivals. A famous comic song from Qemaret concerns a proud tax collector who loses his wig, sandals and writing board while pursuing a goose through a flooded courtyard.

## Forms of dependence

Seasonal canal duty required households to contribute labour or provide substitutes. Temple dependants worked land assigned to a sanctuary and could pass obligations to their children. Debtors pledged harvest shares, property or household service. War captives and purchased slaves could be sold away from their families.

These conditions were distinct even when royal inscriptions called all of them service. A seasonal worker expected to return to a field after the canal opened. An enslaved quarry worker possessed no such right. Debt arrangements could become hereditary through manipulated accounts. Temple asylum sometimes protected a fugitive and sometimes returned that person to an owner with powerful patrons.

Manumission occurred through payment, adoption, service or a patron's decision. Freed people often remained tied to the old household through work and obligation. Their descendants could rise within towns while royal genealogies continued to describe them as outsiders.

House-Builders gave dependence an eternal form. A proprietor could free a servant in public law and require that person's posthumous service as the price of training, land or protection. Other attendants entered willingly to remain with spouses, relatives or beloved employers. The final rite treated these different circumstances as equivalent consent. Successful bindings preserved complete human souls; unsuccessful attendants became ordinary dead, residue or imposed household roles.

## Religious life

Villages kept river, field, household and burial observances. Some flood ceremonies addressed a local Menhir manifestation or a place-bound court person. Their terms concerned water, boundary, rot, fertility or passage. A successful inundation established that a relationship had functioned; it offered no judgement upon the king who claimed credit.

Royal cults arranged these local powers into dynastic families. Pride and Greed Masks exploited tomb ownership, tribute and the claim that the crown possessed every useful thing in the Basin. The dead ruler's human soul remained separate from the statues, copied persona and office maintained in the tomb estate.

The White Measure attempted something more exact. Its successful rite caught the founder's complete human soul before departure and bound it to a prepared necropolis which anchored a private demiplane in the Egg White. Such founders became Second-House Patrons. Their realms and powers were real, but they remained self-bound humans who had delayed judgement. Many costly rites failed or attracted Greed Masks able to imitate the expected ancestor.

The Boatman appeared beneath several titles in funeral practice. Priests described him as a servant of a local death power or as a royal ferryman. He remained the commissioned psychopomp and belonged to no basin temple.

## People whose relationships carry the history

### Neshara Senareth

Neshara transformed transport and canal relationships into the first durable human crown. Her supply settlements saved districts from famine and imposed labour which later descendants treated as royal property. Basin tradition remembers her as a ruler who could name every quay between Tenaret and Qemaret. Household songs remember the brother she left in charge of her boats and never trusted with a crown.

### Talmera of Isethar

Talmera supplied rival courts during the first Twin-Reed division. She married a Sakhret officer, employed Qemaret pilots and lost a son to each side's hostage system. Her surviving daughter inherited the boats. Later judges cited Talmera as a warning against divided loyalty; boat crews poured the first cup of a shared cargo in her name.

### Meresha of Isethar

Meresha financed grain during the famine of 1468 BR and took workshop shares from households unable to repay her. She used the resulting fortune and a damaged Ishkatari receiving plate to develop the White Measure. Her final rite opened the Court of Unspent Lamps around 1439 BR. Meresha gave descendants useful counsel and protected her household while holding the freed scribe Hesenu within the realm as a bound servant. Her history joins practical generosity, calculated acquisition and a genuine refusal of the soul-road.

### Khaseret and Tamekha

Khaseret of Qemaret built a commercial Second House whose cellars sheltered some families during Aramen's conquest and excluded others for inherited debts. Tamekha Rasen combined medicine, military supply and experiments upon prisoners before opening the Copper Orchard outside Tenaret. Their demiplanes made both women effective Patrons after death. Khaseret pursued scattered cargo and distant anchors; Tamekha demanded bodies, medical knowledge and the completion of her copper-leaved estate.

### Khevar II

Khevar restored travel and a common flood calendar. He also killed rival relatives after promising their household protection. His reign demonstrates why later Qahuri memory can praise reunification while condemning its founder.

### Talzara Yasharet

Talzara held Qemaret after her husband's death, brought mixed military households into the royal court and prevented the expulsion of immigrant districts. Her dynasty's officers dispossessed opponents and made southern grain serve lower-basin wars. Restoration histories reduced her to a foreign enchantress; Qemaret retained her as a human queen whose choices created allies and victims.

### Aramen of Sakhret

Aramen grew up among the court he later overthrew. He used its friendships to open Qemaret and failed to control the killing which followed. His heirs built the Basin's largest human empire. The dynasty called him Liberator of the Reed; survivor houses called him the Guest Who Opened the Gate.

### Nehara of the Blue Hands

Nehara erased images for Ithren III and eventually concealed a small group she had been ordered to destroy. Later praise made this final refusal the whole of her life. Her story retains the years of obedience, the workers she protected and the dead whose images she did not save.

### Lemara, Tazirun and Hesara

Lemara of Esharat and Prince Tazirun Zaheret joined a carrying family to a junior basin branch around 548 BR. Their daughter Hesara helped hide children from Qarzeth's collectors and later became Hadrima's mother. Court genealogy enlarged the marriage into a union of river and sea. Its historical importance lay in kin, knowledge and prestige rather than dominion.
