# The Spared Towns and the Nerethan Settlement

## Survival after Qarzeth

The destruction of Qarzeth left the Gateway country without its principal government. It did not destroy the country. Esharat retained cultivated land and carriers. Belqara still produced grain after fields damaged by the war were resown. Sarqen possessed a sheltered harbour and repair shore. Hazreth and several fishing towns remained inhabited.

The first winter brought hunger, crowding and disputes over property. Refugees entered houses abandoned by people killed or enslaved. Returning captives found relatives dead and fields granted to Atherian soldiers. Qarzeth officials sought protection in towns which had resisted their collections. Families who had supplied the besiegers demanded reward.

Atherian commanders distributed food and imposed reparations through the same local leaders. Their priorities were order, supply and payment. They preserved productive districts because the occupying force needed them. Some officers protected households; others seized warehouses and sold access to confiscated land.

## The league of spared towns, c. 520–499 BR

Esharat, Sarqen, Belqara and Hazreth sent representatives to seasonal meetings concerned with grain movement, refugees, ransoms and coastal defence. The arrangement became known as the **Spared Town League**, although it possessed no single constitution. Each town retained its own officers and bargained separately with Atherian supervisors.

Esharat's carriers organised the movement of seed and repaired tools inland. Belqara supplied food in exchange for reductions in war payment. Sarqen became the principal naval station because its harbour could receive large ships. Hazreth watched the outer coast and sheltered families avoiding confiscation.

The league kept the old Harbour Judgeship in Sarqen and Hazreth as a municipal office. The Council of Houses dispersed. Some members died at Qarzeth, some entered exile and some joined town governments under new names. The citizen assembly of the former capital had no countrywide successor.

Public ceremonies began to commemorate rescue from the holding rooms and the towns which had hidden children. They also became occasions for reading people killed, enslaved or missing after the sack. Caleran officers disliked the second list and could not suppress it without damaging the cooperation they needed.

## Tazirun, Lemara and Hadrima's maternal house

Lemara belonged to an Esharat family which owned fields, store space and pack animals. Prince Tazirun Zaheret entered the town's carrying trade after losing influence in a Tenaret succession dispute. They married around 548 BR and raised their household at Esharat.

Tazirun brought scribes, linen workers, mortuary singers and knowledge of basin court ceremony. He acquired no claim over Esharat beyond the relationships his marriage established. Their daughter Hesara inherited land, storage interests and kin in both countries.

Hesara helped conceal children during the Years of Three Collections and later transported grain during the siege. Her household saved lives and improved its political position through the same work. She also purchased enslaved labour for its estates after the sack. Her daughter Hadrima inherited a Gateway home, Qahuri kin and a family reputation built from rescue, commerce and advantage.

Hadrima's maternal family therefore joined Gateway survival to a genuine but junior Qahuri relationship. The connection gave her access to kin, ritual specialists and political language. It could support a ceremonial claim without making her heir to a basin crown.

## Nerethan of Delkos, 499–462 BR

Caleran appointed Nerethan of Delkos to command Sarqen's harbour in 499 BR. He arrived with officers, soldiers, engineers and creditors. Local pilots and repair households determined whether his ships could move. Esharat and Belqara determined whether the garrison could eat.

Nerethan married Hadrima in 497. Affection became part of a political partnership whose practical interests were clear. He needed her kin and knowledge; she wanted protection for Esharat's holdings and influence over Sarqen's new government. Their surviving letters concern shipments, family illness, appointments and repeated arguments over fields assigned to veterans.

Hadrima prevented several confiscations by proving continuous cultivation and threatening to withdraw carriers. She accepted compulsory labour for harbour works and failed to protect households with weaker ties. Nerethan punished an officer who raped a local woman and allowed another officer to keep enslaved children taken during a punitive expedition. Their government combined restraint, coercion and negotiated recovery.

Sarqen's enlarged stores attracted merchants and workers. Apprenticeships and marriages joined Delkosi, Esharati, Qarzeth exile and older harbour households. Several languages were used at the quay. The first royal residence grew from the commander's compound beside the repair shore.

## Arenes and the crown, 462–434 BR

Nerethan died around 462 and Hadrima around 459. Their son Arenes had served in the garrison and supervised inland provisions. Caleran's attempt to appoint an outside successor divided the military household and alarmed town leaders who expected new grants against their property.

Arenes secured the harbour and called the spared-town representatives to Sarqen. Esharat supported him through Hadrima's kin. Belqara demanded fixed grain obligations. Hazreth wanted command of its own coastal watch. Arenes accepted these conditions and promised to preserve the sacrifice prohibition.

Caleran recognised him as hereditary ruler in 457 in return for payment, military access and ships. The recognition converted a command household into a crown. It did not make the towns his possessions. Their leaders retained powers over water, markets, worship and local property, while the king controlled foreign policy, garrisons and much of the port revenue.

The accession ceremony combined a Gateway harbour oath, Qahuri linen and burial forms, and Atherian military recognition. Later court historians called the rite ancient. Its components had been assembled within living memory.

## Tavren and Olymes, 434–375 BR

Tavren, son of Arenes, succeeded in 434. He established convoy patrols, enlarged Sarqen's autumn market and funded a rescue feast at Esharat. He married **Lysara of Hazreth**, whose family controlled coastal watch stations. Their partnership brought the outer port firmly into the kingdom.

Tavren's government prospered from shipping and stable harvests. It also used forced labour to extend a breakwater. A collapse killed enslaved workers and indebted townsmen. The royal household paid compensation to free families and treated the enslaved dead as loss of property. Hazreth songs preserve Lysara's unsuccessful attempt to have every victim named at the dedication.

Olymes succeeded his father in 408 BR. He cultivated relationships with Atherian commanders and paid heavily for recognition during a dispute with his cousins. New collections fell upon cultivators and port traffic. His court developed elaborate claims to Qarzeth's whole country, the spared-town rescue and Tazirun's Qahuri ancestry.

Olymes's children grew up among several possible inheritances. Merath built relationships with older Gateway houses. Samyra learned the languages of town envoys and cultivated Esharat. Nethar depended upon the garrison and minister Vardek. The dispute after Olymes's death arose from these living political constituencies as well as sibling ambition.

## Samyra and the end of the ancient balance

Samyra's contested succession, restoration and later alliances belong to the already developed imperial-founding history. Package A contributes the country beneath her court. She inherited a working harbour, cultivated towns, Atherian obligations, a public rejection of sacrifice and a dynasty created through marriage and military office.

Her Qahuri claim descended through Hadrima, Hesara, Lemara and Tazirun. Tenaret genealogists recognised the marriage and disputed its political meaning. Samyra's court used joined reeds, sea birds and the open harbour in royal art. The symbols expressed aspiration rather than ownership of every place they represented.

Thalion's annexation in 350 ended independent Nerethan rule. Sarqen, Esharat, Belqara and Hazreth continued. Qarzeth remained ruined. Families within the same kingdom remembered liberation, occupation, profit, loss and rescue in combinations no royal ceremony could make uniform.

## Institutions carried forward

|---|---|---|
| Harbour Judges | Qarzeth republic | municipal officers at Sarqen and Hazreth |
| town elders and water households | pre-republic local communities | local government, property and cultivation |
| sacrifice prohibition | opponents of the Qarzeth collections | accession oath and public royal claim |
