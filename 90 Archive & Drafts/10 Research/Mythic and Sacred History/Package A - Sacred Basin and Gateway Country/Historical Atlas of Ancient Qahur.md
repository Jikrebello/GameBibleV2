# Historical Atlas of Ancient Qahur

## The river civilisation

Ancient Qahur was the civilisation of the Sacred Basin before the Covenant. Its communities cultivated a long river corridor between dry northern country and the approaches leading south and east. The modern name *Qahuri* is used here for clarity. The people of the earliest centuries identified themselves more often by town, district, temple, household or the stretch of water they maintained.

The Basin supported several kingdoms across its history. Kings could claim both reeds, patronise the same burial rites and send officials along the same waterway without holding the whole country. Later historians call these regimes the **Twin-Reed Crowns**. The expression describes a changing political tradition rather than a single royal house.

The river made concentrated government possible and limited its reach. Silt, water and boat transport sustained large towns, temple estates and royal workshops. A neglected gate could drown one district and starve another. Canal elders, boat households, grain measurers and field crews possessed knowledge that a conquering army could seize only by keeping its owners alive or persuading them to serve.

## Abandoned works, c. 2300–1900 BR

The Great Unbinding destroyed Ishkatar's imperial centre and broke several lesser Giant courts near the basin. Ancient embankments, quarry roads and burial estates lost the rulers who had commanded them. Human overseers, captives and labour households remained. Some works were abandoned because their scale exceeded any surviving community. Others continued under village councils, former estate stewards and burial fraternities.

The Basin's living river was not Ishkatar's lost Serpent's Spine. Refugees carried Ishkatari techniques, cult images and stories into the country, and scavengers brought objects from buried royal sites. Later kings exaggerated these inheritances into claims of Pharaoh-Serpent descent. The surviving human population remained Orphaned: it had endured the fall outside the Glass refuge and possessed complete human souls.

The first generations after the collapse are remembered as the **Years of Loose Water**. Local communities cut through estate walls to recover grain, burned ownership tablets and divided cattle whose Giant owners had fled or died. Rival settlements also seized one another's gates. At Tenaret, households around the middle river maintained a chain of spillways; Qemaret stood among the channels of the lower fan; Sakhret guarded cultivated terraces above the southern narrows. These names became durable because later crowns repeatedly used the three towns as seats, granaries or ritual centres.

Survival involved ordinary pleasure as well as emergency. Reed boats carried beer jars between harvest feasts. Linen workers developed bright edge patterns to distinguish town crews at shared drying grounds. Children learned flood heights through a counting song whose final verse mocked officials unable to keep their sandals dry.

## The Senareth kings, c. 1880–1580 BR

The first long crown arose at Tenaret. **Neshara Senareth**, daughter of a boat owner and a flood measurer, inherited a group of transport households during a succession of poor floods. She supplied upstream seed in exchange for labour on three abandoned channels, married the captain of a Sakhret patrol and defeated a Qemaret toll house which had been seizing grain boats. Her settlement joined enough of the middle basin to support a permanent royal household around 1840 BR.

Neshara's descendants built store courts, maintained a river fleet and assigned fields to soldiers expected to work during the flood season. They also turned public labour into royal service. Households that refused a canal summons could lose land or children to debt bondage. Prisoners from campaigns cleared silt and quarried stone. Royal monuments later described this work as the willing labour of a grateful river.

The Senareth kings established mortuary estates whose fields supported a ruler's burial cult, priests and descendants. These estates preserved workshops and irrigation over several reigns. They also removed grain and labour from ordinary councils. The dead kings did not remain in their tombs as human persons. Praise, copied court personas and predatory Masks sometimes answered in their names, while the souls of the rulers followed the human road.

Mortuary professionals studied the difference between a preserved royal presence and the departed ruler. Embalmers, estate suppliers and ruin traders compared Qahuri practice with damaged Ishkatari receiving instruments. Their experiments eventually produced the White Measure, a discipline which promised wealthy human beings a private Second House beyond death.

The dynasty weakened when two poor floods coincided with a disputed minority. Northern officials kept grain at Qemaret, southern commanders withheld boat levies and the royal cousins appointed to reconcile them hired rival troops. By about 1580 BR, the Basin had divided.

## The first Twin-Reed division, c. 1580–1412 BR

The northern **House of Qemaret** controlled the broad cultivated fan and access to the gateway roads. The southern **House of Sakhret** held the narrows, pastures and stone approaches. Each claimed to guard one reed on behalf of an eventual reunification. Both treated the other as a rebel crown.

Division encouraged local achievement. Qemaret's boatwrights built broader grain hulls. Sakhret's metalworkers improved the fittings used on flood gates and quarry sledges. Towns between the crowns bargained for repairs and remission. A boat-owning widow, **Talmera of Isethar**, carried grain for both courts during the famine of 1468 BR. Each later condemned her as a collaborator with the other. Her household kept copies of both seals on a single steering board.

The wars also brought raids, hostage-taking and executions. Royal soldiers cut embankments to force the surrender of opposing districts. The water drowned fields whose cultivators had no voice in the quarrel. Young relatives of rival houses were confined in temples and killed when negotiations failed.

**Meresha of Isethar**, the daughter of a funerary-linen household, built a fortune by financing grain during the famine of 1468 BR and taking shares in workshops unable to repay her. She used that wealth to develop the White Measure. At her death around 1439 BR, her final rite bound her complete human soul to the Court of Unspent Lamps and opened a small demiplane in the Egg White. Meresha became the earliest well-attested Second-House Patron. One of the freed scribes attached to her tomb, Hesenu, entered the same realm as a captive human soul rather than an equal companion.

**Khevar II**, a Senareth cadet raised at Sakhret, reunited most of the Basin in 1412 BR. He married a Qemaret princess, accepted northern grain officials and entered the lower town by treaty. He then executed two adult rival claimants and three adolescent members of their household after promising that the dynasty would retain its estates. The killings ended an immediate succession war and created a blood claim which later rebels repeatedly invoked.

Khevar's scribes popularised the image of the joined reeds. It became a promise that the Basin's two great regions would retain their own rites beneath one crown. Later kings used the formula even when one half of the country had ceased obeying them.

## The Yasharet house, c. 1248–1076 BR

The reunified order fragmented again after several weak reigns. Military and merchant families settled around Qemaret had grown wealthy through coastal exchange. They included descendants of older basin households, immigrants from the dry approaches and soldiers whose families had lived in the lower country for generations.

**Talzara Yasharet** became queen after her husband died during a palace struggle in 1248 BR. She secured the garrison, reopened a grain channel closed by the fighting and adopted the Twin-Reed coronation. Her family governed much of the lower and middle Basin for nearly two centuries. Their court spoke several languages, employed foreign chariot crews and married into older temple houses.

The Yasharet kings repaired canals and protected mixed households against periodic demands for expulsion. Their officers also seized estates, deported opponents and settled troops on cultivated ground. Southern temples denounced them as foreigners while accepting royal gifts. Later restoration poetry presented every person associated with Qemaret as an invader, erasing families whose basin ancestry reached back before the dynasty.

White Measure partnerships flourished in this mixed commercial world. Their members offered reliable weights, credit and burial arrangements across political boundaries. **Khaseret of Qemaret**, a ship investor and royal supplier, opened the House of the Returning Cargo at her death in 1126 BR. Her Egg White estate stored goods and route memories for living clients. During Aramen's attack, its Material cellars sheltered some Yasharet families and refused others whose ancestors had defaulted upon Khaseret's ventures.

The last Yasharet rulers drew heavily upon southern grain while fighting eastern rivals. Sakhret commanders rebelled. **Aramen of Sakhret**, who had spent his youth as a royal hostage at Qemaret and later commanded troops on the cataract road, joined the rebellion in 1081 BR. He understood the court he attacked and possessed friends within its army.

Qemaret fell in 1076 BR after part of its garrison opened a river gate. Aramen's soldiers killed members of the royal household, priests, foreign merchants and civilians sheltering in store courts. Retaliatory searches continued for months. Talzara's last known granddaughter escaped with craft families towards the gateway country. Aramen stopped the killing only after the harvest organisation began to fail.

## Restoration and empire, c. 1076–858 BR

Aramen founded the **House of Aramen** and presented his victory as the release of the southern reed. He retained Yasharet boat officials, reopened Qemaret's workshops and married the daughter of a lower-basin grain house. Those decisions restored production and angered officers who expected the conquered estates.

His successors built the Basin's widest human empire. Qahuri armies secured the southern narrows, desert mines, eastern caravan stages and selected gateway ports. River grain fed garrisons beyond the flood country. Copper, stone, captives and tribute returned to royal and temple estates. Subject soldiers became important within the army, while foreign craft households enlarged Tenaret and Qemaret.

The imperial phase brought wealth and sustained cruelty. Captured families were divided among crown, temple and officers. Rebellious towns lost walls, children as hostages and rights to their own water. Quarry gangs and military labourers died on roads praised as evidence of royal order. At home, temple festivals, medical houses and artisan villages flourished from the same revenues.

This wealth produced the White Measure's greatest necropolises. House-Builders received land, prisoners, metal and royal contracts. Their tombs anchored private demiplanes whose dead owners could continue advising descendants and leasing occult services. Some attendants entered voluntarily; others were bound through slavery, debt or deception. Many died without entering any Second House and survived only as residue or imposed funerary roles.

The best-known work community stood outside Tenaret. Its stonecutters, painters, plasterers, brewers and healers served the royal tomb estates. In 903 BR, managers of the Copper Orchard diverted grain to repair rooms collapsing within the dead physician **Tamekha Rasen's** Second House. The workers went unpaid for three months. They laid down their tools and sat before the store court with empty bread baskets. Soldiers initially beat the leaders; the stoppage spread to boat crews and forced the temple to release grain. Later kings celebrated the delivered ration and omitted both the beating and the dead Patron whose demands helped cause it.

## The Single Radiance, c. 842–814 BR

Imperial contraction strengthened rival temples and military houses. **Ithren III** inherited the middle crown in 842 BR and blamed division upon the Basin's many cults. He established a new residence at **Aseret**, closed major sanctuaries and directed public offerings towards a single celestial image called the **Radiance Above the Reeds**. Estates confiscated from older temples funded the new court.

Some worshippers welcomed the programme because it curtailed hereditary priesthoods. Provincial commanders supported a cult centred upon the king. Closed temples deprived families of burial work, food distributions and local festivals. Custodians who resisted were imprisoned or killed, images were smashed and communities were forced to carry the new emblem in procession.

Ithren courted the House-Builders' money while attacking their independent mortuary authority. Some entered royal service and placed the Radiance above their tomb images. Others concealed bodies and anchor objects beneath workshops. The apparent Second House of **Nemeret the Pure** prospered by serving both factions. Nemeret's soul had departed after a failed rite; the voice in his tomb came first from an estate-office and later from a dependent Greed Mask which learned his memories.

The chief artisan **Nehara of the Blue Hands** supervised the erasure of older images. She obeyed for nine years, protecting her household and the workers assigned to her. When ordered to destroy the small funerary figures of executed children, she concealed several behind the new sanctuary's foundation blocks. Her later confession did not restore the works already lost.

The Radiance answered the royal cult through dreams of ownership and purification. Authorially, these responses came through a Pride-shaped Mask cultivated by Ithren's system of praise. The Mask was neither the sun, a Menhir nor the dead king's soul. Ithren died in 821 BR. His heir held Aseret for seven years before **Queen Zaheret of Tenaret** reopened the temples and dispersed the court.

The restoration included its own violence. Officials of the Radiance were executed, their families lost property and mobs killed people associated with the new city. Nehara survived to identify hidden works and was later praised as though she had opposed the policy from its beginning. Aseret remained inhabited as a craft and garrison town after its royal sanctuaries were dismantled.

Restoration officials opened several White Measure tombs and found enslaved attendants, false proprietors and a small number of genuine bound human souls. Some captives were released towards the soul-road. Other necropolises avoided confiscation by financing reopened temples. The movement became more secretive without disappearing.

## Foreign kings and basin restorations, c. 800–520 BR

No later house held the whole Basin securely for long. Qemaret, Tenaret and Sakhret supported rival crowns; temple estates commanded retainers; eastern and southern rulers intervened in succession disputes. Arqeshite armies occupied the eastern approaches during the eighth century BR and installed collectors in several middle-basin districts. They repaired one neglected canal, deported skilled households and executed rebels after a failed rising.

Arqesh's fall around 690 BR allowed local claimants to return. The eastern **High-Road kings** then acquired supremacy through garrisons, tribute agreements and support for selected crowns. Their governors retained basin officials because water management could not be improvised from abroad. They also demanded grain, horses, soldiers and road labour. A ruler who fulfilled the flood rites might receive a real local response while remaining a conqueror.

The **House of Zaheret**, claiming descent from the restorer queen, held Tenaret intermittently. The **Lesser Reed kings** at Qemaret controlled delta shipping. Sakhret's commanders governed the southern narrows through marriage with pastoral and mining houses. These courts competed through temples, funerals and building as well as battle.

Foreign rule fragmented the great tomb estates. The surviving Second-House Patrons relied increasingly upon merchants, priests and displaced descendants to maintain their Material anchors. Khaseret's agents carried funerary seals towards the Gateway roads, while several kings confiscated tomb property and then bargained privately for the Patron's advice.

Qahuri merchants dealt with both Caleran and the Gateway Republic during the Gateway Wars. Basin grain reached armies on opposing sides through intermediaries. Soldiers from divided Qahuri houses served in gateway, High-Road and Caleran forces. Refugees travelled in both directions. The Basin did not participate as a single belligerent.

## Qahur and the fall of Qarzeth, c. 525–500 BR

The last Gateway war disrupted the lower routes. Families fleeing Qarzeth entered Qemaret and Tenaret; some had resisted the child collections, while others served the capital's government. Qahuri temples received rescued children and ransomed captives. Merchants also purchased people enslaved during the Atherian sack.

**Prince Tazirun Zaheret**, a younger son with little prospect of the Tenaret crown, entered Esharat's carrying trade during an earlier succession dispute and married **Lemara of Esharat** around 548 BR. Their daughter **Hesara** later helped conceal children from Qarzeth's collectors and became the mother of Hadrima. Tazirun's Qahuri dependants introduced burial songs, linen styles and coronation gestures which the later Nerethans adopted selectively.

This relationship supplied kin, ritual knowledge and prestige. Esharat and the House of Nerethan acquired no ownership of the Basin through it. Qahuri genealogists disagreed over whether a prince who settled abroad had surrendered his place in the Twin-Reed rites.

## The Basin in 350 BR

At the time of Thalion's rise, the Sacred Basin contained three principal crowns and numerous temple and canal jurisdictions. Tenaret possessed the most prestigious Twin-Reed genealogy. Qemaret controlled much lower-river shipping. Sakhret's house held the southern road and much of the army. High-Road agents collected tribute and backed rival claimants, while Caleran's annexation of Sarqen gave Atherian officials influence over coastal approaches.

The country remained wealthy enough to attract intervention and divided enough to resist simple conquest. Its rulers could assemble infantry, river craft and chariot forces, though moving them beyond the cultivated corridor required foreign wells and pack transport. Local councils could delay royal supply by disputing labour or water schedules. Temples commanded estates, medical knowledge, skilled work and popular loyalty.

The White Measure survived as a wealthy minority discipline and an initiatory secret. Meresha, Khaseret and Tamekha were believed to remain active through their Second Houses; Nemeret's more accessible tomb offered a convincing counterfeit. Ancient observers could prove that some mummies spoke and some funerary objects granted power. They could not infer the continuity behind every response.

The modern Covenant state of Al-Qahur eventually inherited these towns, canals and memories. Ancient Qahur had no knowledge of Mujalib, the Witnessed Seal or the 4,444. Its people lived within older religions and political worlds whose achievements and cruelties remained present in the ground beneath later sacred history.
