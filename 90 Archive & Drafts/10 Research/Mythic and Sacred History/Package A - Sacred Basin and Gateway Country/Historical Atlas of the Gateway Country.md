# Historical Atlas of the Gateway Country

## The country between sea and dry roads

The Gateway country occupied the approaches where the inner Thalassian Sea met cultivated coastal ground and roads leading towards Qahur and the northern wells. Its inhabitants drew food from fields, orchards, fishing grounds and herds as well as maritime exchange. Ports prospered because a populated country supplied grain, timber, rope, animals and labour between voyages.

The region produced two major political orders before Atherian unification. The **Gateway Republic**, centred on Qarzeth, grew from an association of ports and inland towns. Its wars with Caleran ended in the destruction of Qarzeth in 520 BR. The **Kingdom of Sarqen** arose among the surviving towns and later passed to Samyra Nerethan.

These societies shared a country without possessing the same constitution or memory. The Republic's capital claimed authority over communities whose inhabitants often regarded that authority as conquest. The later kings ruled from a spared harbour and could neither restore Qarzeth nor escape its inheritance.

## Shore survivors and northern migrants, c. 2165–1400 BR

Fishing villages, cultivated settlements and fortified landings survived the collapse of nearby Giant and client powers. **Esharat** developed inland around springs, grain fields and the cart routes descending to the coast. **Sarqen** began as a sheltered working harbour with repair beaches and modest stores. Farther along the coast, **Qarzeth** possessed a deeper anchorage and defensible rise above its water basins.

Households from the Near-Glass continuum reached the coast over many generations after the northern fission. Those who would later be called proto-Ahdari brought forms of guest protection, defended-well organisation and witnessed restitution. They settled among people who already understood the shore, cultivation and older local cults. Marriage, adoption, enslavement, ransom and ordinary employment enlarged the mixed population.

No single fleet founded the Gateway people. A household might speak a road dialect at home, worship at a shore shrine and owe labour to an inland town. Former captives entered ship crews and workshops. Foreign merchants remained through winter and married locally. Political identity eventually rested more upon residence and civic standing than upon a pure descent which the country had never possessed.

## Qarzeth and the port league, c. 1400–1050 BR

Qarzeth's merchants financed voyages between the gateway, island corridors and eastern coasts. Its storekeepers provided credit against future cargo, while ship-owning families hired crews from several towns. Harbour dues funded walls and dredging. Armed escorts protected convoys and sometimes became raiders when trade failed.

The city built an association with Sarqen, the island anchorage of **Tammur**, the outer port **Hazreth** and the cultivated district of **Belqara**. Agreements differed. Sarqen supplied repair labour and received access to protected convoys. Tammur bargained through its pilots. Belqara entered after defeat and owed grain at rates set in Qarzeth. Esharat retained its elders and carrying houses while paying for access to the main road.

The association survived because it offered real advantages: safer shipping, shared measures, arbitration between merchants and a force able to answer piracy. Its burdens also accumulated in the capital. Qarzeth could declare a war whose grain came from Belqara and whose dead came from dependent towns excluded from the final decision.

## The Republic, c. 1050–650 BR

By about 1050 BR, Qarzeth possessed the institutions later called the Gateway Republic. Two **Harbour Judges** conducted public business for fixed terms. The **Council of Houses** gathered established land, shipping and cult patrons. An assembly of recognised citizens voted upon war, taxation and selected appointments. Commanders held separate commissions and could build personal followings through pay, booty and client relationships.

The government rewarded argument, calculation and public display. Election feasts, boat races and household patronage formed part of political life. Successful captains hung broken oars or captured prows outside their homes. Satirical singers compared ambitious judges to gulls fighting over a fish while the tide carried the boat away.

Citizenship remained unequal. Women could own ships, estates and workshop shares, and elite women arranged credit and marriage alliances, while formal assembly power belonged mainly to recognised male householders. Enslaved people worked estates, ships, homes and warehouses. Dependent towns supplied levies and revenue under agreements they had little power to change.

The Republic expanded overseas through trading quarters, protected settlements and conquest. **Oresh**, a productive coastal district beyond the gateway, became the principal base of its military households. Local rulers retained land in exchange for troops and supplies. Gateway settlers joined existing communities; garrisons seized estates and captives elsewhere.

## The bronze-faced provider

One of Qarzeth's major sanctuaries housed the image called the **Bronze-Faced Provider**. Its priests taught that the city's greatest gifts had to be met with an irreplaceable return. The establishment sacrificed living children. In some years families offered their own; in others wealthy patrons purchased children or officials took captives and dependants. Animals served as substitutes in particular households and towns, but the capital's collectors repeatedly insisted upon human victims during war, plague or political fear.

The rite had supporters, beneficiaries and opponents. Some parents believed the death of one child preserved siblings and city. Priesthoods controlled land, workshops and distributions. Politicians displayed devotion through offerings. Other families hid children, bribed collectors, sent them to relatives or fled. Temple servants carried accounts of the killings to hostile towns.

The Provider was a dependent Gluttony Mask whose local presence fed upon the civic conviction that safety required consuming what could not be replaced. It offered visions, coincidences and periods of apparent plenty. Human priests and magistrates chose the collections, guarded the victims and killed them. The Mask's influence neither removes their responsibility nor makes every inhabitant complicit.

## The First Gateway War, c. 650–625 BR

The war began at Tammur after a quarrel between its Gateway garrison and local households. One faction expelled the garrison and appealed to an Atherian port; another asked Qarzeth to restore the old arrangement. Both republics sent forces in the language of protection.

Harbour Judge **Qelmar Suthar** organised Qarzeth's response. He expected a short expedition supported by established dues. Caleran's **Aster Vey**, a senator from a ship-financing family, assembled hulls from several Atherian cities and imposed new service on allied ports. Neither side possessed enough experienced crews at the outset. Storms, disease and wrecks killed heavily while carpenters and rowers learned through failure.

The conflict spread from Tammur to anchorages, supply fields and the approaches to Qarzeth. Gateway raiders burned Atherian stores. Caleran seized island harbours and sold captives. Qelmar delayed one payment to dependent troops in order to repair the fleet, beginning the grievance that later consumed the Republic.

Caleran won after a Gateway relief fleet broke apart in a storm and Atherian squadrons blocked the surviving ships from Qarzeth. The settlement stripped the Republic of Tammur, restricted its fleet and imposed a large payment. Aster returned to public honours. Allied Atherian towns inherited ship levies and debts which the victory did not discharge.

## The Rope Camp revolt, c. 625–619 BR

Qarzeth summoned its returning army to the **Rope Camp** outside the city and offered partial payment. Paid companies expected their contracts fulfilled. Local levies wanted release. Belqara cultivators refused new collections imposed to meet the indemnity. **Damen of the Rope Camp**, an experienced foreign captain, and **Yasra of Belqara**, a landholder whose brothers had died in the first war, became the most influential rebel leaders.

Council rivals disrupted negotiations and seized a rebel delegation. Damen's followers tortured captured officials and killed men who urged compromise. Government commanders cut food to the camp and executed prisoners along the road. Yasra tried to protect the rural coalition from the soldiers' reprisals; she later authorised the killing of loyalist households accused of hiding grain.

**Meleq Ordan**, a commander from a modest Qarzeth shipping house, restored the government through alternating truce and betrayal. He secured cavalry and grain from **King Harzun of Hadrath**, an inland border kingdom, and married Harzun's daughter **Sapha**. Meleq separated several local contingents with payment, trapped Damen's remaining army and starved the Rope Camp. Damen was killed. Yasra was captured and executed after refusing to identify Belqara families which had supported her.

The revolt left farms burned, prisoners mutilated and dependants sold by both sides. Meleq also cancelled part of Belqara's arrears and rebuilt its water stores because Qarzeth required the next harvest. His victory made the Ordan household indispensable and gave Hadrath land and marriage claims along the inland border.

## Oresh and the Ordan commanders, c. 619–600 BR

The Council sent Meleq to Oresh with authority to obtain revenue, secure metals and recruit troops. He negotiated with local rulers, conquered resistant districts and settled veterans near the principal harbour. Oresh prospered under protected shipping while losing land and political freedom.

Meleq and Sapha raised their children between Qarzeth, Hadrath and Oresh. Their daughter **Zamaret Ordan** learned the languages used among their troops and suppliers. She inherited command after the deaths of two brothers and won the Council's approval by delivering revenue when other commanders failed.

The Ordan following contained Qarzeth citizens, Hadrathi riders, Oreshi infantry, paid companies, subject levies and allied contingents. Their obligations differed. Zamaret could promise release from Caleran dues to Atherian allies; she could not assume that every soldier wanted Qarzeth to rule the resulting country.

## The alliance-breaking invasion, c. 600–580 BR

Zamaret's war aimed to dissolve Caleran's alliance system. Her army crossed the upland approaches from an allied landing, carrying grain by ship and pack animal until it could use successive harvests. She offered lighter obligations to towns willing to defect and returned captured local leaders who accepted her terms.

The invasion destroyed several Atherian armies. At the **Red Fords**, Zamaret drew a larger force into broken ground and cut its retreat to water. Prisoners were ransomed, recruited or sold. Communities which resisted lost stores and inhabitants. Several allies abandoned Caleran; others feared their local rivals or distrusted promises dependent upon a distant victory.

The Atherian commander **Celor Marath** refused the decisive battle his opponents demanded. He protected the core army, burned forage ahead of Zamaret and allowed exposed allied districts to suffer. His strategy preserved Caleran and ruined households whose protection had justified the alliance.

Meanwhile **Dema Varin** attacked Oresh. She took local allies, exploited resentment against Ordan settlements and cut the revenue sustaining Zamaret's army. A Caleran expedition then crossed to the Gateway homeland with support from Hadrath's new king, who sought territory promised to his mother Sapha's rivals. Zamaret returned too late to prevent defeat.

The peace restricted Qarzeth's fleet and foreign campaigns. Zamaret later became a Harbour Judge and attacked the use of public credit by a small group of houses. Her reforms recovered money and created powerful enemies. She died in exile at an eastern court around 566 BR, refusing a Caleran demand for surrender.

## Recovery and the child collections, c. 580–525 BR

The Republic repaired its agriculture and trade while paying the settlement. Esharat carriers and Sarqen shipyards benefited from routes diverted away from restricted military ports. Qarzeth remained rich enough to alarm Caleran. Hadrathi rulers encroached on border farms under Atherian protection.

Military defeat increased the Provider's influence. Priests argued that Qarzeth had survived because the city had paid what safety required; every later danger proved that the payment had been insufficient. The **Years of Three Collections**, c. 548–540 BR, extended demands into dependent towns.

**Ilya of Qarzeth**, a sanctuary storekeeper, fled with a list of children held for the third collection. **Sapha of Esharat**, a midwife unrelated to the earlier Hadrathi princess, organised households which moved threatened children through grain carts and dry channels. A collector's force killed her husband and seized her eldest daughter. The daughter died at Qarzeth before the rescue network could reach her.

The opposition remained politically divided. Some wanted the sanctuary purified and the Republic preserved. Some dependent towns wanted independence. Exiles asked Caleran to destroy the city. Their testimony established the reality of the killings and showed where prisoners were held.

## The final war and the destruction of Qarzeth, c. 525–520 BR

The Caleran senator **Orsen Valeor** made the end of the child collections the central argument of his old age. He spoke beside escaped families and sanctuary workers. He also believed Qarzeth's recovery would eventually threaten every Atherian alliance. Merchants, land-seekers and ambitious commanders supported the same policy for their own purposes. Senator **Mera Castren** opposed destruction and advocated inspection, disarmament and permanent hostages.

Qarzeth surrendered weapons and selected hostages. Caleran then demanded that the people abandon the city and rebuild inland. Opponents of sacrifice would have lost their homes alongside the priests and patrons they had resisted. The assembly chose war.

The siege lasted several campaigning seasons. Belqara and Esharat supplied Atherian forces under agreements promising survival. Sarqen admitted refugees and repaired allied vessels. Within Qarzeth, inhabitants made weapons in homes and dismantled buildings for timber. The priesthood continued killings during the siege, while some citizens attacked the sanctuary and tried to release prisoners.

A breach in 520 BR led to fighting through inhabited streets. Atherian detachments opened the holding chambers and recovered children. Other soldiers murdered residents, raped captives and sold survivors. Commanders permitted looting and mass enslavement. Captured priests and officials were executed.

Caleran burned and razed the central city. Salt brought through the harbour was spread over the sanctuary enclosure, elite gardens, public squares and selected residential ground. Cisterns were spoiled and waterworks broken. The policy required organised transport and labour; it was a deliberate exclusion of Qarzeth from the country's future.

The Gluttony Mask lost its sanctuary, public offerings and civic protection. Its Throne did not die. Fragments of the cult survived among exiles and fraudulent claimants, none of whom possessed the old Republic's authority.

## The spared towns, c. 520–457 BR

Esharat, Sarqen, Belqara and several minor ports survived. Their reasons differed. Some had opposed the collections, some surrendered early and some supplied the besiegers. Caleran also needed their grain and harbours. Survival brought garrisons, reparations and confiscations rather than freedom from all consequence.

Refugees returned to occupied houses. Freed captives discovered that their fields had new owners. Former Qarzeth officials lived beside families whose children they had failed to protect. Harbour Judges in two towns retained municipal duties, while the old Council of Houses ceased to govern the country.

An alliance of spared towns managed food, refugees and damaged routes under Atherian supervision. Sarqen grew because its harbour could be enlarged without rebuilding Qarzeth. Esharat supplied cultivated produce and carrying families. The old capital remained a salted ruin whose stones were prohibited in public buildings.

Nerethan of Delkos arrived as harbour commander in 499 BR and married Hadrima of Esharat in 497. Her mother Hesara was the daughter of Lemara of Esharat and the junior Qahuri prince Tazirun Zaheret. Hadrima brought land, credit, carriers and basin ritual knowledge. She defended several recovered holdings against assignment to Nerethan's officers while accepting compulsory labour for harbour repair.

Their son Arenes obtained hereditary recognition in 457 BR through local support and Atherian patronage. The crown emerged because the town alliance required sustained defence and because a military household learned to make its office inheritable.

## The Kingdom of Sarqen, 457–350 BR

Arenes and his successors governed through the port, local town leaders and negotiated access for Atherian forces. Royal festivals commemorated rescued children and the towns which had sheltered them. Families of people killed or enslaved by Caleran used the same occasions to read their dead.

The dynasty promised that the collections would never return. It used that promise to legitimise monarchy. Critics observed that the old Republic's crimes had become royal property in the form of confiscated land, ceremonies and claims to protect every surviving town.

By Samyra's birth in 392 BR, Sarqen possessed a royal court, working fleet, grain country and several languages of government. Her claim to Qahuri descent ran through a junior maternal line and supplied ceremonial prestige. She was queen of the Gateway country, not of Qahur.

Samyra's independent rule ended with Thalion's conquest in 350 BR. Sarqen survived as a working port. Qarzeth remained uninhabited at its centre, surrounded by villages and fields whose people refused the suggestion that the whole country had died with the city.
