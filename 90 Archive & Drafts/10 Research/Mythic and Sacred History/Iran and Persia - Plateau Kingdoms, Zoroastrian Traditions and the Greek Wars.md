# Iran and Persia — Plateau Kingdoms, Zoroastrian Traditions and the Greek Wars

## Scope and historical vocabulary

Iranian history joins plateau, mountain, steppe, river and lowland societies. The word **Iranian** properly includes a broad family of peoples and languages. **Persian** refers more particularly to the people and political traditions associated with Persis or Parsa and the empires that grew from it. The Achaemenid Empire was ruled by a Persian dynasty and inhabited by many peoples who did not become Persian simply by paying its taxes or serving its armies.

Elam, Babylon and Media belong to the history from which Persian imperial power emerged. They are not successive ethnic versions of Persia. Elam possessed a much older and linguistically distinct civilisation in south-western Iran. Babylon developed within Mesopotamia. The Medes and Persians were related Iranian-speaking peoples whose political histories overlapped. Achaemenid government inherited personnel, writing practices, royal forms and local institutions from all of them.

This is the most useful starting point for the World Egg east. Arqesh may carry Assyrian and Babylonian grammars of siege, deportation, canal wealth and cultural endurance. A later upland dynasty can carry Persian grammars of coalition, restoration, roads and many-peopled rule. Treating the two as separate empires gives the eastern world political succession without turning every civilisation into another phase of one evil kingdom.

## Before Persia: Elam, Mesopotamia and the plateau

### Elam and the south-western highlands

Elamite history extended across millennia, with important centres at Susa and in the highland country of Anshan. Political unity varied. Lowland and upland rulers controlled different territories, entered Mesopotamian wars and adopted titles that expressed authority over several regions. Elamite language remained distinct from the Semitic and Indo-European languages around it.

Elam fought, traded and exchanged religious and artistic forms with Mesopotamian states. Its rulers carried captured monuments home, while Mesopotamian kings invaded Elamite territory. Assyria's seventh-century campaigns culminated in the sack of Susa and the public claim that Elam had been annihilated. That claim was imperial boasting. Elamite communities, administrators, cults and language continued and became part of the environment in which Persian rule developed.

Persian kings first rose in Anshan, an old Elamite political landscape. Their administration used Elamite scribes and documents alongside Old Persian, Akkadian and Aramaic. Persepolis tablets reveal a working imperial economy in which rations, journeys, animals, labour and several languages had to be coordinated. The relationship is inheritance and transformation rather than replacement of a vanished people.

### Babylon and Assyria

Babylonia preserved one of the ancient world's most prestigious traditions of kingship, scholarship and temple government. Assyria conquered it repeatedly and could rule it only by engaging with Babylonian institutions. Babylonian and Median forces then helped destroy Assyria at the end of the seventh century BCE. The Neo-Babylonian kings inherited an empire, rebuilt monumental Babylon and deported rebellious populations, including Judeans.

Cyrus entered Babylon in 539 BCE and adopted Babylonian royal language. The Cyrus Cylinder describes the conquest as the restoration of proper worship and return of divine images after the failures of Nabonidus. It is an important document of imperial legitimation. Calling it the first declaration of human rights removes it from that political setting and obscures the king's interest in presenting conquest as lawful restoration.

World Egg should preserve this double character. A successor king may reopen sanctuaries, permit displaced people to return and protect local languages because those acts repair genuine wrongs and secure the new empire. The same king takes tribute, appoints governors, demands soldiers and kills rebels. Beneficial policy does not need to be a fraud, and it does not make imperial power innocent.

### Iranian-speaking peoples and Media

Iranian-speaking groups entered and developed across the plateau and Central Asia over a long period. Assyrian records mention Medes and Persians among many highland peoples. Median political organisation remains difficult to reconstruct because later Greek narratives and Achaemenid interests shape much of the surviving account. It may have consisted of powerful houses and regional coalitions rather than the tightly centralised empire later historians imagined.

Cyrus's overthrow of Astyages did not erase Median elites. Medes held high office under the Achaemenids and appeared beside Persians in imperial representation. Kinship, marriage, household allegiance and shared aristocratic practice helped the new dynasty absorb the former ruling world.

This pattern suits a World Egg transition after Arqesh. The conquering house need not destroy every provincial dynasty. It can win by attracting governors, upland rulers, Tarkhan circuits and subject cities that no longer trust the Arqeshite throne. Former enemies then occupy the new court together and dispute whose ancestors made victory possible.

## The Achaemenid achievement

### Cyrus and the making of an empire

Cyrus II united Persian and Median power, conquered Lydia in western Anatolia and took Babylon. His realm stretched across societies with different legal, religious and political traditions. Local elites often continued under imperial supervision. Military victory remained the foundation: armies were defeated, rulers deposed and wealth transferred. Accommodation made conquest durable.

Stories about Cyrus multiplied. Herodotus gives a royal infant exposed because of a dream and raised away from court before overthrowing his grandfather. Xenophon's *Cyropaedia* presents an idealised ruler. Babylonian documents use their own political theology. These accounts preserve the attraction of Cyrus as a just conqueror while revealing the needs of authors writing for different audiences.

Cambyses conquered Egypt. Ancient Greek and Egyptian traditions often portray him as mad or sacrilegious, although many details are hostile and disputed. Darius seized power after a succession crisis and represented his enemies as liars. The Behistun inscription records rebellions across the empire and the violent restoration of royal authority. Captured leaders were mutilated and executed. Darius's language of truth and order served a real moral world and a conqueror's justification of his own rule.

### Satrapies, roads and royal government

The empire governed through provinces conventionally called satrapies. Their boundaries and arrangements changed. Royal appointees worked with local rulers, temple institutions, city governments and military commanders. Provinces provided tribute, taxes, soldiers, animals and supplies. Rebellions could bring sieges, executions and the replacement of elites.

Roads connected courts, provincial centres and staging posts. Royal messengers and officials could travel with authorisations, animals and provisions. Merchants and travellers also used parts of the network, although the famous Royal Road was not a public service equally available to everyone. Maritime routes joined Phoenicia, Egypt, Cyprus, Anatolia and the Aegean. Imperial power moved through food, horses, ship crews, interpreters and local knowledge as much as through decrees.

The court itself travelled among centres such as Susa, Babylon, Ecbatana and Persepolis. Royal women owned estates, managed resources and acted within dynastic politics. Great houses retained regional power. The king of kings stood at the centre of a hierarchy whose unity had to be recreated through audiences, gifts, marriages, ceremonies, images and campaigns.

A World Egg successor empire should possess similar material depth. A road is maintained by villages owing labour, remount herders, bridge craftsmen and protected way stations. Return decrees require grain, timber and guards. One governor can honour local law while another extorts the same province. The empire's achievement is intelligible because the costs are visible.

### Languages, art and plural rule

Achaemenid inscriptions and administration used several languages. Aramaic served widely, while Babylonian, Elamite, Egyptian and other local traditions continued. Imperial art brought peoples into an ordered royal image: delegations carry gifts, guards wear varied dress and the king appears under divine favour. This was pluralism arranged around hierarchy.

Local worship often continued. Persian kings endowed or consulted important institutions while intervening when politics required it. Accommodation could express genuine respect, inherited practice and practical government at once. It did not amount to modern religious equality. Revolt, disloyalty or disputed succession could transform a protected sanctuary into a target.

This is valuable for the eastern empire preceding Thalion. Jirahar returns can occur within a wider policy that restores several subject sanctuaries. Arqeshite scribes can remain employed. Tarkhan protectors can receive pasture. Qahuri houses can send grain and royal spouses. Each group preserves a different memory of the same king.

## Zoroaster and the Zoroastrian traditions

### Sources and chronology

Zoroaster or Zarathustra is the prophetic figure associated with the oldest Zoroastrian compositions. His date and homeland remain disputed. The Gathas, hymns preserved within the Avesta, use an archaic Iranian language and present a religious world of pastoral communities, patrons, ritual specialists and conflict over truth and worship. Later Avestan texts, Middle Persian literature, inscriptions and living traditions belong to different centuries and political settings.

The Avesta did not survive intact. Sasanian and later accounts remember larger collections, while the extant text was transmitted through priestly communities and later written manuscripts. Modern summaries frequently compress early Avestan thought, Achaemenid royal religion, Sasanian institutions and contemporary practice into one timeless system.

The religion of the early Achaemenid kings remains debated. Darius and his successors invoke Ahura Mazda and speak of truth, order and the Lie. Their inscriptions do not reproduce the later Avesta in full, and scholarly disagreement continues over when the dynasty should be called Zoroastrian. A World Egg comparison should preserve the attraction of the connection without claiming certainty that the evidence does not support.

### Truth, creation and moral choice

The opposition between *asha*—truth, right order and proper reality—and *druj*, the Lie, stands near the centre of Zoroastrian moral thought. Human beings choose through thought, word and deed. Later tradition gives fuller form to the conflict between Ohrmazd and Ahriman and to the spiritual powers associated with creation, virtue and destruction. Eschatological traditions include judgement, punishment, resurrection and the final renovation of creation.

These ideas cannot be imported into World Egg cosmology as a second ultimate revelation. The Lord alone is uncreated. Idols are guilty created sovereignties, not an evil principle equal to Him. Menhir express parts of the created order and are not divine virtues. Saints are redeemed human persons under commission.

The useful translation is cultural and moral. An eastern school may describe justice as walking in the true grain of creation and tyranny as making one's subjects inhabit a lie. Royal proclamations can use that language sincerely and self-servingly. An Idol Mask of Pride or Envy can exploit the belief that visible success proves possession of truth. The religion's insistence upon choice gives human resistance meaning without creating metaphysical dualism.

### Fire, water and purity

Fire occupies an honoured place in Zoroastrian worship as a visible focus of purity and truth. It is tended rather than worshipped as a god. Water also receives reverence, and purity practices govern contact with substances understood to carry pollution. Funerary customs developed to avoid contaminating earth and fire with the corpse; exposure in structures later known as towers of silence belongs to particular historical forms and should not be projected unchanged into every period.

World Egg must keep ritual fire separate from Wrath, Phoenix manifestation and the Elemental Shell. A tended flame remains created matter used in human worship. It can be extinguished, polluted or stolen without becoming a supernatural person. If a true angelic or Menhir event occurs at one fire, the event validates only its particular commission or relationship.

Purity law offers narrative material with real human consequences. Care for water and corpses can protect communities from contamination. Priestly estates can also use pollution categories to exclude workers, foreigners or rival funerary specialists. A reforming king who protects sacred fires may still burn a rebellious town.

### Priests, households and political change

Zoroastrian institutions changed under successive empires. Priestly families preserved liturgy, law and interpretation. Sasanian kings later gave the religion a different public and institutional setting from that of the early Achaemenids. Royal patronage could strengthen communities and intensify disputes over orthodoxy. Zoroastrians also lived under foreign and later Islamic government, carrying ritual through households and local priesthoods after imperial support ended.

For World Egg, this offers a history in which a truth-centred eastern religion predates the empire that later patronises it. Court theologians then claim that the dynasty has always defended the faith. Local fire houses remember earlier kings, compromises and persecutions which contradict that clean account.

## Persia and the Greek world

### Ionian cities and the first wars

The Greek–Persian wars began within a connected Aegean and Anatolian world. Cyrus's conquest of Lydia brought the Greek cities of western Anatolia under Persian rule. These cities retained local institutions under tyrants, satraps and changing arrangements. Their inhabitants were not external spectators to an encounter between two sealed civilisations.

The Ionian Revolt began in 499 BCE through local political failure, resistance and assistance from Athens and Eretria. Persia suppressed it and destroyed Miletus after siege. Darius's later expedition ended in defeat at Marathon in 490. The battle mattered greatly to Athenian memory but did not seriously threaten the survival of the Persian Empire.

Xerxes assembled a vast multiethnic army and fleet for the invasion of 480. Ancient figures for its size are inflated and must be treated cautiously. Thermopylae became famous for the Spartan-led defence and its destruction; the same campaign included other Greek forces, Persian subjects and political divisions among the Greek cities. Athens was evacuated and burned. The naval battle at Salamis damaged Persian operations, while Plataea and Mycale in 479 ended the immediate invasion.

These wars contain courage, coercion and competing loyalties on every side. Some Greek cities fought Persia, others submitted, and Greek-speaking troops served the empire. Persian commanders worked through Phoenician, Egyptian, Cypriot and Ionian fleets. Later celebration of free Greece against enslaved Asia oversimplified oligarchy, slavery and imperial ambition within the Greek states themselves.

### Athens, Sparta and Persian diplomacy

The end of Xerxes' invasion did not remove Persia from Greek politics. Athens built a maritime league and transformed tribute and allied ships into its own empire. Sparta led a rival alliance. Their long conflict culminated in the Peloponnesian War, during which Persian satraps funded fleets, negotiated with Greek factions and pursued the recovery of Anatolian cities.

Sparta won with substantial Persian support and later campaigned against Persian authority in Anatolia. Greek mercenaries served rival Persian claimants, most famously in the expedition remembered by Xenophon after Cyrus the Younger's failed rebellion. The King's Peace of 387/386 used Persian authority to impose a settlement among the Greek states while recognising the empire's control in Asia Minor.

The historical lesson is stronger than an East–West war. Persian rulers could lose a naval campaign and later dominate Greek diplomacy. Greek champions of freedom could compel tribute from allies and sell captives. Border cities changed sides according to local factions and survival.

World Egg should therefore give Atherian leagues and the eastern successor empire several wars and long intervals of diplomacy. A coastal Atherian city may revolt against its eastern governor, call for help and discover that its liberators want naval taxes. Tarkhan cavalry can serve both sides in different generations. One Persian-shaped king can restore Qiryath and later destroy an Atherian-backed rebel city without contradiction.

## Alexander, conquest and mixed succession

Macedonian power grew within the Greek political world and under Philip II defeated a coalition of Greek states. Alexander invaded the Achaemenid Empire in 334 BCE. Victories at the Granicus, Issus and Gaugamela broke successive royal armies. Darius III was killed during the collapse by men within his own following. Persian resistance continued in different regions after the great battles.

Alexander's forces looted and burned Persepolis. Ancient accounts disagree over motive and staging, but the destruction was deliberate. The conquest killed, enslaved and displaced people while transferring enormous wealth. Alexander also retained Persian officials, adopted elements of court ceremony, married Roxane of Bactria, arranged marriages between officers and Iranian women and sought service from Iranian nobles. These policies produced anger among some Macedonians and no simple reconciliation among the conquered.

Bactrian and Sogdian resistance forced difficult campaigns. After Alexander's death, his commanders fought over the empire. The Seleucid kingdom preserved parts of Achaemenid territorial government within a Greek-speaking royal order. Local dynasties and mixed cities developed between imperial centres.

The Parthian Arsacid dynasty later built a durable Iranian empire whose great houses, cavalry and regional kings resisted Roman expansion. Rome and Parthia fought over Armenia, Mesopotamia and royal succession without either permanently conquering the other. The Sasanian dynasty eventually replaced the Arsacids and gave kingship and Zoroastrian institutions new forms.

This longer history prevents a World Egg eastern civilisation from ending when Thalion arrives. Atherian conquest can destroy its reigning dynasty and still inherit provincial houses, roads, religions, soldiers and epic memory. Eastern aristocrats can become Atherian commanders without ceasing to care about the country conquered by their emperor.

## Iranian epic and legendary history

### From Avestan figures to the *Shahnameh*

Iranian traditional history formed through many layers. Avestan texts preserve rulers and heroes connected with cosmic history and religious struggle. Middle Persian compilations arranged royal sequences. Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh*, completed around the beginning of the eleventh century CE, gave monumental poetic form to the history from the first kings through the Arab conquest. It preserves older material through the literary and political world of its own age.

The Achaemenids occupy little space in this traditional sequence. Memories of ancient eastern and Arsacid houses entered the epic world in transformed form. Later performance, prose epics and folktales further changed the stories. The tradition therefore offers a precise lesson for World Egg historiography: an epic can carry genuine ancient names, regional houses and moral wounds while rearranging their dates and political identities almost beyond recovery.

### Jamshid, Zahhak and royal glory

Jamshid begins as a great civilising king associated with prosperity, crafts and the enlargement of the world. Pride leads him to claim the achievements as his own, and royal glory departs. Zahhak then becomes a monstrous tyrant. In the later familiar form, serpents grow from his shoulders and demand human brains. The blacksmith Kaveh raises his leather apron as a banner, and Fereydun overthrows the tyrant. Zahhak is bound rather than simply killed and remains a feared future danger.

The sequence gives World Egg several separate grammars. A ruler's visible splendour may depend upon just relationships and collapse through pride. An Idol Mask can imitate that splendour after the ruler has lost it. A human tyrant can feed an Archmask through regular child or captive sacrifice without becoming the Idol itself. A craft apron or workshop tool can become a national symbol because ordinary people carried it into revolt.

The *farr* or royal glory must remain culturally interpreted. It cannot prove grace, rightful blood or metaphysical election. It may describe reputation, a Menhir correspondence, an inherited court sign, a Patron bargain or an Idol counterfeit in different cases.

### Fereydun's sons and the war of Iran and Turan

Fereydun divides the world among his sons. Jealous brothers murder Iraj, and later descendants pursue vengeance. Iranian traditional history develops wars between Iran and Turan from this divided family. In its older form, Turanians are not simply Turks; both sides belong to Iranian legendary genealogy. Later history and storytelling altered the identification.

The cycle contains marriages across the frontier, divided loyalties and children belonging to both worlds. A national epic can therefore remember the enemy as kin while sustaining generations of blood feud. This is valuable for the Tarkhan and eastern riverlands, where mobile and settled houses should share ancestors, spouses and heroic songs despite later political labels.

### Zal, Simurgh and Rostam

Zal is born with white hair and abandoned by his father, who fears shame and demonic influence. The Simurgh raises him on a mountain, later returns him to his repentant father and gives him a feather through which aid can be sought. The bird's help assists the difficult birth of Rostam and later heals Rostam and his horse. Its knowledge does not prevent tragedy; aid in the final conflict with Esfandiyar carries a foreknown cost.

Rostam stands partly outside the royal court as the champion of a great regional house. He repeatedly rescues foolish or endangered kings. His strength does not free him from guilt. He kills his unknown son Sohrab in single combat and recognises him too late. He later kills the prince Esfandiyar after a conflict involving royal demand, honour and foreknowledge. Victory leaves an irreversible wound.

The World Egg can divide these functions. A Menhir court person may foster an abandoned eastern child without becoming a benevolent goddess or universal patron. A renowned champion may belong to an upland house whose military power repeatedly saves a king it also resents. A father–son killing should retain ignorance, recognition and consequence if used; substituting a paperwork error would destroy the story.

### Siyavash, ordeal and unjust death

Siyavash refuses the advances of his stepmother and is falsely accused. He passes through fire to demonstrate innocence, later enters the enemy country and is killed despite his blamelessness. His death produces grief, vengeance and continuing political consequence. The story joins sexual coercion, ordeal, exile, hospitality and the inability of innocence to guarantee survival.

A World Egg translation must treat the ordeal carefully. Passing through fire may be a received epic wonder, an actual Menhir relationship or a later ritual elaboration. It cannot prove a universal procedure for detecting truth. The false accusation and political murder remain central regardless of whether the fire miracle occurred.

### Performance and popular variation

Epic stories were recited, illustrated, reshaped and expanded beyond one authoritative book. Later storytellers combined episodes and introduced additional heroes. The killing of Sohrab became a particularly dramatic performance. Regional houses could preserve versions which gave their ancestors a larger place.

This fits the World Egg eastern world better than a single sacred chronicle. Arqeshite court editors, Jirahar exiles, Tarkhan singers and Atherian translators can all preserve related stories. Each may move the hero to its own border and alter the ruler he served. The shared moral wound survives even when the chronology does not.

## World Egg applications

### Arqesh and the successor empire

Arqesh should remain the empire of siege, canal control, deportation and royal terror already established in research. Its fall around 690 BR creates space for an upland and river coalition. The leading Persian-shaped successor can:

- recruit former Arqeshite governors while killing those who resist;
- secure Tarkhan and highland alliances through marriage and pasture guarantees;
- enter the old capital under a restoration claim;
- permit displaced peoples, including Jirahar households, to return;
- retain Arqeshite scribes, canals and provincial divisions;
- build or restore a road and relay system;
- demand tribute and troops from autonomous regions;
- suppress revolts with exemplary violence;
- fight Atherian maritime leagues without reducing the war to civilisation against barbarism.

The dynasty should arise from a real older eastern people rather than from Arqeshite exiles alone. Its country needs upland estates, horse breeding, cultivated valleys, fortress routes and at least one older religious centre. This missing regional shelf should be developed before canon promotion.

### Jirahar restoration

The successor ruler's restoration of Qiryath can be sincere, strategic and incomplete. Timber, money, guards and returned cult property make rebuilding possible. In exchange, the new government receives road cooperation and a loyal provincial counterweight. The decree does not gather all Jirahar or settle their internal disputes.

One strand of Jirahar memory may praise the ruler as the Anointed Stranger. Another may remember relatives executed when an eastern governor later crushed a revolt. The Lord can use a foreign king providentially without giving him the Prophet's office or approving his entire empire.

### The Atherian–eastern wars

The Persian–Greek relationship suggests a long sequence rather than one invasion:

1. eastern authority inherits Atherian-speaking or allied coastal cities;
2. a local revolt draws aid from maritime leagues;
3. eastern retaliation destroys one city and attempts to discipline the supporting leagues;
4. an invasion wins land battles but fails after naval loss and supply strain;
5. Atherian leagues turn defensive success into tribute and coercive maritime power;
6. the eastern court funds rival Atherian cities and recovers influence through diplomacy;
7. Atherian mercenaries enter an eastern succession war;
8. Thalion later conquers an eastern world already entangled with Atherian households.

Each phase can involve different rulers and generations. No battle needs to decide the moral worth or permanent superiority of either civilisation.

### Iranian material beyond the Jirahar

Iranian epic grammar belongs especially to the Tarkhan, upland eastern houses and Arqeshite successor world. Jirahar authors may preserve minority versions, but their sacred history should not absorb the complete epic. The royal-glory tradition can also inform later Atherian interpretation of eastern kings, while Zoroastrian-shaped truth language can survive inside an Atherian province long after its dynasty falls.

## Comparative boundaries

1. Elam, Babylon, Media and Persia remain distinct societies and political inheritances.
2. Arqesh is not Persia. Its successor carries the main Persian imperial comparison.
3. Persian accommodation and restoration are genuine policies pursued within a conquering, taxing and sometimes brutal empire.
4. Zoroastrianism is not reduced to fire worship and supplies no co-eternal rival to the Lord.
5. Ritual fire is not Wrath, a Phoenix, a Menhir or a Shell person.
6. *Asha*, *druj* and *farr* can shape human theology and political language without establishing World Egg metaphysics.
7. Atherian–eastern wars involve border cities, shifting coalitions and diplomacy. They are not a racial war between free West and servile East.
8. Iranian legendary persons remain source figures. Their World Egg descendants require separate biographies, cultures and consequences.
9. No dragon, serpent tyrant, sacred bird or fiery ordeal automatically receives a supernatural identity from resemblance alone.
