# Causality Pass — 10F Bending the Road to 1360

> [!note] Author-only record
> The whole-history pass of [[Causality Pass - 09 The Road to 1360]] kept its anchors, so several sequences on the road to 1360 still followed their real analogues closely ([[Real-History Concordance#Sequences still close to their analogues]]). On the creator's instruction (14 November 2026) this file bends six of them under the brief and standing rules of [[Causality Pass - Direction]] and the Forward Rule of [[The Divergence Pass — Torque Register]]: the Twin Cardinals, Alessior VI (renumbered **Alessior II**), the Reformation, the Fiorenzian sequence, Meshalem and Qarayel's burial. Changes to files this writer owns are already made; changes to shared files are in [[#Integration patch]]. Index: [[Causality Pass - 00 Proposal Index]].

## Summary

The road from the Sweat to the Accord now runs on the world's own powers. A cardinal died unsung on his election night and his twin reigned in his ring. A bought Primarch was an ascetic who believed the Chancery's seal did the sending, and was seen walking under his own dome of names. Ansgar's Grievances were sung over drowned miners and spread as a funeral strike; the synod put his book, not his body, to the fire, the book would not burn, and a spring flood took him from a bridge-tower two authorities would not take responsibility for. His followers swore in the river and fought out of the mines. The reformers' order was written by the Sanctum's own commissioned prosecutor, who refused the reforming duke, and the old Church's council answered the book with its author sitting in the hall. The counter-reform order was an outlaw brotherhood of night-singers. In Fiorenzia the bank lit the pyre against the friar's warning and fled with the city's dead, the friar never ruled and died in a mask, the city bought its dead back with a dukedom, and the advice-book was written by a bondsman in a debtor's coat. A Jirahar claimant twice turned back from the gate. A khan's stallion carried him out of the camp, and the herds came home without him.

Every outcome the present depends on is kept: Eclessia's three-way split; the Freeflame and *The Order of the Open Flame*; the Order of the Lantern; the Council's abolition, Arrears, seminaries and five tests (1352); the Accord's date (1343), formula, terms, river oath and Unsworn; the Unburnt Psalter in the Sanctum's vault; the Preacher's Water; the Medori's reach and registers; Severin, his four years and the Pyre of Masks (1346); Vetti and the *Mirror*; the four Hordes and the Moon Horde's prohibition; the Jirahar Keepers' presumption against claimants. All victims and atrocities of 09 are kept, and four are added (the Saint-Leon mother, the Brenholt chaplains, the drowned drifts, the drowned mask-makers).

## Constraints honoured

- **Fixed anchors.** No 1360 ruler, state, faith or Locked Ruling changes. Present-Day Courts and Crowns keeps every ruler; only its *How it came to this* paragraphs change.
- **Weirds.** The Quiet Ninth and the Held Breath are untouched. Qarayel's Grave keeps its weird status with new, non-Genghis details and no explanation.
- **Held proofs.** No ninth proof is added. The Psalter's test at Aldersbruck is voided, so the proof stays unopened. Meshalem never touches the gate, so the Empty City's gate stays untested. Ferro's unwritten refutation and Severin's mask are recorded as pressures, not proofs.
- **Grenzburg and playable settings.** See [[#Grenzburg and playable-settings check]]: nothing they use has changed.

## 1. The Twin Cardinals (1240–1242)

**Old sequence (the Western Schism).** Plague-thinned conclaves elected rival heads who issued incompatible burial and succession rules until a third, Gelasin II, was accepted by both. A reader who knows 1378–1417 predicts rival lines, rival cities and a council to end them.

**New sequence and causes.**
1. *1240.* Fourteen surviving cardinals elect **Aimery of Corvel**. He sickens that evening and dies before dawn, before his first rite.
2. Under the Sweat's quarantine rule a Sanctum whose head died unsent is unclean, and the electors would be sealed in with the fever. His twin, **Cardinal Aubert of Corvel**, buries him in the conclave garden without a rite, puts on his ring and comes out to the loggia as Aimery.
3. *1241.* Aubert's **Ferry Rule**: any baptised person who knows the names of the dead may sing the funeral where no priest lives. Thousands of parishes bury by it.
4. *Spring 1242.* Sacristans see a cardinal in red at the garden wall at night with the Primarch's face while the Primarch sits at supper. One names *Aimery* and sings; the figure goes. The garden is dug; Aubert confesses.
5. The **schism** is the quarrel over his acts: one college holds that his bishops, cardinals and Ferry Rule stand; the other that all he sealed is void. Parishes do not know whether their Ferry-Rule dead were sent.
6. *Late 1242.* Both colleges, sitting together, elect **Gelasin II**, who confirms the Ferry Rule for the plague years, forbids it afterwards, keeps Aubert's bishops and refuses to rule on Aubert (d. 1251 in a Brauvalis abbey).
7. Consequences: the doubt feeds the Chancery of Sendings (1252), which offers to sing again rites the Ferry Rule had sung; lay singing after 1242 is unlicensed (hence Gerlind's trial, 1266); the Freeflame later cite the Ferry Rule as precedent for congregational Sendings.

**Bent and why.** The schism is now the plague's speed inside the conclave, exposed by an unsent soul. No rival cities, no antipopes, no council to depose them. It gives the Chancery a second cause and the Freeflame a precedent.

**In-world disagreement.** Aurelian canonists: the Usurper of the Ring, his name off the roll of Primarchs. Brauvalis abbeys and the Freeflame: the Ferryman Primarch.

**Authorial truth.** Aimery's unsent soul stood at the wall. Aubert's motives were both real: fear of a sealed conclave, and wanting the chair. Ferry Rule funerals worked where the singers named the dead truthfully; no authority changed any soul's passage.

## 2. Alessior II (1281–1296)

**Old sequence (Alexander VI Borgia).** A Sestaran outsider buys the papacy with bank credit and rules it like a principality: nepotism, probable poisonings, wars, a monumental rebuilding, funded by industrial indulgences. Number VI matches the analogue.

**New sequence and causes.**
1. A Malvora of Sestara orphaned at twelve by the Sweat, raised by the chapter that sang for his family; as bishop known for singing haunted villages quiet; an ascetic who slept on a board.
2. *1281.* Bartolo Medori's credit buys the conclave's votes for him because Bartolo has read his preaching on the seal. Alessior learns the price after the election and pays it.
3. *1283.* The **Bull of the Sufficient Seal**: a letter sealed by the Chancery places the named soul in the Church's keeping from the day the wax is pressed; the rite follows in the Church's time. Sales lose every limit; Quarne industrialises them.
4. He rebuilds the Sanctum's fallen dome to carry, in gold, the name of every soul for whom a letter was sold (about a ninth painted at his death), signs letters himself by night, and canonises Gerlind (1284) out of real devotion.
5. *Early 1296.* Dies of a winter fever in the copy-room, is buried under the dome with a sealed letter in his hands. Within the month painters and sacristans report him walking under the names; the Chancery's choir sings him out; the Sanctum denies it. By summer the story is on every road: *the Primarch's seal did not send the Primarch.*
6. He dies in debt for his election and his dome; the Medori take the registers. The story of the dome sets Ansgar counting.

**Bent and why.** The Borgia shape (nepotism, poison, a son carving a state, wars) is gone. The sin is sincerity: a true believer's doctrine did the damage, and the one who knew better was his treasurer. The renumbering (VI → II) removes the analogue's number. Ansgar's First Grievance now answers a specific bull, and the Council's Great Arrears follow from repudiating it.

**In-world disagreement.** The Sanctum: a holy man misled by his treasurer. The Freeflame: the author of the lie. Sestaran historians: a saint who could not count.

**Authorial truth.** Sincere and wrong; the Seal doctrine was false in every particular. His own Sending was sung. What walked under the dome was residue at a thin place made by thousands of unsung names painted overhead.

## 3. The Reformation (1296–1352)

### 3a. The Blackwater funerals (bending the nailed theses)

**Old.** Ansgar nailed the Hundred Grievances to the Brenholt chapel door in autumn 1297, over the Sending House's rate table.

**New.** Ansgar's Count (1296–97) is kept, now begun when the story of the dome reaches the Weiss. In the late spring of 1297 the Blackwater pit at Brenholt floods on the night shift and drowns forty-one miners, eleven of them boys. The Sending House chaplain sets a trestle with the rate table at the pithead and the widows queue. Ansgar overturns it, has each family say its dead aloud, and sings the Hundred Grievances as a litany in place of the rite, the crowd answering each with Gerlind's *named, not bought*. A pit-tally girl chalks them on the shift board. They go down the Weiss as a song; families bury the Brenholt way, the **unbought funerals**; by 1300 the Sending Houses of the upper Weiss have no buyers, and chaplains are stoned out of three towns. The Ironvale printing (c. 1299) follows the tally-board copy; the tally board is the Freeflame relic their theology forbids them to enjoy.

**Why.** The door is the most recognisable image of the analogue. A song over the drowned, answered by a saint's saying, is land before law; a funeral strike is a native mechanism with a material effect (the Houses starve), and the date is moved out of autumn.

### 3b. Aldersbruck (bending the synod burning)

**Old.** The Synod of Aldersbruck (1305) tried Ansgar; he refused to recant and was burned on the riverbank; his psalter came unburnt from the pyre; his ashes were shovelled into the Weiss (Hus at Constance, ashes in the Rhine).

**New.**
1. At the demand of the Weiss dukes and the starving Houses, a synod meets at **Aldersbruck**, the bridge-town below Brenholt, under the legate **Tiburce Vaudrey**, and takes up the Hundredth Grievance.
2. By old relic-court custom the fire is put to the book, not the man. Ansgar's psalter, its margins full of the Brenholt dead he had sung out, is laid on a brazier in the bridge chapel before the synod, **Duke Ortwin of Ironvale** and a crowd on the bridge. It does not burn.
3. The legate declares the test void "for a draught and damp vellum", seals the psalter in lead, and holds Ansgar in the duke's toll-tower on the bridge while he writes for orders. The duke wants him out of the duchy; the legate will not move him without orders.
4. The thaw comes. On the night the Weiss takes the bridge the guards flee with the keys; **Rainald**, fourteen, sent by his father to bring the prisoner off, is turned back at the bridgehead and watches the tower go. The body is never found.
5. **Lucian Ferro**, the synod's junior notary, writes all of it down; the record is copied before anyone stops it.
6. The reach below the broken bridge becomes **the Preacher's Water**.

**Why.** No recantation scene, no execution, no ashes: the test happens, answers, and is voided, and the man dies of two authorities' refusal to carry the consequence, which is the held-proofs engine in its first modern form. Contingency (a flood) and messy people (a legate, a duke, fleeing guards) carry the outcome.

**In-world disagreement.** Freeflame: murdered by neglect. Old Church: the river gave the verdict the brazier would not. Bargemen: the river took back the man who named its drowned. Hill parishes: he climbed out, and the nameless friar at mine-head funerals is him.

**Authorial truth.** Ansgar drowned in 1305; the hill legend is false (the nameless friar is several men over sixty years). Why oaths on the water hold remains unresolved, even here, as does Ansgar's sainthood.

### 3c. The war from the mines (bending the peasant war and the wagon-forts)

**Old.** The Ashenmen Risings (1306–1329): twenty-three years of wagon-fort war behind literate quartermasters, won by nobody, ended when Rainald burned the seals (the Peasants' War's causes with the Hussites' wagon-forts and length).

**New.** The oath in the Weiss (spring 1306, at the broken bridge, faces smeared with the grey iron-washings) and its letter are kept. The war is fought out of the ground: the Ashenmen hold the pits and drifts of the upper Weiss, come up by night to raid Sending Houses and church estates, carry the chests down the shafts and burn the seals in the deep galleries, hang the Brenholt chaplains from the winding gear and smash the Mother's images. Armies burn the villages above and cannot dig them out. In **1314** Caldrian engineers turn the Weiss into the Brenholt drifts and about three hundred people drown, women and children among them. The iron country's forges starve for twenty-three years. In **1329** Rainald, needing his iron and carrying the tower, burns Ironvale's Sending letters at Brenholt; the oath is fulfilled, the bands come up out of the pits into his army, and the ore comes with them.

**Why.** No pitched battles, no wagon-forts, no crushing defeat: a mining people uses the ground it knows, the cost falls on iron, and the war ends because a duke needs his mines. The siege-craft of shafts and flooded galleries enters the military record.

### 3d. Ferro and Rainald (bending the systematiser and the sheltering prince)

**Old.** Ferro carries his record to Ironvale c. 1312 and writes *The Order of the Open Flame* (c. 1316) there under Duke Rainald's protection; his students govern (Calvin's Geneva with Frederick the Wise's shelter).

**New.**
1. After Aldersbruck Ferro becomes a Sanctum canonist at the university of Veridance.
2. c. 1312 the Sanctum commissions him to set out the Brenholt teaching in full and refute it. c. 1316 he delivers the first half, *The Order of the Open Flame*, so fair that the reformers adopt it whole as their order. It cites the Count, the Aldersbruck record, Gerlind and the Ferry Rule.
3. The refutation never comes. His licence to teach is suspended (1317); he is never tried, because a trial would mean reading the book aloud.
4. Rainald, son of the jailer, reads the record with his own father's name in it, and the book. In 1330 he invites Ferro to Ironvale; Ferro refuses: *I wrote their case. I did not promise to plead it.* Ironvale's discipline is built from the book by elders, without its author.
5. Ferro teaches privately at Veridance; his students include reformers and Ivo of Saint-Leon. He sits as the Council's witness from 1337 and dies in the spring of 1352.

**Why.** The systematiser is the movement's prosecutor and never its member; the prince is the jailer's son and is refused. This also explains a standing oddity of canon (the Lantern seminaries teach the reformers' books accurately): both orders came from one desk.

**Authorial truth.** Ferro believed the parts of his book resting on the Count, Gerlind and his own record, and never believed congregations could govern themselves without cruelty. He could not write the refutation because he could not answer the Count. He never converted.

### 3e. The Council of Veridance (bending the council's shape)

**Old.** Celestin IX convenes a long reforming council (1337–1352) which abolishes sales, orders the Arrears, rebuilds seminaries, codifies the five tests and founds a new order (Trent).

**New.** Celestin IX convenes it in 1337 at Veridance because Ferro lives there, and its first act is to summon him to deliver his refutation. Ferro, fifty-eight, tells the fathers he cannot refute the parts that are true and offers to show them which. The Council keeps him by the stove in the guild-hall for fifteen years as its unvoting witness, and its decrees are a line-by-line answer to his book with its author in the room: sales abolished; **the Sufficient Seal repudiated**, which turns every unsung letter into a debt and so orders **the Great Arrears**; the Lantern chartered (1338) to sing them; seminaries teaching the reformers' books accurately; the five tests codified in the last year, drafted around Sarnwell. Ferro dies in the spring of 1352; the Council closes that autumn, in the year of Vessing.

**Why.** The reader of Trent expects a council answering an outside heresy by definition. This council answers one book with its author present, and its central decree is the repudiation of a dead Primarch's doctrine, which is why the old Church's reform is a debt.

### 3f. The Order of the Lantern (bending the lamed-soldier founder)

**Old.** Father Ivo the Lamed, a crippled veteran of the Zanthiri border wars turned mystic, founds the Lantern under the Council (Loyola).

**New.** **Father Ivo of Saint-Leon** (c. 1283–1350) was carried out of the burning Saint-Leon quarter in 1285, aged two; his mother died in the fire. He grew up among the quarter's scattered survivors, who went on singing the dead out for nothing at night by lantern-light because by day it was forbidden. He came to Veridance as a grown man to study under Ferro and was ordained. In 1338 the Council, needing singers for the Arrears, chartered his outlaw brotherhood as the **Order of the Lantern**. Its first great case proved the Saint-Leon apparition staged. Ivo designed it, as Ferro had taught him, to understand an opponent's doctrine accurately before judging it. "The Lamed" is retired.

**Why.** No soldier, wound or sickbed conversion. The Church's reform order is a brotherhood the Church's treasurer burned out, which makes the Lantern's investigative culture and its standing with the poor causal.

### 3g. The Korrheim Accord (causes bent; date and terms kept)

**Kept by necessity.** The date (1343), drafting at Heldenheim, formula (*every roof under its lord's lamp*; the canonists' *whose the realm, his the rite*), terms, river oath, Unsworn and silence on reunion are present-day terms and are used by playable settings (Hollow Keep's 1338 massacre "five years" before the Accord; the Accord Wardens).

**Bent.** The oath is sworn below the broken bridge at Aldersbruck, in the water that took Ansgar, not on his ashes. A new human cause forces the talks: in the winter of 1341 Ironvale's new Grand Ministry, having inherited a war it had not chosen, banks the furnaces and sells iron to neither confession until the princes talk (**the Iron Fast**); by spring the old-Church gun-pits of Caldria and Brauvalis are cold. The 1342 water-test (inconclusive, sealed) remains the supernatural cause that makes the old-Church delegates willing to swear. Ironvale relights its furnaces in the summer of 1343.

## 4. The Fiorenzia sequence (c. 1340–1349)

**Old sequence (Savonarola, the Medici, Machiavelli).** Lauro dies (1341); a Sermoran pamphlet calls the Medori vault the Bank of the Dead (1345); Frate Severin preaches; the city expels the Medori and burns its vanities in the Pyre of Masks (1346); Severin holds the city until 1349, when a Medori-restored government, returned with Aversian lances, hangs and burns him; Vetti, a dismissed Sermoran under-secretary, wrote the *Mirror* in exile c. 1340, dedicated to Lauro.

**New sequence and causes.**
1. *1336–c. 1340.* **Nicanor Vetti**, a Sermoran-born Medori factor whose foreign desk failed in the Reform Wars defaults, is sold into Fiorenzia's Debtors' Corps in 1336 and writes the *Mirror for Sovereigns* in its grey coat, on the dikes by day and in the barracks by night. He dedicates it to Lauro, who holds his paper, as an offer to pay the rest of the debt in counsel. Lauro dies in 1341 without answering; the dedication copy is filed with Vetti's debt-paper. Corps men copy and sell it on the roads.
2. *1341–1345.* Lauro's son **Arrigo** inherits as the Accord ends the war-loans. The Lantern has been buying register copies sheet by sheet since 1337; copies leak, and in 1345 a Sermoran pamphlet prints Fiorenzian extracts and calls the Medori vault **the Bank of the Dead**.
3. Frate Severin preaches that the Medori hold the city's dead in pawn, that the carnival masks have become doors for the Hungers, and that a door is shut by naming what stands behind it, never by fire.
4. *Carnival 1346.* Arrigo answers with a spectacle: a Medori-paid **Pyre of Masks** in the cathedral square, the family's own masks first, then mirrors, portrait-panels and gowns. Severin begs them not to light it. They light it; witnesses still argue about what stood in the fire. The crowd turns from the fire to the palace, and Arrigo flees down the river to **Orsena** with the master registers.
5. *1346–1349.* Severin holds no office. The senate governs, tears up Medori paper and frees the Debtors' Corps (Vetti among them); the city follows the friar into the churches for **the Long Vigil**, four years of singing every Fiorenzian name anyone can remember without the registers that would say which were owed. Credit dies. Crowds drown about a dozen mask-makers in the river as door-makers; Severin preaches against it and is not obeyed. Painters paint through the Vigil (the source of [[The Severin Panels]]). Vetti dies a free man in the winter of 1347.
6. *Carnival 1349.* The senate allows the first carnival in three years. Severin goes alone into the cellar of the mask-makers' hall where the impounded masks are kept and is found in the morning sitting upright, wearing a gilded mask nobody can lift from his face. He is buried masked outside the walls.
7. *1349.* The bankrupt senate sends to Orsena. The Medori come home by invitation, give the Fiorenzian sheets of the registers to the city's churches to finish the Vigil, and take as their price the **ducal chair, hereditary in their line**. Arrigo is the first hereditary duke. Fiorenzians say the city bought back its dead with its liberty.

**Bent and why.** The bonfire is the bank's, lit against the friar's warning; there is no expulsion vote, only a flight with the city's dead; the friar never rules and leads a Vigil; there is no ordeal by fire and no hanging; the restoration is a purchase by invitation that explains the hereditary dukedom; the adviser is a bondsman writing before the crisis, not an exile writing after it. Severin, his four years, the Pyre in 1346 and the panels are kept for Grenzburg's quest library.

**In-world disagreement.** Severin's death: Medori agents (they came home weeks later), the Hungers, despair, or a sacrifice. The Freeflame claim him as a kindled prophet; the Lantern studies him; Sermorans call the pamphlet heroism and Fiorenzians point out that it ended with the Medori as dukes.

**Authorial truth.** A grief-Mask of the Weeping Courts' kind had been feeding through the carnival since the pamphlet named the city's unsung dead. The Pyre broke its doors and did not destroy it; it was what stood in the fire. Severin was genuinely kindled in the first years of the Vigil, when the singing closed thin places. In 1349 he put on the mask the thing had been wearing, deliberately, meaning to carry it out of the city in his own body, and it killed him. Whether that was sanctity or pride, and whether the Mask left Fiorenzia with him, stays open. The Medori did not kill him; Arrigo took the registers because a crowd might do anything to liens it could not read. Vetti's dedication was a genuine offer; the irony is his.

## 5. Meshalem the Twice-Turned (1288–1291)

**Old sequence (Sabbatai Zevi).** A claimant proclaims himself the Mesakha (1288); half the tower towns sell up and begin the return; arrested by Qahirid authorities under Marzuq I (1291) and offered proof or conversion, he converts.

**New sequence and causes.**
1. Meshalem is a road-clerk of Migdal Melach. In 1287 a Black Wind ride burns a caravan camp on the Iqashar road and passes his wagons by. In 1288 he proclaims himself the Mesakha because the Wind turned aside for him.
2. Families of half the tower towns sell or mortgage their goods to Sestaran creditors and take the return road to the Empty City on roads the Black Wind has emptied of Covenant garrisons. **Qarayel gives the return his word** for a flat price per wagon (1289); the Road Companies' flat peace-price dates from it.
3. Behind them **Marzuq I's courts declare the charters of every family that left forfeit**: they have no tower-town homes to return to.
4. *1291.* Tens of thousands camp outside Qiryath Reqa in Eclessian territory; the riverland lords threaten to clear the camp. To save the people, the Keepers ask Meshalem to go down the stair to the silent gate before them and the whole return and lay his hand on it. Twice he goes down; twice he comes back up without touching it. One account gives him the words *not with these hands*.
5. The return breaks up within the month. Sestaran creditors foreclose; many die on the roads; many settle landless in Eclessian river towns. Meshalem stays as a mourner, sweeping the outer court, until his death in 1303. A few households, the Sweepers, hold that his turning proved him true.
6. Since Meshalem, no claimant may go down the gate stair before the Keepers' examination, the Keepers prohibit any attempt to wake the gate, and every claimant is presumed false.

**Bent and why.** No arrest by a sultan, no apostasy, no exile. The sign, the road and the ruin are native (the Black Wind, Qarayel's word, the Covenant's charter law), and the climax is a refusal at a held proof that leaves the proof untested. The epithet keeps its form with a new meaning.

**Authorial truth.** He was not the Mesakha. He believed he was until the stair, turned back because he had begun to doubt and would not lay a doubting hand on the gate. Nothing about the gate was tested or decided.

## 6. Qarayel's burial (1303)

**Old (the Genghis legend).** Burial riders concealed the grave and did not return; a thousand horses were driven over the ground.

**New.** Qarayel dies in his tent in the spring of 1303 while preparing war on Iqashar. On the first night, before his sons' lines have all ridden in, his old stallion, a grandson of the black colt, breaks its tether and comes to the tent; the watchers see it go out into the grass with the dead man across its back and no one at its head. Every grace-mare in the great camp pulls her pickets and follows it into the dark. Nobody follows: the wind-speakers forbid it, and the riders' own horses will not take the trail. On the eleventh day the herds come home, lean and quiet, without the stallion and without the body. The Partition of 1304 divides the herds that came home. Faction readings are recorded in the reader article (White: to the sky; Iron: back to where the grace came from; Moon: the herds came home, and that is enough).

**Weird status.** This is a weird by policy and by nature. No document explains where the stallion went, why the mares followed or why they came back. Faction readings stay faction readings.

## What a reader of the analogues would now fail to predict

- The theses are sung, not posted, and the reform spreads as a funeral strike before it is a doctrine.
- The reformer is not burned and does not recant: his book survives the fire, the test is voided, and a flood takes him from a tower.
- The rising is a war out of the mines, ended by a duke's need for iron and the letter of an oath.
- The systematiser is the Sanctum's own prosecutor, never joins the movement, refuses the prince, and sits in the old Church's council as its witness.
- The counter-reform order is founded by an outlaw night-singer the Church's treasurer burned out, not a wounded soldier.
- The bonfire is lit by the banker against the friar's warning; the friar never rules and dies in a mask; the bank comes back by invitation as a hereditary dukedom; the advice-book is written by a bondsman before the crisis.
- The bought Primarch is an ascetic believer, not a Borgia.
- The claimant neither converts nor is arrested; he turns back from a gate.
- The schism is one dead twin and one living one.
- The khan's grave was made by his horses.
