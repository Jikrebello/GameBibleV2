# From Sky Roads to Horse Banners — Hunnic, Turkic, Mongol, Arabian and Apache Comparisons for Package C

## Purpose and conclusion

This study asks how the human subjects of [[Vathra in the Upper Air - Sky Giants, Flying Cities and the Grounding of the Tarkhan|Vathra's aerial kingdoms]] become the ancient Tarkhan societies of the northern dry belt, and how those societies can later undergo the genuine horse revolution already fixed as the **First Catch**. The answer requires several comparisons because no single historical people supplies the complete sequence.

The Huns, early Turks and Mongols entered history as accomplished riders. Their great political changes concerned coalition, command, tribute, diplomacy and the government of conquered peoples. The mobile communities of pre-Islamic Arabia show how camels, wells, temporary camps, market towns and client kings could sustain a hard and sophisticated open-country world without producing a steppe cavalry empire. Apache histories offer a different and especially useful transition: existing hunting, trading and family societies acquired foreign horses rapidly through Indigenous exchange, barter and raiding, then changed their ranges, work, warfare and relationships with neighbouring peoples. Even that transformation varied sharply between Apache peoples and occurred amid Spanish conquest and Comanche expansion.

Package C should therefore preserve **two separate revolutions of mobility**.

1. During the Great Unyoking, human communities seize the ground half of Vathra's air-and-land economy. They retain camels, small stock, carts, textiles, wells, animal medicine and caravan government while losing most royal horses and the infrastructure which made cities fly.
2. After the Atherian Fall in 905 AR, eastern Tarkhan acquire large numbers of horses, handlers, tack and breeding knowledge. Their own networks spread and transform these resources during the First Catch and the generations which follow.

The first change creates the ancient Tarkhan. The second creates the Grass-Tarkhan horse world. More than three thousand years separate them. Vathra gives the Tarkhan a deep history of movement and sky symbolism; it does not give them an unbroken cavalry tradition.

## Evidence and terms

The five comparisons do not rest upon equal evidence. Hunnic history survives principally through Roman observers, archaeology and ancient DNA. Early Turkic rulers commissioned their own monumental inscriptions, although Chinese, Iranian and Byzantine sources remain essential. The Mongol conquests produced Mongol, Persian, Chinese, Latin and other accounts written from sharply different political positions. Ancient Arabian nomads left tens of thousands of brief inscriptions in their own words, while later poetry and narrative preserve selected memories through long transmission. Apache history combines archaeology, Indigenous oral tradition, tribal historical work and Spanish, Mexican and United States records created inside colonial conflict.

Several familiar labels require care.

- **Hun** could name a ruling formation or feared political identity containing people of several origins. It is not a secure synonym for Xiongnu, Turk or Mongol.
- **Turkic** describes a language family and many historical peoples. The Türk khaganates, Oghuz, Kipchaks and later Turkic states did not form one continuous tribe.
- **Mongol** could denote a narrower pre-imperial grouping, the Chinggisid political people or the inhabitants of later Mongol successor realms.
- **Bedouin** is a useful later designation for Arabic-speaking mobile pastoralists. It should not be projected upon every ancient Arabian pastoral community as a single ethnicity.
- **Apache** names several Southern Athabaskan-speaking peoples with distinct histories. Plains Apache, Jicarilla, Lipan, Mescalero and Chiricahua experience horses and colonial pressure differently.
- **Nomad** describes forms of mobility. It does not mean stateless, propertyless, isolated, technologically backward or perpetually in motion.

The term **horse-lord** will be used only for a society in which access to breeding herds, remounts, pasture and mounted service structures political power. A person who owns or rides one horse is not thereby part of a horse-lord civilisation.

## I. The Huns: a fast empire of many peoples

### Origins and the limits of ancestry

The European Huns appear north of the Black Sea in the late fourth century CE and become a decisive power in the Carpathian Basin during the fifth. Their relationship to the earlier Xiongnu Empire of Inner Asia has long been disputed. Archaeology and written evidence never supported a simple migration of an intact nation across the continent. A 2025 genomic study has nevertheless identified long shared segments connecting several individuals in fifth- and sixth-century Europe with people from the highest ranks of Xiongnu society. The result supports direct trans-Eurasian ancestry in a small number of cases. The same study found broad diversity across Hunnic-period burials and describes Attila's realm as a multiethnic and multilingual empire.[^1]

This distinction is valuable for Tarkhan history. An imperial formation may contain a genuine travelling lineage without being the biological continuation of one ancient people. Political names attract clients, captives, spouses, interpreters and defeated rivals. Later chroniclers can mistake the prestige ancestry of a few houses for the ancestry of everyone who fought beneath the name.

### Pastoralists in an agricultural country

The Huns did not occupy an empty grassland. They entered landscapes containing Roman provinces, villages, fields, roads and Germanic, Iranian-speaking and other populations. Isotope studies of fifth-century cemeteries in Hungary show varied diets and movements within local communities. Some people combined animal husbandry with cultivation. Locals could adopt pastoral practices while newcomers used farming resources. The evidence fits households adapting to a mixed frontier rather than two sealed populations labelled nomad and farmer.[^2]

Hunnic armies depended upon horses, but their empire depended upon grain, craftsmen, tribute, interpreters and subject warriors as well. Attila's court included people speaking Hunnic, Gothic, Latin and Greek. The Roman diplomat Priscus describes captives, envoys, officials, wooden halls and formal feasting. His famous encounter with a Greek-speaking man in Hunnic dress reveals that captivity, social advancement and cultural preference could unsettle the categories used by Roman observers.[^3]

### Tribute, terror and incorporation

Hunnic rulers fought Rome, supplied warriors to Roman commanders, received gold and exploited Roman civil wars. Attila's campaigns devastated Balkan cities and carried away captives. His demands for tribute and the surrender of fugitives converted movement across the frontier into a contest over people as property and subjects. Roman engineers and other specialists could strengthen Hunnic siege warfare. Allied kings and war-bands preserved their own followings while serving the greater coalition.

This system produced concentrated power quickly. It also placed extraordinary weight upon Attila's household and his ability to distribute wealth, commands and fear. After his death in 453, his sons contested the inheritance. Former subject and allied peoples fought them at Nedao in 454, and the empire dissolved into several competing powers. Some Hunnic groups moved east; some entered other armies; former subjects founded their own realms.[^1]

### What the Hunnic comparison contributes

The Huns belong principally to the **late Tarkhan** comparison. Qarayel can gather existing Tarkhan houses, client towns, interpreters and conquered specialists without turning all followers into one ancestry. His success may depend upon tribute from settled neighbours as much as upon pasture. His death can release suppressed rivalries within months because his sons and commanders inherit troops, hostages and debts without inheriting his personal authority.

Package C should plant only the earlier conditions which make such a gathering possible:

- names that can widen from a household to a coalition;
- client war-bands which enter the service of neighbouring states;
- hostages and fosterlings who become genuine cultural intermediaries;
- settled markets which feed mobile armies and fear the same armies;
- prestige genealogies which later followers adopt without sharing biological descent.

The Hunnic analogy should not make ancient Tarkhan a westward invasion horde, nor should it assign Hunnic ancestry to them.

## II. Turkic worlds: dynasties, routes and travelling political names

### Equestrian societies before the khaganates

The first Türk Empire arose in the sixth century CE among communities already familiar with mounted pastoralism. The invention here was not riding. It was the creation of a durable imperial coalition under the Ashina dynasty, with eastern and western centres, ranked commanders, tribute relations and authority over peoples spread between Mongolia and the western steppe.

Pastoral households required seasonal pasture, winter protection and a herd large enough to survive animal loss. Sheep and goats supplied most daily products; horses carried riders and political prestige; cattle and camels mattered by region. A household which lost too many breeding animals could become dependent upon a wealthier patron. Mobile aristocrats therefore governed through access to pasture, animals, marriage and redistribution as well as through victory in battle.

### The khagan and the people

The eighth-century Orkhon inscriptions present imperial history from within the ruling tradition. They commemorate Kül Tegin, Bilge Qaghan and Tonyukuk and recount campaigns, revolt, hunger, subjection, restoration and service. They claim authority granted by Heaven, praise rulers who gathered and fed a dispersed people, and condemn leaders and subjects whose disloyalty brought enslavement and death. The monuments are political arguments carved by victors; they are also indispensable evidence that early Turkic government possessed its own historical thought.[^4]

The political people described by the inscriptions is neither a loose crowd nor a modern nation. Houses owe service to rulers. Rival confederations can be enemies, subjects or partners at different moments. Defeated men and women can be described through terms of bondage. Dynastic failure scatters followers and turns nobles into servants of another empire. Restoration occurs when a ruling household gathers bodies, animals, food and loyalty again.

This pattern suits the ancient Tarkhan better than a fictional catalogue of timeless tribes. A coalition name may be real while the coalition exists. When it breaks, some houses keep the name, some enter a neighbouring kingdom and some join a later gathering under a different name. Genealogy, language and political allegiance remain related but separate.

### Pasture and city

The Türk empires governed a world containing oasis farmers, caravan cities, Iranian-speaking Sogdian merchants and large sedentary empires. Sogdian intermediaries helped provide chancellery practice and long-distance diplomacy. The earliest known inscription associated with the Türk Empire at Bugut is written in Sogdian. Sogdian merchants connected steppe courts with China, Iran and Byzantium and benefited from the routes opened by Turkic power.[^5]

This was cooperation under unequal conditions. Khagans gained tolls, silk and diplomatic reach; merchants gained protection and access; towns supplied food and craft; armies could also seize the same goods and people. Trade did not civilise an otherwise empty steppe. Pastoral government and urban expertise formed a political economy in which each side could bargain, profit and suffer coercion.

### Oghuz, Kipchak and the movement of names

After the early khaganates, Turkic-speaking coalitions repeatedly migrated, divided and recombined. Oghuz and Kipchak names covered changing alliances rather than immutable blood communities. Some groups founded dynasties among Iranian or other settled peoples. Some entered armies as slaves or clients and rose to rule. Languages and political names travelled with smaller ruling and military populations, while local communities retained much of their ancestry and material life.

Package C can draw three conclusions from this history.

1. Ancient Tarkhan identities should be regional and political as well as genealogical.
2. Towns and mobile households belong inside the same history; neither is merely a backdrop for the other.
3. Later expansion may spread a Tarkhan language, title or royal genealogy far beyond the descendants of Vathra's original subjects.

The Türk inscriptions' theology must not be copied as proof that Kokh is a real Menhir or that conquest carries divine approval. Later Tarkhan rulers may use the sky to explain authority. Their claim remains a human interpretation of rule.

## III. The Mongols: the political use of a mature horse world

### Temüjin's household crisis

The Mongols of the twelfth century already lived within an equestrian pastoral world. The *Secret History of the Mongols* begins Chinggis Khan's rise at the scale of a damaged household. Temüjin's father dies; allied households abandon his widow Hö'elün and her children; the family survives through her labour; Temüjin kills his half-brother Bekter during a conflict over food; rivals later capture him; his wife Börte is abducted and recovered through an alliance which includes his sworn brother Jamukha. These episodes mingle remembered history, court genealogy and legitimating narrative, but their intimate violence is indispensable.[^6]

The narrative does not portray greatness as hereditary inevitability. Kin abandon kin. A future conqueror commits fratricide. Marriage creates political ties and exposes women to abduction. Poor followers choose patrons who can feed and defend them. Temüjin's later coalition becomes powerful because he attracts people across older lineage divisions, rewards service, punishes betrayal and reorganises military obligations around his own command.

This offers a strong grammar for Qarayel, not for Vathra's ancient fall. Qarayel should rise inside an already mature Tarkhan horse society. A ruined household, a broken alliance and the rescue of abandoned people can explain his first following. They cannot excuse his later massacres or prove that he was destined to rule.

### Herds, remounts and speed

Mongol operational reach arose from pastoral systems as much as from individual skill. Warriors travelled with several remounts and changed horses to preserve endurance. Scouts gathered information over enormous distances. Herds supplied food, transport and replacement animals, although drought, winter and exhausted pasture could still break an army. Camps carried households and wealth; women and servants performed much of the animal, textile and logistical labour on which campaigning depended.

Mounted archery, feigned retreats and encirclement required discipline. Hunting trained coordinated movement. None of this removed the problem of fortified cities. Mongol forces used Chinese, Muslim and other engineers, miners and craftsmen to build and operate siege equipment. Specialists entered Mongol service through patronage, conscription, enslavement and forced deportation.

Ögedei's postal relay organised stations with horses, fodder and authorised messengers. The network accelerated government, intelligence and trade across territories that no single pasture circuit could join.[^7] It offers a later model for Qarayel's road government. It should not appear in Package C as an intact Vathran inheritance. Ancient Tarkhan can preserve route hosts and agreed stages; a true imperial relay belongs to a later ruler with the means to requisition animals and supplies.

### Conquest without romance

Mongol conquests destroyed cities, massacred resisting populations, enslaved survivors and transferred craftsmen to distant rulers. Threatened annihilation induced some settlements to surrender; surrender could preserve many lives, but it did not guarantee freedom or prevent confiscation. Conquered soldiers were conscripted. Irrigation and cultivation suffered both deliberate destruction and the collapse of labour and administration.

The same rulers protected merchants, invited religious specialists and moved techniques, people and art across Eurasia. These actions belonged to one imperial system. The movement celebrated as exchange by a court historian could begin with a craftsman's family killed or separated. The source tradition itself is divided: the Persian official Juvaini admired Mongol institutions and patrons while recording the ruin they caused; the *Secret History* preserves royal intimacy and self-justifying violence; Latin envoys wrote from threatened Christian states.[^8]

World Egg should retain this double history. Qarayel's government may make roads safer for licensed merchants while destroying a town which refuses his levy. He may protect a captured engineer and enslave that engineer's family. Later Hordes may preserve art, markets and religious plurality while inheriting land acquired through massacre.

### Succession and the Four Hordes

Chinggisid conquests became several regional empires ruled by branches of the family. These realms retained a shared prestige genealogy while governing Chinese, Iranian, Turkic, Rus' and other populations through different administrative and religious arrangements. They fought one another as well as their external enemies.

This remains the strongest comparison for the Four Hordes. Each Tarkhan successor should contain mobile houses, subject towns, merchants, captives, mixed families and former enemies. Each inherits particular tools and claims from Qarayel. None is an ethnic colour on a map or the sole authentic continuation of the Black Wind.

## IV. The mobile peoples of pre-Islamic Arabia

### Camps within a populated desert

Northern and central Arabia contained pastoral camps, oasis towns, caravan markets, cultivated districts, temples and client kingdoms. Mobile households travelled through known territories to find water and grazing. Their movements brought them repeatedly to the same wells, winter grounds, neighbours and markets. A camp could divide when pasture failed and gather when rain, pilgrimage, trade or war made larger numbers useful.

The Safaitic inscriptions carved in the basalt deserts of southern Syria, Jordan and northern Arabia preserve brief first-person records from many of these mobile communities. Writers name parents and lineages, pasture animals, wait for rain, mourn relatives, pray for safety, curse vandals and remember raids. One man grieves for a brother killed while tending another household's livestock and asks several gods for vengeance. Another grieves for a brother held prisoner during a conflict with Rome. A newly published inscription appears to record either the division of twenty-one captured horses or a covenant supplying twenty-one horsemen for a raid.[^9]

These are not complete histories, but they give the open country a human scale. A permanent spring matters because families and animals return to it. A raid matters because the stolen herd represented milk, transport, marriage wealth and survival. A dead herder leaves a brother whose grief and demand for vengeance outlive the camp in a few lines cut into stone.

### The camel transformation

Dromedary domestication probably intensified around the transition from the second to the first millennium BCE. The animal's tolerance of heat and water loss transformed long-distance movement through Arabia and the adjoining deserts. Camels carried freight, people and military supplies and supplied milk, meat, fibre and wealth. Genetic evidence reflects later movement along caravan routes and recurrent mixing between populations.[^10]

Camel pastoralism did not create empty freedom. Herds required labour, breeding knowledge, pasture and access to wells. A ruler who controlled water could demand payment from caravans. A wealthy house could lend animals and reduce a debtor to dependent service. Raiding a camel herd could condemn children and old people who could no longer move. Conversely, a dispersed camel economy could survive the destruction of a palace because ordinary households, rather than a royal stud alone, possessed animals and knowledge.

This is the best real-world comparison for Vathra's ground system. The aerial courts consume grain, ore, cloth and sacrificial wealth, while camel households move the bulk below. When royal flight fails, the caravans lose privileged customers and coercive overseers. They retain the means to travel and feed themselves.

### Horses in Arabian society

Horses existed in Arabia but did not displace the camel's daily economic role. Evidence from southern Arabia suggests that mounted horses became more prominent in late antiquity, while cavalry remained numerically limited in recorded armies. Horse ownership could mark elite status. Horses required more reliable fodder and water than camels and therefore clustered around rulers, wealthy houses, oases and military patrons.[^11]

Package C should use this distinction rigorously. A Vathran prince may keep a fast horse aboard a war-platform or at a watered landing estate. A human driver may know how to handle it. This does not produce thousands of breeding animals across the open country. When civil war destroys royal studs and fodder depots, camel transport survives because it was more widely distributed.

### Raiding, captivity and feud

Raiding could acquire livestock, avenge a killing, punish a rival or win prestige. Temporary covenants assembled participants who did not remain one army afterward. Success redistributed animals and captives; failure left dead relatives, captured fighters and vulnerable households. Ransom could restore a prisoner. Enslavement, sale, coerced marriage and sexual access could follow capture. A poet or intercessor could negotiate peace, and a sacred season or sanctuary could suspend violence, but neither erased the harm already done.

Generosity and predation could belong to the same famous person. Hospitality protected a guest because the desert made such protection necessary. Its violation carried exceptional shame because a host possessed immediate power over a traveller's life. Feud gave bereaved kin a recognised demand for vengeance or compensation; it could also sustain killings across generations whose youngest participants had not caused the first offence.

Package C should show these consequences directly. A caravan chief may rescue a starving stranger and later sell prisoners. A well confederacy may enforce peace during a market and attack the departing weak beyond its boundary. Captive children may grow within another people, retain memories of their first families or become brokers between enemies. No single deed turns an entire culture into either noble hosts or irredeemable raiders.

### Cult and political clients

The inscriptions reveal several divine names, sacrifice, ritual shelters, pilgrimage, divination, mourning and an impersonal Fate. They do not reveal a single Arabian pantheon. Mobile cults interacted with oasis sanctuaries and the religions of neighbouring empires. By late antiquity, Arab client dynasties such as the Lakhmids and Ghassanids guarded imperial frontiers, fought one another and connected mobile war-bands with Persian and Roman government.[^12]

World Egg can use this pattern for ancient border kings who broker between Tarkhan circuits, proto-Ahdari wells, Qiryathi roads and Qahuri officials. Their service does not turn their subjects into the people of the patron empire. Their cult claims may conflate atmospheric Menhir pressure, Vathran relics and human ancestors. Authorial controls must keep those identities separate.

## V. Apache histories and rapid horse adoption

### Why the comparison must remain specific

Apache peoples are not a substitute for a generic image of the mounted Indigenous raider. Southern Athabaskan-speaking communities entered the Southern Plains and Southwest before sustained European colonisation, probably through several movements whose dates and routes remain debated. They developed different societies in plains, mountain, desert and river environments. The comparison used here concerns the **Plains Apache and related Apachean adaptations**, with Jicarilla, Lipan, Mescalero and Chiricahua histories used only where named.

Before abundant horses, Apache local groups hunted bison and other game, gathered plants, traded with Pueblo farming communities and, in several regions, cultivated crops in small settlements. Dogs hauled possessions on travois. Seasonal gatherings renewed kinship and ceremony. Plains–Pueblo exchange moved meat, hides, tallow and salt in return for maize, pottery, obsidian, turquoise and other goods. Intermarriage and trading partnerships accompanied the exchange.[^13]

This history matters because the horse did not transform an empty people. It entered a mature system of skills, relationships and seasonal knowledge.

### A revised chronology of the horse

Older summaries treated the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 as the event which released horses to Indigenous peoples of the Great Plains. The revolt certainly disrupted Spanish control and made large numbers of animals available. A major interdisciplinary study published in *Science* in 2023, undertaken with Indigenous scholars and knowledge holders, dates domestic horses in the central Plains and northern Rockies to the first half of the seventeenth century at the latest. Horse remains show riding, veterinary care and ceremonial treatment before eighteenth-century European observers arrived.[^14]

The better account is therefore a **networked diffusion**. Horses escaped, were captured in raids, transferred through trade, bred by Indigenous households and moved along existing exchange routes. The process began earlier than one revolutionary event and accelerated when colonial control failed. The animals remained Spanish-derived, but Indigenous people determined much of their subsequent distribution and use.

This sharpens the First Catch. The escape of Atherian remounts after 905 AR remains the decisive opening. It need not mean that one band captures the only herd on one day. The First Catch can name fifteen years during which several Tarkhan communities acquire animals from abandoned forts, fugitive handlers, raids, trade and reproduction. Later singers condense those years around Grey Sister and one exemplary capture.

### What horses changed

By 1680, Plains Apache used horses to extend hunting and trading ranges. Animals carried larger lodges and more household goods, improved mounted bison hunting and became trade wealth. Women and children travelled with managed herds; animal care enlarged daily work. Skilled breeders, healers, tack makers and traders gained importance. Poor households could become dependent upon families controlling mares and winter pasture.

Raids travelled farther and retaliation became harder. Apache groups obtained animals from Spanish settlements through barter and theft and traded horses northward for agricultural and manufactured goods. Colonial markets also drew captives into exchange. Spanish slave-raiding struck Apache communities; Apache raiders took captives from others. The horse multiplied reach without deciding how that reach would be used.

Ecology imposed costs. Larger herds consumed water and grass. A drought or severe winter could turn wealth into dead animals and hunger. Farming hamlets made some Apache groups easier for enemies to find. Mobility could preserve a community under pressure while separating relatives and reducing time available for cultivation.

### Displacement by another horse power

The Comanche acquired horses and entered the Southern Plains around the beginning of the eighteenth century. Their mounted hunting, trade and raiding system expanded rapidly. Comanche pressure helped drive several Apache groups south and west, where they negotiated, allied and fought with Spanish settlements and other Indigenous peoples. The Plains Apache who remained eventually maintained a different history through alliance with the Kiowa.[^15]

The importance for World Egg is sobering. Adopting the horse does not guarantee dominance. A people may transform successfully and still face a rival whose herds, trade, alliances or geography support a more powerful mounted system. Eastern Tarkhan can win the First Catch and later lose pastures, markets or people to other Tarkhan coalitions. The Horse Centuries should contain displacement inside the wider people, not only triumph over settled outsiders.

### Cultural ownership and limits

The Apache comparison contributes a structural sequence, not names, sacred beings, ceremonies, clothing or a borrowed tribal identity. Apache sources should be named, and the differences among Apache peoples retained. World Egg should not transfer living religious traditions into Tarkhan cult. The useful elements are:

- a mature pre-horse society;
- rapid adoption through Indigenous-controlled networks;
- simultaneous trade, escape, barter, theft and captive knowledge;
- changes in household labour, hunting, transport and inequality;
- greater raiding reach and new ecological vulnerability;
- severe pressure from a second mounted people;
- remembered horse culture which does not erase pre-horse history.

## VI. There is no single nomadic ladder

The five histories disprove a convenient sequence in which wandering hunters acquire horses, become warriors and inevitably found an empire.

| Question | Huns | Early Turks | Mongols | Mobile Arabians | Apache peoples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Were horses new? | no | no | no | no, but uneven and often elite | yes, following Spanish introduction |
| Principal transport economy | horses within mixed frontier systems | mixed pastoral herds | mixed herds with several remounts per warrior | camels for long arid movement; horses for narrower uses | dogs before horses; horses later expand family transport |
| Existing settled relationships | Roman provinces and subject peoples | Chinese empires, Sogdian and oasis cities | Chinese, Central Asian, Iranian and other states | oases, markets, agricultural kingdoms and imperial clients | Pueblo and Plains farming communities, then colonial settlements |
| Main political innovation | rapid tribute coalition | khaganate and route empire | cross-lineage command and continental administration | camp, well, raid covenant and client kingship | altered mobility and interregional political economy |
| Characteristic danger | succession collapse | revolt and dynastic fragmentation | conquest, massacre and imperial division | herd loss, feud, captivity and well control | colonial violence, captive trade, ecological strain and displacement |

The decisive variables are material:

- the number and distribution of breeding animals;
- winter pasture, water and fodder;
- who performs herd labour;
- how animals are transferred between households;
- which settlements supply food, metal and specialists;
- whether rulers can convert temporary war-bands into continuing obligations;
- how captives, strangers and mixed families enter the society;
- whether succession preserves or destroys the coalition.

Package C should answer these questions for each Tarkhan region instead of labelling everyone nomadic.

## VII. Vathra's vertical empire

### The upper and lower countries

Vathra should be understood as a **vertical empire**. Its royal houses occupy crown-cities, rising estates and war-platforms. Most human subjects live below in landing towns, mine camps, wells, farms, hunting territories and caravan households. Altitude expresses political privilege because rulers can descend upon their dependants, move courts between provinces and remove a city when rebellion threatens.

The upper country possesses concentrated marvels: pressure mantles, anchor engines, carpet fleets, royal horse studs and specialists who understand prepared wind routes. The lower country possesses dispersed necessities: camels, goats, sheep, grain, rope, cloth, wood, ore, water knowledge and the labour which keeps the upper country alive.

This structure explains both Vathra's power and its weakness. A crown-city can fly across a province, but it cannot feed itself. Every movement requires landing fields, stores, route forecasts and human crews. When several royal houses fight for altitude and withhold one another's routes, their cities consume reserves while ground communities retain the ability to hide water, divert animals and cut tethers.

### The Great Unyoking

The Great Unyoking combines dynastic civil war with human revolt. Vathran houses attack one another's anchor towers and seize skilled workers. Some discard dependent wards to stay aloft. Crashing cities kill rulers, servants, slaves and people in the settlements below. Royal troops torture pilots suspected of sabotage. Rebels kill overseers, loot collaborators and sometimes murder rival human households beneath the language of liberation.

The human victory comes through distributed knowledge. Drivers move camel herds beyond collection points. Weavers alter command knots. Well keepers poison or conceal stores intended for royal war-platforms. Captive smiths break replacement fittings. Pilots choose forced landings or abandon passengers in the upper air. The revolt succeeds because Vathra concentrated flight while dispersing the labour required to sustain it.

The commissioned Phoenix acts only against unlawful recurrence, Titanic reassembly and war-brood continuation. It does not teach the rebels, select their leaders or destroy every city. Vathra's political collapse remains the result of Giant civil war, subject revolt, material failure and limited heavenly severance operating together.

### Why camels survive and horses do not

Camels are spread across ordinary ground households. They provide milk, transport, wool and movement between wells. Herd knowledge passes through families whose work continues when the palace stops buying freight. Royal officials can seize animals, but no single archive or stud contains the whole population.

Vathran horses are different. The courts concentrate them at watered estates and military platforms. Royal handlers track lineages; depots provide fodder; landing systems permit movement between favoured pastures. War destroys these centres. Besieged courts eat animals. Rivals seize surviving mares. Neighbouring powers capture some stock, and many remnants fail in the driest country.

Scattered horses may survive for a few generations without supporting a horse-lord economy. Package C can preserve rare prestige animals, disputed horse burials and stories of sky princes who rode. It should establish that the breeding system, tack production and remount obligations disappear. The First Catch then remains a genuine social and demographic transformation.

## VIII. Package C historical architecture

### 1. Vathra before the Unbinding, before c. 2300 BR

Package C should begin with several different human populations inside the Vathran sphere rather than one ancestral Tarkhan nation.

- **Landing-town households** cultivate grain, repair anchors, house crews and suffer direct royal collection.
- **Western camel houses** provision dry approaches and maintain wells beneath seasonal sky routes.
- **Eastern hunters and mixed herders** supply hides, meat, draught animals and guides across colder grass country.
- **Mine and furnace communities** extract metal and stone for the upper cities.
- **Captive service populations** have been moved from neighbouring lands and retain different languages, cults and family memories.
- **Free border clients** trade with Vathra, hire as guards and sometimes sell prisoners to its estates.

Some communities cooperate because royal protection keeps rival predators away. Others revolt repeatedly. A human official may profit from the system without possessing Giant ancestry. A giant-blooded human retains a complete human soul and must be identified individually rather than used to classify a whole ruling class.

### 2. Collapse and the Grounding, c. 2300–2260 BR

The fall should contain several linked but separate wars.

1. Giant houses close rival wind routes and attack one another's ground anchors.
2. Food collection intensifies while cities remain aloft beyond their safe season.
3. Ground crews coordinate animal release, sabotage and refusal.
4. Some royal courts descend and bargain; others crash, drift or cast off their human wards.
5. Heavenly severance ends selected recurrence systems while leaving ordinary combat and survival to human actors.
6. Neighbouring states seize border estates, horses, prisoners and metal during the confusion.

The bodily costs belong in the history: mass death beneath crashes; people thrown from platforms; villages stripped to feed airborne courts; prisoners tortured for route knowledge; rebels executed at tether towers; collaborators killed after surrender; surviving slaves divided among human victors.

### 3. Grounded recovery, c. 2260–1900 BR

The first post-Vathran generations rebuild around what can still move and what still holds water. Camels, small stock, carts and sleds support different environments. Fallen city material becomes well lining, roof metal, tools and feared ruins. Former pilots possess knowledge which is impressive but often useless without anchors and prepared routes.

Households remember status differently. A former caravan slave may own the animals released during the revolt. A landing-town official may claim that an old collection role entitles his descendants to govern a well. Refugees from a crashed ward may enter an eastern hunting population through marriage and adoption. Captives taken during the last wars may preserve songs which call the Great Unyoking a change of masters rather than freedom.

The **Arabian comparison** supplies the western pattern: camel houses, recurring wells, seasonal markets, raids, sanctuary and caravan clients. The **Apache pre-horse comparison** helps the east: mobile hunting and gathering, trade with settled food producers, small cultivation where conditions allow, dogs or draught stock, and seasonal assemblies of related local groups. Neither region is culturally complete without towns and neighbours.

### 4. Hearths and early Tarkhan identities, c. 1900–1400 BR

Related northern societies become recognisably Tarkhan over many generations. Shared descent from Vathran subjects matters, but so do marriage, adopted captives, common speech, exchange and repeated defence against outsiders.

Western communities organise around wells and camel circuits. Their leaders gain authority by feeding guests, arbitrating routes, lending animals and defending market peace. The same leaders may raid rivals, take captives and foreclose debt into service. Eastern communities gather for large hunts, seasonal grazing and defence, then divide into smaller winter groups. Some maintain river hamlets or fields; others rely more heavily upon game and herd products.

Assemblies should remain practical. Households decide who may camp at a water source, how to pursue stolen animals, when several bands will hunt together and which neighbour will receive an envoy. A successful speaker cannot compel every absent group. Oaths bind named participants and expire or fail under stated conditions.

Vathran memory changes during this period. The sky becomes the place from which rulers once descended and the open expanse beneath which no roof can imprison a traveller. Both meanings inform later devotion to Kokh. Neither establishes Kokh's objective identity.

### 5. Bronze Roads and regional powers, c. 1450–800 BR

The Breaking of the Bronze Roads disrupts the markets which connect wells, eastern hunting grounds, Qiryathi towers, Qahuri estates and other towns. Caravan houses lose customers and protection. War leaders seize stored grain and animals. Some communities turn more heavily to raiding; others enter client service with surviving states.

This period should develop three different kinds of power:

- **route leagues**, formed by camel houses and market settlements to reopen specific journeys;
- **open-country federations**, assembled for hunting, war or seasonal defence and liable to divide afterward;
- **border kings**, supported by a town, marriage network and foreign patron and able to employ mobile war-bands without owning them as a people.

The early Turkic comparison supports travelling coalition names and relations between pasture and city. The pagan Arabian comparison supports client kings, raid covenants and sacred market peace. The Hunnic and Mongol imperial models remain too late and centralised for this period.

Violence remains local and material. Raids steal breeding animals and children. A market ruler executes men who break sanctuary. A route league burns a well-house that aided robbers, killing dependants inside. A victorious confederation divides captives and quarrels over ransom. No single war creates all Tarkhan society.

### 6. The balance before Atherian unification, c. 800–350 BR

By 350 BR, the northern country contains several Tarkhan societies rather than one state.

| Regional world | Economy and movement | Political form | Principal neighbours | Horse condition |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| western well country | camels, small stock, irrigated plots, caravan brokerage | well houses, market councils and temporary route leagues | proto-Ahdari wells, Glassward towns, Qahuri and Gateway interests | rare imported prestige and courier animals |
| central dry marches | mixed herding, carts, seasonal markets and military service | client captains and disputed border kings | Narash, Arqeshite successors, Qiryathi roads | small elite holdings; no remount system |
| eastern grass country | hunting, mixed herds, river hamlets and wide seasonal circuits | local groups and periodic federations | Vashari regions, eastern towns and other Orphaned populations | scattered animals and foreign imports; no horse-lord society |
| crash belts | salvage, mining, guarded cultivation and pilgrimage | ruin towns, hereditary keepers and predatory strongholds | all neighbouring circuits | stories and occasional relics outweigh living stock |

Tarkhan identity can name related speakers and customary partners without creating a universal council. Western, central and eastern people recognise differences in livelihood and reputation. Captives, spouses and clients can become Tarkhan through household incorporation. Foreign service does not erase ancestry automatically. A border king's subjects may include Tarkhan, proto-Ahdari, Jirahar and town populations without becoming one ethnicity.

Package C should end here. It may state the conditions inherited by later history, but it should not narrate the First Catch, Qarayel or the Four Hordes as if they were inevitable.

## IX. The later horse revolution as a continuity check

Although Package C ends in 350 BR, its ancient history must support the already established post-905 sequence.

### The First Catch, 905–920 AR

The recommended model combines the Apache transition with the existing Atherian collapse.

- Atherian forts lose remounts, handlers, mares, tack and fodder stores.
- Some Tarkhan raid the abandoned or weakened facilities.
- Some imperial handlers flee with animals and seek protection.
- Some prisoners are forced to teach breeding and treatment.
- Horses move between Tarkhan communities through exchange, bridewealth, theft and gifts.
- Several first herds die because their owners lack winter pasture or protect prestige stallions while neglecting breeding mares.
- Camel houses finance horse keeping and acquire claims over future foals.
- Local specialists adapt existing animal medicine, ropework and route knowledge to the new stock.

Recent Indigenous horse research is especially valuable here: the decisive historical change can spread through existing local networks before outside chroniclers recognise it. The First Catch remains a genuine turning point without becoming a single-day origin myth.

### Remount generations and political change

Only after breeding populations grow can mounted hunting, courtship, seasonal distance and warfare change. Several horses per warrior require far more grass and labour than one prestigious mount. Winter pasture becomes political property. Poor riders serve patrons who own mares. Captured towns supply iron, grain and craftsmen. Eastern power grows while western camel circuits remain effective in drier country.

The early Turkic and Mongol comparisons become strongest here. Coalitions reorganise old obligations around remounts. Political names travel. Captured specialists alter siege warfare. Hunnic precedent becomes useful when one ruler converts many peoples and foreign clients into a short-lived empire of tribute.

### Qarayel and the Black Wind

Qarayel's rise should use the Mongol household and coalition grammar and the Hunnic speed of imperial concentration. His followers need not share one ancestry. His government can incorporate interpreters, traders, subject towns and defeated captains. His success should rest upon food, remounts, intelligence and redistribution as much as personal prowess.

His violence must remain personal and political. He can order massacre, deportation, enslavement and hostage execution while also protecting merchants and rewarding skilled captives. Later celebration of safe roads cannot remove the people killed to create them.

The Four Hordes then inherit different populations, routes, institutions and fragments of his family. Their division resembles the regionalisation of Mongol successor realms and the rapid release of subject powers after Attila. It does not repeat either history exactly.

## X. Narrative and material opportunities

The research suggests several kinds of episode for Package C. These are developmental functions, not approved Garden roots.

### The tether cutters

Human carpet pilots and camel drivers coordinate one city's forced landing. Their success saves dependent wards from being stripped for lift, but the descending city crushes a settlement whose inhabitants were never warned. Later houses dispute whether the cutters liberated the region or chose which strangers would die.

**Historical function:** makes the Great Unyoking bodily and local.  
**Remainder:** marriage ropes made with a recognisable Vathran command knot.  
**Collision boundary:** no company causes Vathra's whole collapse.

### The last rain caravan

A caravan house moves grain toward a starving crash belt after royal collection ends. An allied captain diverts part of the convoy to feed his fighters, and one well closes its gates to refugees. The surviving drivers establish a recurring route which later generations celebrate while omitting the families abandoned at the shut well.

**Historical function:** joins camel logistics, hospitality and predation.  
**Remainder:** a bitter travel cake broken first for absent members.  
**Collision boundary:** not another Exodus remnant company.

### The grounded city

A Vathran court accepts a controlled landing and retains local authority for one generation. Human farmers continue feeding its residents because the city still guards them from rivals. When the last Giant ruler dies, human heirs, freed workers and neighbouring raiders fight over land and stored metal.

**Historical function:** shows continuity, collaboration and succession after liberation.  
**Remainder:** a market held in the shadow of an immovable hull.  
**Collision boundary:** the city does not retain a flying engine or become a hidden Giant empire.

### The covenant of stolen horses

During a later ancient border war, several captains divide a small captured horse herd. They cannot sustain it. Their covenant survives as a heroic tale even though the animals die or pass into foreign hands within two generations.

**Historical function:** demonstrates that owning horses is not yet a horse revolution.  
**Remainder:** horsehair braided into camel tack.  
**Collision boundary:** expressly not the First Catch.

### The permanent-water lament

A herder is killed while guarding another household's animals. His sister carves his name near a spring and publicly demands blood-price. The accused family offers animals; a war captain refuses the settlement because continued feud strengthens his following.

**Historical function:** gives open-country law a personal cost without courtroom prose.  
**Remainder:** travellers speak the dead man's name before watering borrowed stock.  
**Source boundary:** draws upon the intimacy of Safaitic mourning without copying an inscription or Arabian deity.

### The people who refused the shadow

One eastern population abandons a traditional market whenever a drifting crown-city passes overhead. Their caution saves them during a royal collection, but it also leaves allied households exposed. Centuries later, descendants disagree whether refusing a Vathran shadow was courage, cowardice or an ordinary survival rule.

**Historical function:** establishes the sky as lived political geography.  
**Remainder:** camps are pitched so no person's sleeping face lies beneath a high shadow.  
**Collision boundary:** the custom does not reveal where any surviving city flies.

## XI. Recommended Package C structure

The next development delivery should use the following order.

1. **The country beneath Vathra.** Name the principal river, well belt, grass country, landing basins and mountain approaches. Establish human populations before calling them Tarkhan.
2. **The aerial houses.** Name a limited set of crown-cities, ruling houses and ground estates. Give each a different economy and fate.
3. **The Great Unyoking.** Follow royal civil war, human revolt, city-by-city collapse and neighbouring intervention through identifiable participants.
4. **The grounded generations.** Develop western camel houses, central border communities, eastern hunters and herders, landing towns, captives and mixed settlements between c. 2260 and 1400 BR.
5. **The Bronze Roads and first kings.** Build route leagues, seasonal assemblies, client dynasties, markets, cults and wars through c. 800 BR.
6. **The world of 350 BR.** Describe several Tarkhan regions, their self-understandings, neighbours and material inequalities, ending before Atherian unification.

The historical account needs named households and settlements. The names should arise during the geographic and collision review rather than being imported from Hunnic, Turkic, Mongol, Arabic or Apache languages.

## XII. Canon and collision review

### Recommended commitments

1. Vathra is a network of aerial Giant crown-cities above a more populous human ground civilisation.
2. Its subjects include several regional populations; they are ancestors and contributors to later Tarkhan, not a single proto-nation.
3. Camel husbandry and caravan knowledge are widely distributed before the Great Unyoking.
4. Royal horses are real but restricted. Their supporting system collapses and does not produce an ancient Tarkhan cavalry empire.
5. The Great Unyoking is both Giant civil war and human revolt, with several city outcomes.
6. Ground survivors retain sophisticated mobility, craft and political practice.
7. Western and eastern Tarkhan societies diverge materially while remaining related through speech, exchange and ancestry.
8. Ancient Tarkhan political names can travel, divide and recombine without defining fixed biological populations.
9. The First Catch is a fifteen-year networked horse transition, later condensed into an origin story.
10. Hunnic, Turkic and Mongol imperial grammars apply principally after 905 AR; Package C plants their institutional preconditions.

### Matters requiring Package C development

- the names and relational geography of Vathra's principal crown-cities;
- the names and fates of particular human subject populations;
- which landing towns, wells and crash sites remain inhabited;
- how Tarkhan self-names develop between c. 1900 and 800 BR;
- which proto-Ahdari, Jirahar, Qahuri and eastern states border each regional Tarkhan world;
- the number and dates of route leagues, border kingdoms and major conflicts;
- whether one surviving Vathran horse line lasts long enough to become a prestige legend;
- the exact distinction between inherited sky craft and later frauds.

### Protected or excluded conclusions

- Vathra does not become a sixth Imperial Horizon.
- Vathra does not occupy the Air Shell and does not create a native elemental civilisation.
- Tarkhan humans do not inherit Giant continuity.
- Kokh is not identified authorially with Zephyraxis or another Menhir through this research.
- No flying city, royal house or Titan fate is fixed before the Package C placement review.
- Ancient horse remnants do not diminish the novelty of the First Catch.
- The Huns, Turks, Mongols, Arabians and Apache peoples do not become one composite source culture.
- No modern Tarkhan outcome or event after 1360 AR is established.

## XIII. Source-fidelity and sensitivity ruling

The comparative translation retains hard historical consequences where they perform causal work: livestock theft, murder, massacre, enslavement, captive sexual coercion, hostage execution, torture, forced deportation, settlement destruction, abandoned dependants and deaths caused by famine or animal loss. It also retains work, music, feasting, hospitality, hunting skill, textile knowledge, affection, fosterage, trade and religious devotion. Neither category is included to cancel the other.

The Apache material remains attributed to named peoples and sources. World Egg borrows the structure of rapid animal adoption through Indigenous networks, not Apache sacred stories, names, clothing or identity. Arabian evidence is distinguished between first-person ancient inscriptions, external testimony and later literature. Hunnic ancestry claims remain graded in light of the limited direct genomic connections. Turkic and Mongol names are not used as synonyms for all Inner Asian pastoralists.

No source population is described as a developmental stage on a ladder from primitive to civilised. Mobile societies make reasoned use of difficult environments, create government and exchange, and commit violence for intelligible human purposes. Their victims remain people with families and futures rather than scenery for a conqueror's greatness.

## Research assessment

The new comparison strongly supports the aerial Vathran proposal and clarifies its limits. Vathra gives Package C a distinctive ancient civilisation: sky-ruling Giants rely upon camel roads and human labour until dynastic war and revolt tear the vertical empire apart. The aftermath produces societies whose mobility is practical, inherited and grounded.

The most valuable change is the separation between **mobility expertise** and **horse-lord power**. Ancient Tarkhan know animals, weather, routes, temporary government and the movement of households. The First Catch later adds an animal population and military economy capable of reorganising those skills. That second transformation can occur quickly because the human foundations are old, yet it remains new enough to disrupt class, work, ecology and war.

This architecture gives Package C its own history before 350 BR while preserving the later drama already attached to Grey Sister, Qarayel and the Four Hordes.
