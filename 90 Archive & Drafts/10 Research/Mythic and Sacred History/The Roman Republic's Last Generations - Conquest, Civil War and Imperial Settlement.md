# The Roman Republic’s Last Generations — Conquest, Civil War and Imperial Settlement

Rome acquired an extensive overseas empire while it was still governed as a republic. Conquest enlarged the resources available to its citizens and the responsibilities of its magistrates. It also created opportunities for commanders to remain abroad for years, reward followers and negotiate with foreign rulers. The eventual monarchy grew out of struggles over an imperial world that already existed.

This study follows those struggles from the Gracchi to Augustus. Its closest attention falls on the generation linking Caesar’s conquests to Octavian’s victory. The distinction matters for Atheria: the soldier who breaks an existing political order and the successor who makes personal rule durable can possess different abilities, loyalties and historical reputations.

This is comparative research, consulted and written on **4 September 2026**. It establishes no World Egg events. [[Caesar's World - People, Households and Rivalries]] develops the people; [[Atheria's Imperial Founding - Alternative Histories and Canon Review]] compares two possible histories for Thalion.

## Reading the evidence

Contemporary voices survive unevenly. Caesar describes campaigns he commanded. Cicero’s letters reveal uncertainty, friendship and negotiation as events unfold, alongside his assumptions as a wealthy Roman senator. Sallust writes shortly after the republican collapse but arranges his material around moral and political arguments. Augustus’s retrospective inscription explains why posterity should honour him.

Plutarch, Suetonius, Appian and Cassius Dio wrote under the empire, often generations or centuries later. They preserve earlier material now lost. Their speeches, private conversations, exemplary anecdotes and explanations of character require particular caution. Agreement among later authors can reflect a common lost source. A biographer’s vivid scene is evidence of a received account, not a recording.

Coins demonstrate that rulers circulated particular titles and images. Excavated siege works can test an account of operations. Neither supplies direct access to a person’s private intentions. Modern scholarship helps distinguish these kinds of evidence and exposes disagreements which older popular narratives conceal. The annotations below identify access limitations; abstracts are not represented as fully read books.

## 1. The inherited crisis, 133–60 BC

### Land, citizenship and political killing

Tiberius Gracchus’s tribunate in 133 BC brought a dispute over occupation of public land into a confrontation about how the republic could act. His agrarian proposals threatened established interests, including those of people who had invested in occupied holdings. He was killed with supporters during political violence. Gaius Gracchus subsequently pursued a wider programme involving grain, colonies, judicial arrangements and the position of Rome’s allies. His death in 121 followed another armed confrontation.

These struggles involved rival elite initiatives as well as material hardship. Poor citizens, wealthy landholders, Italian allies and provincial subjects had different interests. The conditions of landholding varied across Italy; the ancient picture of an entirely ruined peasantry displaced everywhere by slave estates should not be treated as a complete economic survey. The important institutional inheritance was that victory in a domestic dispute increasingly included killing opponents and denying survivors effective redress. [Appian, Civil Wars I.7–27](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Appian/Civil_Wars/1%2A.html); [Metropolitan Museum, republican expansion](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-roman-republic).

Rome’s Italian allies provided soldiers while lacking the full political position of Roman citizens. The Social War, beginning in 91 BC, combined demands for admission with the creation of an alternative Italian political organisation. Rome extended citizenship through wartime legislation and subsequent incorporation, while continuing to fight. Admission then raised further questions about registration and influence. The conflict demonstrates how an imperial partner may seek fuller membership, independence or different terms at different moments.

### Armies and the memory of Sulla

Marius’s career connected victories abroad with exceptional prominence at home. Sulla’s disputes over command culminated in marches on Rome, civil war and his appointment as dictator in 82 BC. Proscription publicly exposed named people to killing and property seizure; descendants suffered political disabilities. Sulla also altered institutions and eventually relinquished the dictatorship. The date of his resignation is disputed and should be distinguished from his consulship in 80 and subsequent retirement. His withdrawal left both constitutional changes and families who had lost relatives or acquired their property.

Military change accumulated across this period. Recruitment, equipment, command duration and expectations of reward did not all change in one “Marian reform”. Conscription continued alongside volunteering. A soldier’s attachment to a general developed through shared service, pay, confidence and prospects for settlement, with room for refusal, mutiny and bargaining. Brunt’s study specifically challenges the assumption that service became predominantly voluntary after Marius. [Brunt, “Conscription and Volunteering”, accessible abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/47293/chapter-abstract/422657330).

Sertorius’s resistance in Spain, the revolt associated with Spartacus and the war against Mithridates left Rome with powerful commanders and competing claims to victory. Crassus defeated Spartacus’s main forces; Pompey returned from Spain and claimed a share in ending the revolt. Their rivalry would continue into an alliance. The crucifixion of thousands of captured rebels on the road to Rome belongs to the violence underlying Crassus’s reputation.

In 63 BC Cicero ordered the execution of arrested conspirators associated with Catiline after a senatorial debate. Caesar argued for a different punishment; Cato urged execution. Sallust’s paired speeches are literary reconstructions of a real political disagreement. Cicero’s later exile shows that an emergency measure celebrated by supporters could remain politically dangerous to its author. [Sallust, Catiline 50–55, including Caesar’s speech](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0124%3Achapter%3D51).

## 2. Three men with different needs, 60–56 BC

Pompey came to the partnership with an extraordinary military reputation. His eastern settlements and arrangements for veterans needed political support at Rome. Crassus possessed immense wealth, clients and interests connected to finance and public contracts. Caesar needed backing for his consulship and a subsequent command capable of extending his standing. Cooperation promised each something that obstruction at Rome had made difficult.

The agreement conventionally called the **First Triumvirate** was a private political alliance, formed around 60 BC. It had no magistracy called “triumvir for governing the republic”. Caesar’s consulship in 59 supplied legislation and access to provincial command. Pompey married Caesar’s daughter Julia. Her marriage strengthened political cooperation and, according to the biographical tradition, became affectionate.

The partners continued to compete. They worked through elected offices, assemblies, personal supporters, intimidation and negotiated legislation. Opposition also contained different interests; *optimates* and *populares* describe political alignments and approaches more usefully than permanent parties with membership lists.

At Luca in 56 the alliance was renewed. Pompey and Crassus obtained the consulship for 55 and subsequent commands; Caesar’s Gallic command was extended. Pompey’s Spanish provinces could be administered through legates while he remained close to Roman politics. That combination of distance and delegated command helped make provincial armies part of domestic bargaining. [Plutarch, Pompey 47–53](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Pompey%2A.html); [Appian, Civil Wars II.9–19](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Appian/Civil_Wars/2%2A.html).

## 3. Conquest and personal power

### Pompey’s maritime and eastern achievements

Pompey’s extraordinary anti-piracy command in 67 BC combined fleets, subordinate commands and regional coordination. His success depended on clearing connected routes rather than winning one duel at sea. Defeated pirates could be killed or captured; some surrendered communities were resettled. Shipping, food supply and the interests of coastal populations belonged to the same campaign.

His command against Mithridates from 66 produced a much larger eastern settlement. He defeated opponents, dealt with Armenia, reorganised territories and clients, and made Syria a Roman province. Intervention in Judaea included a violent siege of Jerusalem. The eastern rulers concerned had their own dynastic conflicts, armies and diplomatic options. Their later support for Pompey grew from particular relationships, obligations and expectations.

Pompey therefore entered the civil wars with more than a reputation for past battles. He possessed a dispersed network of rulers, cities and former associates. The weakness of such a network was that loyalty could change when a defeated patron arrived needing protection. [Plutarch, Pompey 24–45 and 76–80](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Pompey%2A.html).

### Caesar in Gaul

Caesar’s campaigns from 58 BC began within an already entangled political world. The Helvetian migration, relations with the Aedui and conflicts involving Ariovistus gave him grounds for intervention which he presented as protection of allies and Roman interests. Further campaigns enlarged both the area of Roman coercion and the opposition it generated.

Gaul contained towns, agricultural districts, commercial networks and competing aristocracies. Some communities allied with Caesar, others resisted, and alignments changed. Grain supplied by allies, requisitioned animals, local guides and winter quarters were essential. Seasonal dispersal made an occupying army vulnerable; concentrating all its forces made provisioning difficult. Caesar’s ability to move quickly mattered because separate communities had limited time to coordinate before he arrived.

The wars financed themselves partly through plunder, enslavement and demands on subjected populations. Captives became saleable wealth. Officers and soldiers acquired expectations attached to their commander’s continued success. Reports sent to Rome made campaign achievements instruments of political competition. Caesar’s account is indispensable for this process while repeatedly explaining why his own actions were necessary. [Caesar, Gallic War, books I–III and VI, linked text and chapter guide](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Gallic_War/home.html).

In 55 and 53 Caesar crossed the Rhine. His expeditions to Britain in 55 and 54 extended Roman knowledge, intimidation and diplomatic demands, but left no permanent Roman occupation. Weather damaged ships; transport and repair constrained what victories could accomplish. These ventures are particularly useful for Atheria: a commander can gain immense prestige from reaching a country he cannot hold. [Caesar, Gallic War IV.16–36](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Gallic_War/4%2A.html) and book V in the chapter guide.

The uprising associated with Vercingetorix in 52 required the cooperation of communities whose interests remained distinct. Scorched-earth measures threatened the inhabitants’ own food and homes. Caesar suffered a reverse at Gergovia. At Alesia his forces built works facing both the besieged army and an approaching relief force. Excavation supports the broad reality of the siege system; it does not establish every number or speech in Caesar’s narrative. [MuséoParc Alésia, archaeology](https://alesia.com/archeologie/); [Musée d’Archéologie nationale, siege works](https://musee-archeologienationale.fr/vercingetorix-et-alesia).

The conquest retained its bodily consequences. Caesar describes the killing of inhabitants at Avaricum; at Alesia civilians expelled from the town were refused passage through Roman lines. Vercingetorix’s surrender led to captivity and eventual execution. At Uxellodunum in 51, Hirtius records Caesar’s cutting off the hands of those who had borne arms, leaving survivors as warnings. The punishment followed the cutting of the defenders’ water supply. It cannot be reduced to a generic loss of political rights. [Caesar, VII.28 and 77–89, chapter guide](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Gallic_War/home.html); [Hirtius, VIII.40–44](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Gallic_War/8A%2A.html).

### Crassus and the limits of wealth

Crassus’s invasion of Parthian territory ended in defeat at Carrhae in 53 BC. His opponents combined mounted archery, heavily armed cavalry and sustained resupply. Roman infantry could resist attacks while becoming exhausted and unable to bring the enemy to the kind of engagement it needed. Routes, allied advice and access to supplies mattered before the main fighting began.

Crassus’s son Publius died during the battle. Crassus subsequently died during an attempted meeting with the enemy; the circumstances and allocation of blame come through hostile and dramatic accounts. Surena’s victory served the Parthian king Orodes II, whose relations with Armenia and with his own powerful commander followed a separate political history. Plutarch reports Surena’s later execution by the king. The famous humiliations of Crassus’s corpse require attribution, and competing stories should not be assembled into one certain death scene. [Encyclopaedia Iranica, Carrhae](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/carrhae-harran-town-in-mesopotamia-where-in-may-53-b/); [Plutarch, Crassus 16–33](https://classics.mit.edu/Plutarch/crassus.html).

## 4. The alliance fails, 54–45 BC

Julia died in 54 and Crassus in 53. Their deaths removed personal and political connections, but Caesar and Pompey continued to cooperate afterward. A complete explanation requires the negotiations over command, elections, honours and security which deteriorated in 50–49. Neither grief nor an inevitable collision between two ambitious men supplies the whole sequence.

Caesar feared loss of status and hostile proceedings on surrendering command; Pompey increasingly supported demands that he relinquish his army. Each side could describe its position as defence of lawful government. Caesar’s crossing into Italy with troops in January 49 turned the dispute into open civil war. The Rubicon became memorable because an armed decision crossed a political boundary; the exact famous words belong to later literary tradition.

Pompey withdrew from Italy and sought to use wider resources. Caesar first dealt with hostile forces in Spain, then fought in the Adriatic and Greece. He suffered a setback at Dyrrhachium before defeating Pompey at Pharsalus in 48. Pompey fled to Egypt, where members of Ptolemy XIII’s court arranged his murder, seeking advantage from the victor. [Caesar, Civil War, text and campaign guide](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Civil_Wars/home.html); [Plutarch, Caesar 32–48](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Caesar%2A.html).

Caesar’s intervention in the struggle between Cleopatra VII and Ptolemy XIII produced the Alexandrian War. Victory secured Cleopatra’s position with another brother, Ptolemy XIV, as co-ruler. Caesar then campaigned against Pharnaces in Asia Minor, against remaining opponents in Africa, and finally in Spain. Cato died by suicide after the defeat of his side in Africa; fighting against Pompey’s sons continued into the Spanish campaign ending at Munda in 45.

Dictatorship accompanied reform. Caesar revised the calendar, expanded the senate, founded colonies and altered civic arrangements. His clemency admitted former opponents to public life while making their position dependent on a victorious ruler’s decisions. Concentrated authority, honours and the title of dictator for life sharpened fear of permanent monarchy. The republic still contained many institutions; their capacity to constrain Caesar had become precarious. [Suetonius, Julius 35–44, 75–80](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/12Caesars/Julius%2A.html).

## 5. Murder and the inheritance of a name, 44–43 BC

The conspiracy included men previously opposed to Caesar and men who had served him. Marcus Brutus supplied moral and ancestral prestige; Cassius helped organise the undertaking; Decimus Brutus’s trusted position made his participation especially significant. Their aims overlapped without producing a shared, practicable settlement for the morning after the assassination.

Caesar was killed on 15 March 44 during a senate meeting in the complex of Pompey’s theatre. The murder removed a person on whom appointments, expectations and compromises had come to depend. It left his army, resources, legislation and supporters. A settlement preserving his acts while granting the conspirators security could delay conflict without reconciling these interests. The funeral and public response further undermined the assassins’ control of events. [Plutarch, Brutus 8–22](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Brutus%2A.html).

Caesar’s will adopted his young great-nephew Octavian and made him principal heir. Acceptance offered a name, property claim and relationship to veterans; it did not automatically confer command of the state. Octavian had to secure recognition, recruit support and compete with Antony, the surviving consul and experienced Caesarian leader.

Cicero hoped to use Octavian against Antony. After the fighting around Mutina in 43 and the deaths of the consuls, Octavian’s independent strength grew. He obtained the consulship and then reached agreement with Antony and Lepidus. Political partners had changed again. [Appian, Civil Wars III](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Appian/Civil_Wars/3%2A.html).

## 6. The second triumvirate and its changing divisions

The **Second Triumvirate**, established by law in 43 BC, granted Antony, Octavian and Lepidus exceptional powers to reorganise the republic. It was a public office with a term, armies and coercive authority. Their agreement made possible a joint war against Caesar’s assassins and the seizure of resources from opponents.

Proscriptions named victims for killing, promised rewards and confiscated property. The process crossed family and friendship ties. Hiding a condemned person could endanger a household; betrayal could enrich a dependant. Cicero was murdered and his head and hands displayed. The triumvirs’ claims to avenge Caesar accompanied their own responsibility for these acts. Ancient casualty totals vary. [Appian, Civil Wars IV.2–51](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Appian/Civil_Wars/4%2A.html).

| Stage | Changing allocation and practical position |
|---|---|
| **43 BC: initial compact** | Antony’s principal western sphere included Cisalpine and much of Transalpine Gaul; Lepidus held Narbonese Gaul and Spain; Octavian was assigned the remaining western responsibilities, including islands and Africa whose effective possession was contested. Italy was a shared political and recruiting centre. |
| **42 BC: after Philippi** | Antony undertook eastern revenue collection and settlement; Octavian returned to Italy to settle soldiers. Lepidus lost leverage because he had not shared the victory. Intermediate allocations differ in detail between narratives; nominal grants were not complete control. |
| **40 BC: Brundisium** | The best-known east–west division emerged: Antony in the east, Octavian in the west, Lepidus in Africa. Italy remained a common recruiting ground. Antony married Octavia. |
| **39 BC: Misenum** | Agreement recognised Sextus Pompey’s position in Sicily, Sardinia and Corsica, with further promised arrangements in the Peloponnese disputed in execution. Returned exiles and shipping were part of the bargain. |
| **37 BC: Tarentum** | Antony and Octavian renewed their partnership and exchanged promises of ships and troops; the triumviral term was renewed. Delivery and later claims about it became another grievance. |
| **36 BC: Sicily** | After Sextus’s defeat, Lepidus attempted to enlarge his position. His soldiers abandoned him for Octavian; he lost effective territorial command while retaining his chief priesthood. |
| **32–30 BC: final rupture** | The partnership collapsed into war. Octavian mobilised against Cleopatra and Antony’s coalition, won at Actium in 31, and conquered Egypt in 30. |

The table combines Appian IV–V and Dio XLVIII–XLIX. Their accounts should be consulted for individual agreements; simple coloured maps often flatten grants, military occupation and intended allocations into the same thing. [Dio XLVIII](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/48%2A.html); [Dio XLIX](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/49%2A.html); [Oxford Classical Dictionary, triumviri, accessible extract](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548206294).

At Philippi in 42 Antony and Octavian defeated Brutus and Cassius. The deaths of the republican commanders settled one struggle while leaving the triumvirs with soldiers expecting payment and land. The next conflicts grew partly from the means used to satisfy those expectations.

## 7. Families, fleets and the final war, 41–30 BC

Veteran settlement in Italy displaced existing possessors and provoked resistance. Fulvia and Lucius Antonius contested Octavian’s predominance amid these grievances. Their relationship to Antony’s intentions was uncertain even to contemporaries. The Perusine War ended in 40 with defeat and destruction. Hostile stories of mass sacrificial executions require separate assessment rather than automatic repetition as established fact.

Sextus Pompey’s maritime position made him both a refuge for opponents and a threat to supply. Roman crowds experienced the rivalries through hunger and rising prices. His followers included people fleeing proscription and enslaved people escaping their owners, alongside officers and families with other reasons to oppose the triumvirs. Negotiation failed to secure lasting peace. Agrippa’s naval preparations and victories made Octavian’s eventual success possible. [Appian, Civil Wars V.12–143](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Appian/Civil_Wars/5%2A.html).

Antony meanwhile governed a diverse eastern sphere. Parthian advances and Roman counter-campaigns preceded his failed invasion in 36. Heavy equipment, supply, allied cooperation and a punishing retreat shaped its outcome. His later capture of the Armenian king Artavasdes II deepened the dynastic and diplomatic consequences. Cleopatra’s resources became increasingly important to his position.

Cleopatra ruled Egypt through a Ptolemaic dynasty with its own internal violence, temple relationships, revenue systems and international ambitions. Her alliance with Antony joined military and dynastic interests. The Donations of Alexandria in 34 publicly assigned kingdoms and titles to their children; some claims exceeded actual possession. Her position depended on what could be defended as well as what could be proclaimed. [Dio XLIX.24–41](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/49%2A.html); [Metropolitan Museum, The Year One, Egyptian history](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/The_Year_One_Art_of_the_Ancient_World_East_and_West.pdf).

Octavia’s marriage to Antony had provided a living connection between households. Its breakdown had political consequences without making Cleopatra the sole cause of civil war. Octavian’s presentation increasingly turned a Roman conflict into a defence against a foreign queen and an allegedly compromised Roman commander. Claims about Antony’s testament and intentions circulated within that struggle. Dio’s speeches are later compositions through which we can study arguments, not verbatim campaign addresses.

At Actium in 31, Agrippa’s operations had already damaged the opposing coalition’s supply and position. Disease, desertion and naval constraints mattered before the battle. Antony and Cleopatra escaped with part of their forces; interpreting the exact plan and moment of withdrawal remains disputed. Their remaining position collapsed over the following year. Both died by suicide in 30; the precise method of Cleopatra’s death is uncertain. Caesarion and Antony’s son Antyllus were killed on the victor’s orders. [Dio L](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/50%2A.html) and [LI](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/51%2A.html).

## 8. Making a settlement endure, 30–23 BC

Octavian emerged with control of extraordinary military and financial resources. Egypt entered Roman rule under a prefect. Its unusual administration should not be confused with a simple private estate owned outside the Roman state. Demobilisation and rewards reduced immediate military pressures while tying beneficiaries to the victor.

The settlement of 27 returned authority publicly to senate and people while assigning Augustus extensive provincial command. His continued consulships, military position, wealth and relationships made him predominant. The title Augustus reinforced a new standing without recreating an old republican magistracy called emperor.

In 23 he gave up the continuous consulship and relied on tribunician power and superior provincial command. These arrangements allowed other aristocrats greater access to ordinary office while preserving his decisive authority. The precise legal development and chronology of some powers remain debated. The principate acquired its form through adjustments, honours and practical relationships. [Dio LIII.12–17, 30–32](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/53%2A.html); [“Augustus and the Senate: 23 B.C.”, accessible scholarly abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antichthon/article/abs/augustus-and-the-senate-23-bc/5BEDDA72F8A51C5072FFF7CB0ACF4C98).

Religious rebuilding, festivals, images and literary patronage helped explain this order to its inhabitants. The deified Caesar gave his adoptive son a potent relationship to the dead. Augustus’s eventual account emphasised generosity, public service and restoration. Its opening presents private military recruitment as liberation of the state; omissions and evasions matter alongside explicit statements. [Res Gestae, opening chapters](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Augustus/Res_Gestae/1%2A.html).

### Epilogue: succession through a household

Augustus’s longevity exposed successive plans to bereavement and political change. Marcellus, Agrippa, Gaius and Lucius Caesar all died before him. Julia’s marriages and the position of Livia’s sons connected succession with the intimate lives of the household. Tiberius was adopted in AD 4 and acquired powers supporting his eventual succession in AD 14. These arrangements made imperial continuity dependent on more than biological primogeniture.

Livia’s importance is evident; the later stories blaming her for convenient deaths need evidence beyond their usefulness to a dramatic plot. Augustus’s treatment of Julia, his claims about ancestral morality and the compromises of his own household are part of the same history. [Suetonius, Augustus 63–65, 97–101](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/12Caesars/Augustus%2A.html).

### Interpretations deliberately kept qualified

The immediate causes of Caesar's invasion, the sincerity and practical content of republican restoration, Julia's influence, Cleopatra's precise intentions, Antony's Actium plan and the detailed legal basis of Augustus's powers remain matters of interpretation. Literary incest, seduction, poisoning and providential-omen stories receive individual attribution. The Gallic wars' total dead and enslaved cannot be supplied as a reliable census.

No quotation from Shakespeare is treated as ancient testimony. No contemporary speech is reconstructed for dramatic effect. Translations' old descriptions of peoples as inherently treacherous or barbarous are not adopted as historical explanation.

## Findings for Atheria

The most useful inheritance is a sequence in which different kinds of power repeatedly cease to be interchangeable. Money buys equipment but cannot guarantee victory. Military achievement wins followers but leaves civic opponents. Adoption gives a claim which must be made effective. Partition distributes responsibilities that produce new grievances. Sole victory creates the problem of demobilisation and succession.

The people can therefore remain distinct. A celebrated conqueror, an older organiser of provinces, a wealthy unsuccessful campaigner, an experienced lieutenant, a young inheritor, a dispossessed naval rival and an independent queen each offer a separate history. Their importance comes from how they need and injure one another, and from the communities whose lives change when those relationships fail.
