# From Second Qiryath to the Peshara — Revolt, Destruction and Jirahar Religious Formation

## Purpose

The modern Jirahar should develop from a substantially settled Second-Qiryath civilisation, a large pre-existing diaspora and two later catastrophes. The first is the failed Great Tower Revolt and Atheria's destruction of the sanctuary in 70 AR. The second is the Vestian programme of 377–395, when a converted empire turns theological accusation into confiscation, expulsion and the destruction of most remaining eastern towers.

This sequence provides the historical foundation for a Peshara-centred religious culture comparable in some respects to rabbinic Judaism. The comparison must remain historically accurate. Rabbinic Judaism did not appear fully formed in 70 CE, and it was not simply every Pharisee continuing under a new title. Synagogues, diaspora communities, scriptural interpretation, prayer and non-sacrificial institutions already existed. After the Temple's destruction, small circles of teachers gradually developed texts, practices and authority over centuries, alongside other forms of Jewish life.

The World Egg translation should follow the same slow formation. The Peshara exists before the Second Destruction as interpretation, precedent and remembered dispute. The loss of sacrifice makes those traditions more important. Several Jirahar schools then shape them into regional collections. Their eventual prominence follows long argument, persecution, migration and practical usefulness rather than a single council declaring a finished book.

## Historical comparison: Second Temple plurality

Second Temple Judaism included the Jerusalem Temple and priesthood, local and diaspora communities, synagogues or meeting houses, scriptural specialists, Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes and other groups known imperfectly through Josephus, the New Testament, the Dead Sea Scrolls and later rabbinic literature. These sources disagree and often describe rivals for polemical purposes.

The Temple held immense religious and political importance. Pilgrimage, sacrifice, gifts and priestly authority connected communities across the Mediterranean and Near East. Yet many Jews already lived outside Judaea. They maintained communal institutions and participated in surrounding economies and languages. The destruction of 70 transformed the centre of the system; it did not create the diaspora or invent worship outside the Temple.

Pharisees cultivated interpretations of law and traditions extending beyond the written text. Their relationship to the later rabbis is real but cannot be treated as a seamless institutional succession. Post-70 rabbis were initially a small and geographically limited movement. Their literature later constructed lineages of teachers reaching into the earlier period. Those lineages express authority and memory as well as recoverable history.

## Second Qiryath before Isfir

Second Qiryath is consecrated around 660 BR after the Arqeshite fall. It becomes the principal Jirahar sanctuary, a pilgrimage city and a centre of sacrifice. It is never the only Jirahar community. Road courts, tower towns, merchant quarters and eastern exile settlements continue beside it.

By the late republican and early imperial periods, the Jirahar are settled in several senses:

- agricultural villages and market towns surround Qiryath;
- priestly and learned households hold property and office in the city;
- Jirahar quarters exist in Atherian ports and provincial capitals;
- eastern communities live beyond direct Atherian rule;
- travelling judges, merchants, healers and craftspeople connect the settled communities.

Mobility remains an inherited skill rather than the condition of every household. Some families have occupied the same street or field for generations. Others move seasonally or maintain relatives in several cities.

## Parties, schools and ordinary worship

The pre-70 Jirahar world should contain several tendencies rather than two uniform camps.

### Sanctuary houses

Leading priestly families administer sacrifice, pilgrimage stores, purity practice and parts of civic government. Some collaborate with Atherian governors to protect the sanctuary. Others exploit access, gifts and office. Their loss after 70 is material as well as religious.

### The measured teachers

Teachers associated with local assemblies and household law preserve oral interpretations, majority judgements and minority objections. They disagree over purity, food, marriage, debt, Sabbath travel, foreign rule and the authority of inherited custom. These groups provide the nearest Pharisaic grammar. They are not yet the sole leaders of the people.

### The written households

Some communities insist upon a narrower textual rule and reject large bodies of oral precedent. Wealthy priestly houses and provincial readers may both take this position for different reasons. Their traditions do not all disappear after the sanctuary falls.

### Wilderness and expectation communities

Ascetic settlements, apocalyptic readers and separatist households withdraw from the city's compromises. Some await heavenly war. Some practise strict common property or ritual discipline. Their texts preserve fierce attacks on both imperial rule and corrupt Jirahar leadership.

### Militants and popular captains

Anti-imperial groups range from tax resisters to assassins and armed rural bands. They share no single programme. During the revolt, some kill collaborators and moderate rivals. Their claim to defend the sanctuary does not sanctify factional murder.

## Isfir and the divided people

Isfir's life occurs within this plural society. A controlling council faction led by Ezrahel bar Qoreth presses Atheria for his execution; Atheria carries it out. His resurrection and the Silence divide Jirahar interpretation without ending Jirahar history.

Some followers of Isfir remain within Jirahar households for generations. Others form distinct congregations with non-Jirahar converts. Some teachers oppose them through argument; some officials denounce them; some neighbours shelter them during later persecution. The emerging Church and the Jirahar people remain closely entangled before imperial power hardens the distinction.

The hereditary guilt doctrine used under Vestian is false. Responsibility belongs to the people who chose, ordered and performed particular acts. The continued existence of the Jirahar never becomes a metaphysical error requiring state correction.

## The Great Tower Revolt, 66–73 AR

### Cause

The revolt begins through accumulated imperial and local failure. Atherian governors extort money, seize pilgrim wealth and permit or commit violence around the sanctuary. The Silence produces rival claims about sacrifice and foreign rule. Jirahar elites compete for office. Armed groups punish alleged collaborators. A sacrilegious imperial act and the refusal of compromise turn unrest into rebellion.

### Early victory

Rebels expel the garrison and defeat the first relief force. The success convinces many that Atheria can be driven from the eastern province. Coins, public prayers and new offices declare restored freedom. Communities that feared rebellion now face demands for food, men and loyalty.

### Civil conflict

Priestly houses, aristocratic moderates, popular captains and zealots fight over command. Militants kill opponents and seize supplies. Rival factions burn or destroy stores to deny them to one another. People attempting to escape are detained as traitors. These choices worsen the famine which the imperial siege later imposes.

### Imperial reconquest

Atheria retakes the province town by town. Villages are burned; prisoners are executed, enslaved or forced into military service. The army isolates Qiryath's river approaches and builds siege works around the city. Hunger, disease and factional violence kill civilians before the final assault.

### Second Destruction

In 70 AR the legions enter Qiryath. Fighting, massacre and fire destroy the upper tower and sanctuary. Sacred objects are taken for imperial triumph. Survivors are sold, ransomed, impressed or dispersed. The child collections associated with the Gateway have no place here; the event is an imperial siege of a rebellious province and the destruction of its religious centre.

The final cliff fortress falls in 73. Competing accounts preserve murder-suicide under fanatic leadership, imperial killing and heroic refusal. The mass death is fixed; later speeches are not.

## What the destruction changes

The sanctuary priesthood loses the place in which its central service can be performed. Sacrifice is not transferred to every local tower. The Silence that began after Isfir remains. Priestly descent and knowledge continue, but their public authority becomes narrower and often symbolic.

Assemblies, households, teachers and travelling courts take on work once coordinated through Qiryath. They organise prayer, burial, food rules, marriage, charity, calendars and relations with host governments. Communities preserve the sanctuary in liturgy and longing rather than declaring it irrelevant.

Diaspora communities absorb refugees. This creates conflict as well as solidarity. Older residents possess property, local languages and established bargains with host rulers. New arrivals may be poor, traumatised and politically suspect. Some refugees accuse settled communities of cowardice; some hosts fear that militants will bring imperial punishment.

## The long formation of the Peshara

### First period: emergency continuities, 70–c. 160 AR

Teachers preserve rulings needed for life without a functioning sanctuary. How should vows be treated when the promised offering cannot be made? Which purity rules remain? How can a household observe pilgrimage feasts far from Qiryath? What ransom is owed for an enslaved rebel and what happens when the captive endangered the community?

No single academy controls the answers. Local collections circulate as remembered debates, notebooks, letters and teaching sequences. Priestly, measured, textual and diaspora traditions remain distinct.

### Second period: gathered rulings, c. 160–260 AR

Several respected teacher circles begin arranging material by subject. The first widely received **Order of Measures** may serve as the World Egg analogue to the Mishnah: concise rulings, disputes and inherited cases organised for teaching. It should not be a transcript of one council or the complete practice of every Jirahar community.

The collection preserves named minorities because a rejected judgement may become useful under different conditions and because the victorious teacher may still be wrong. Stories, proverbs and moral examples accompany the rulings. These narrative portions make the Peshara a record of people arguing about survival rather than a code detached from life.

### Third period: regional conversations, c. 260–377 AR

Teachers in the old eastern homeland and in the larger river diaspora develop different commentarial traditions. Their discussions incorporate language, trade, medicine, agriculture and host law from the societies in which they live. The eastern and western collections cite one another imperfectly.

This is the best analogue to the two Talmuds. The Peshara should contain **more than one great regional recension**, produced over generations from teaching and argument. Their eventual names can come from the places where they take mature form, not automatically from Qiryath.

Rabbis in late antiquity were not immediately the rulers of every Jewish community. The same should hold here. Peshara teachers gain influence unevenly. Merchant elders, priestly houses, wealthy patrons, local councils, mystics and host-appointed officials remain competitors.

### Fourth period: Vestian rupture, 377–395 AR

After Atheria adopts the Redeemer's faith, court theologians and officials turn Ezrahel's factional guilt into an accusation against the whole people. Property hunger, debt, prophecy panic and religious zeal reinforce one another. Towers are seized or destroyed. Teachers are killed, expelled or forced to surrender collections. Christian neighbours sometimes protect Jirahar households; other congregations participate in the violence.

The destruction scatters schools as well as families. Pupils carry partial collections to Covenant, Eclessian, Thalmyric, Sestaran and Tarkhan territories. Some manuscripts survive because hospitals, merchants or sympathetic clergy conceal them. Others are reconstructed from memory and disagreement.

### Fifth period: diaspora redactions, c. 395–650 AR

Several regional traditions organise the inherited discussions after the persecution. One preserves the old eastern arguments. Another grows among western ports and road communities. A third develops under Covenant rule and addresses relations with a monotheistic society that honours Mujalib without becoming Jirahar.

These collections do not become identical. Later Keepers treat their disagreements as a feature of the tradition. The Peshara becomes the accumulated conversation among them rather than one closed volume issued in a single year.

## Language and learning

Jirahar communities should be multilingual. A sacred and learned language preserves the Scrolls. Regional vernaculars govern homes and markets. Atherian or successor languages serve administration and wider trade. Translation becomes both necessary and contested.

Teachers use repetition, paired argument, memorable cases and public reading. Advanced study remains demanding and often depends upon patrons. Literacy varies by wealth, sex, occupation and region. A culture that honours books still contains many people who receive the tradition through recitation, song, household custom and judgement rather than private reading.

Schools should not be imagined immediately as great formal academies. Early teacher circles may meet in houses, workshops, courtyards or attached rooms. Permanent institutions emerge where population, wealth and political protection allow them.

## Law, story and communal government

The mature Peshara contains several kinds of material:

- rulings and preserved dissents;
- arguments over the meaning and application of the Scrolls;
- accounts of teachers whose conduct complicates their teaching;
- household customs and regional exceptions;
- legal queries sent between distant communities;
- stories of persecution, rescue, folly and hospitality;
- medicine, calendar, agriculture and craft knowledge where they affect religious life;
- warnings against judges, patrons and majorities who mistake power for truth.

This body of thought supports Jirahar communal government without turning every Keeper into a priest or every tower into a state. Courts gain authority because communities recognise their learning and because host rulers grant charters. Either foundation can fail.

## Hard edges which must remain

- Jirahar militants kill rivals and worsen the siege famine.
- Atheria destroys the city, kills civilians and enslaves captives.
- The sanctuary is not quietly retired after Isfir; its loss is violent and irreversible.
- Some refugees are rejected, exploited or accused by other Jirahar communities.
- Vestian persecution is undertaken by a converted empire and includes confiscation, expulsion, murder and the destruction of sacred places.
- Jirahar teachers can become coercive, protect wealthy patrons or use communal exclusion unjustly.
- Later achievement does not turn catastrophe into a necessary good.

## Relationship to Atherian conversion

Current canon fixes legalization of the Redeemer's faith in 292 AR and compulsory conversion from 380. The Second Destruction in 70 therefore occurs under the older imperial order, more than two centuries before state conversion. This sequence should remain.

It produces a stronger history than moving the sanctuary's destruction after conversion. Pagan Atherian power destroys Qiryath during revolt. The later converted empire then turns a Christian theological dispute into inherited accusation and attacks the institutions built after the first catastrophe. The Jirahar consequently remember both imperial Rome-like destruction and Christian-imperial persecution as distinct wounds.

Josephus remains indispensable for the revolt and Second Temple groups, but he wrote as participant, survivor and client of the victorious dynasty. The New Testament is sacred Scripture within the setting's controlling Christian lens and also contains polemical encounters. Later rabbinic texts preserve teaching and constructed lineage rather than a neutral chronicle. These differences must remain visible in the source review.
