# Alexander the Great — Conquest, Kingship, Succession and Legend

Alexander III of Macedon inherited a kingdom, an army and a Greek hegemony created principally by his father, Philip II. Between 334 and 323 BC he used those resources to destroy the Achaemenid Empire, campaign beyond its former eastern limits and establish personal command over lands extending from Macedonia and Egypt to the Indus. His death at Babylon left no adult successor and no settled means of governing the whole. The wars among his officers produced several durable monarchies and a connected Hellenistic world which survived the conqueror's empire.

This distinction is essential for the World Egg comparison. Alexander's historical function comprises three related stories: the father who builds the instrument of conquest, the young king who carries it beyond every expected limit, and the Successors who turn a dead man's appointments, treasury and family into rival states. A translation which keeps only the undefeated young warrior loses most of his historical importance.

## Evidence and the problem of the sources

Alexander's contemporary court and campaign histories are lost. The main surviving narratives were written centuries later by Diodorus, Curtius Rufus, Plutarch, Arrian and Justin, drawing upon earlier works whose purposes and reliability differed. Arrian often supplies the narrative framework because he used accounts attributed to participants such as Ptolemy and Aristobulus, but his preference for royal and officer testimony does not make him neutral. Plutarch states that he is writing a study of character rather than a consecutive political history. Curtius and the traditions behind Diodorus preserve valuable material alongside dramatic shaping and hostile court anecdotes.

Waldemar Heckel's survey of the evidence distinguishes a popular narrative tradition from the more apologetic tradition preserved by Arrian and warns against dividing the witnesses into wholly reliable and worthless groups. Contemporary inscriptions, administrative texts, coins and archaeology correct the literary accounts without supplying a complete alternative narrative. The source problem should therefore become part of the World Egg inheritance: royal journals, veterans' accounts, hostile city histories, eastern records and later romances ought to disagree because their makers witnessed different portions of the same conquest.

## Philip's kingdom and the inherited instrument

Macedonia before Philip was already a settled and regionally varied kingdom. Its court connected lowland agriculture, upland communities, mines, timber, horses and neighbouring aristocracies. Ancient descriptions of Philip turning destitute wanderers into city dwellers belong partly to later royal self-presentation. Archaeology shows deeper settlement, religious and economic histories.

Philip nevertheless changed the scale of Macedonian power. He secured mines and approaches, reorganised military service, enlarged the use of the long-pike infantry and combined it with cavalry, lighter troops, engineers and siege equipment. Marriage, hostage relationships, bribery and the education of aristocratic sons at court joined conquest in holding the kingdom together. His victories culminated in hegemony over most Greek states through the League of Corinth. The league supplied a language of common war against Persia while leaving Macedon as the power able to enforce it.

Alexander had already served as regent and fought at Chaeronea before Philip's assassination in 336. He succeeded at about twenty amid uncertainty concerning rival heirs and the royal household. Executions and politically convenient deaths removed potential competitors. His early campaigns restored authority in the north and crushed a renewed Greek revolt. The destruction of Thebes in 335 killed defenders and civilians, enslaved survivors and gave the other cities a reason to submit before the Asian expedition began.

The useful grammar is therefore inheritance under pressure. The young king's brilliance matters because a murdered father has left him an army, an unfinished continental project, dangerous relatives, coerced allies and officers old enough to remember when he was a child.

## The conquest of the Achaemenid Empire

Alexander crossed into Asia in 334 with Macedonian troops, allied contingents, Greek forces and a campaign staff inherited from Philip. The first victory at the Granicus opened western Anatolia. The campaign combined battles with the negotiation of cities, taxes and provincial appointments. Local governments were sometimes retained, while Macedonian or Greek officers received military and financial authority. There was no single policy of liberation or replacement.

Darius III met Alexander at Issus in 333 and escaped after defeat. His mother, wife and children fell into Alexander's custody. Their treatment became an important part of the conqueror's royal presentation: control of the rival king's household could display restraint, succession and superiority at once. Darius offered settlements which would have recognised major territorial gains. Alexander continued the war for the whole monarchy.

The sieges of Tyre and Gaza in 332 expose the cost of that decision. Tyre's island position required the construction of a causeway and the assembly of a larger fleet. Its capture ended in killing and mass enslavement. Gaza possessed stores and hired defenders; Alexander could have continued past it, but its resistance and the danger to his communications made submission personally and strategically important. Ancient accounts preserve the killing of defenders, sale of women and children and severe punishment of its commander. The proposed World Egg counterpart requires at least one defended city whose inhabitants pay for the conqueror's refusal to leave defiance behind him.

In Egypt, Alexander accepted existing royal forms and travelled to the oracle of Ammon at Siwa. The surviving accounts do not disclose one certain answer. The journey nevertheless contributed to later claims about divine paternity and world kingship. Alexandria and other foundations joined garrisons, settlers, administrators and trade to his name.

Alexander defeated Darius decisively at Gaugamela in 331 and entered Babylon, Susa and Persepolis. The destruction by fire of the Persepolis palace remains disputed in motive and sequence. It could be represented as vengeance for the Persian invasion of Greece, an intoxicated act, a calculated end to the old court or some combination. Treasure seized from the royal centres financed continuing war and changed the monetary life of the eastern Mediterranean.

When Darius was arrested and killed by his own eastern officers, Alexander treated the dead ruler as a royal predecessor and pursued Bessus as a regicide and usurper. Conquest had changed its public meaning. The Macedonian avenger of Greece now claimed to inherit the Persian king's authority.

## The eastern wars and the changing court

The campaigns in Bactria and Sogdiana lasted longer than the initial destruction of Persian field armies. Local rulers possessed fortified places, mobile forces and relationships which did not disappear with Darius. Macedonian reprisals, new settlements, hostage arrangements and alliances accompanied repeated revolt. Alexander married Roxane, daughter of the Sogdian noble Oxyartes, after taking a fortified refuge. The marriage served personal and political purposes; it did not end resistance.

The king increasingly used parts of Achaemenid court ceremony, retained some Iranian officials and recruited Iranian soldiers. These decisions could help govern conquered populations and make his claim to the Asian monarchy intelligible. They also angered Macedonian officers who understood victory as the source of their privilege. Attempts to introduce proskynesis, the training of eastern youths and the later admission of Persians to honoured military and court relationships intensified the conflict.

Alexander's treatment of intimates became part of the political crisis. Philotas was executed after a conspiracy inquiry, and his father Parmenion—one of Philip's senior commanders—was killed before he could react. Alexander killed Cleitus during a drunken quarrel, although Cleitus had saved his life at the Granicus. Callisthenes, the court historian associated with opposition to proskynesis, died after arrest. Ancient writers disagree over many details, but the pattern is secure: the army conquered an empire while the king destroyed relationships which had connected his reign to his father's generation.

The expedition entered the Punjab and defeated Porus at the Hydaspes. Alexander restored Porus and enlarged his authority, turning a defeated ruler into a regional ally. At the Hyphasis in 326, the army refused to continue east. This was the effective limit of conquest. The king could kill individuals and punish units; he could not march an exhausted army indefinitely against its collective refusal.

The return included the disastrous Gedrosian march. Shortage, heat, distance and poor preparation killed soldiers, servants and camp followers. The Susa marriages of 324 joined Alexander and many senior officers to Iranian royal and noble women. Iranian troops entered the army in greater numbers. At Opis, veterans resisted discharge and the apparent loss of Macedonian precedence. Alexander first confronted and punished the mutiny, then reconciled with the army through pay, honour and a public redefinition of kinship.

## Death and the wars of the Successors

Alexander returned to Babylon while preparing further expeditions and administrative changes. He died there in 323 after a short illness, aged thirty-two. Ancient poisoning stories never acquired sufficient evidence to displace illness as the stronger explanation. He left Roxane pregnant, an adult half-brother whose capacity to rule was contested, several royal women and generals commanding armies across immense distances. The reported answer that the empire should pass “to the strongest” belongs to later tradition and cannot serve as a secure testament.

The army itself entered the succession dispute. Senior cavalry officers proposed waiting for Roxane's child; infantry supported Alexander's half-brother Arrhidaeus. The compromise made Arrhidaeus, as Philip III, and the posthumous Alexander IV joint kings under regents. Both kings became instruments of the commanders and were later murdered. Roxane, Alexander's mother Olympias and other royal women exercised political power and also ordered or suffered killings within the dynastic struggle.

The commanders first governed provinces in the kings' names. Their alliances, marriages and territorial allocations repeatedly changed. Ptolemy secured Egypt and Alexander's body; Seleucus eventually established a vast eastern monarchy; Antigonus attempted to restore unity; Cassander dominated Macedonia; Lysimachus ruled Thrace and adjoining lands. Warfare between 323 and 281 killed the remaining Argead heirs and made personal military success a stronger basis of kingship than descent from Alexander.

The resulting kingdoms were not debris awaiting Rome. Their courts founded and enlarged cities, supported libraries and workshops, moved soldiers and settlers, used local institutions and created new forms of Greek, Egyptian, Iranian and other regional life. Coins and court art carried Alexander's image long after his family had been eliminated. The most durable political consequence of his career was therefore a plural successor world.

## The conqueror in later story

Historical biography began turning into romance soon after Alexander's death. Greek, Syriac, Armenian, Persian, Arabic, Hebrew, Latin and vernacular traditions made him a philosopher-king, world traveller, destroyer, seeker of immortality or guardian against peoples beyond the known frontier. The Alexander Romance supplied journeys beneath the sea and into the air, speaking trees, monstrous peoples, the water of life and a gate confining a terrible enemy. Different religious traditions recast his claimed divinity, ancestry and moral purpose.

This afterlife is particularly valuable to the World Egg. One historical conqueror can support several incompatible cycles without any one culture owning the complete man. Atherian singers may remember a Star-born hero. Jirahar accounts may preserve the destroyer of Arqesh alongside a ruler who permitted return. Vashari court poetry may make him Arezan's brilliant but undisciplined ally. Qahuri stories may dwell upon the oracle, the dead, treasure and the limits of worldly kingship. Distant romances may send him into the Egg White, Faewild or constructed Shell without proving that the human person ever entered those realms.

## Narrative functions worth translating

The World Egg figure should preserve the following connected functions:

1. **A father-made instrument.** The conqueror inherits a reorganised kingdom, army, league and unfinished war.
2. **A contested young accession.** Household rivals and rebellious allies test him before the overseas campaign.
3. **Personal speed and physical courage.** He wins loyalty by sharing marches, wounds and danger.
4. **A city destroyed as warning.** Submission elsewhere is purchased by one community's death and enslavement.
5. **Conquest changing its own justification.** A war announced as retaliation becomes a claim to inherit the enemy's universal monarchy.
6. **A rival king's household.** Captive relatives, royal burial and dynastic marriage become instruments of legitimacy.
7. **A changing mixed court.** The conqueror adopts useful practices and people from the defeated empire, angering the soldiers who believe victory belongs to them.
8. **The murder of intimates.** Suspicion and rage destroy officers who built or saved the regime.
9. **The army's limit.** Soldiers ultimately refuse a further march.
10. **A fatal return.** The king reaches the administrative centre and dies before completing government or naming a workable heir.
11. **Successor kingdoms.** Officers, queens, local allies and royal children fight over an empire held together principally by one person.
12. **A world-spanning romance.** Later peoples turn incomplete knowledge into adventures at the limits of the cosmos.

The hard edges are equally indispensable: execution of rivals, destruction and enslavement of cities, mass death during campaigning, killing of a friend, elimination of an experienced general, coercive settlement, dynastic marriage, the suffering of camp followers, and the murder of heirs by Successors. Military brilliance cannot erase these acts.
