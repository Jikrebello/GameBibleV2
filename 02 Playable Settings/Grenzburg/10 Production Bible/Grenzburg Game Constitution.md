# Grenzburg Game Constitution

This note is the highest production authority for the Grenzburg game. It controls the experience being built, its scale, its tone, and the rules that every later design wave must obey. Lore references remain authoritative about the setting; when an older Grenzburg production note conflicts with this constitution, this constitution controls the game.

## The Game in One Paragraph

Grenzburg is a silent-protagonist, party-based, open-world action role-playing game set across one frontier duchy during a year of closing roads, winter siege, spring reclamation, and summer consequence. The player is an outsider debtor serving in the Medori Bank's Blackjack Corps and temporarily assigned as a specialist to Duke Alaric's war effort. They are useful enough to reach places ordinary soldiers cannot, but they do not command the army or rule the city. Human ambition, hunger, weather, debt, faith, land rights, Grobi migration, a territorial drake, and a separately waking Qianglong war complex converge without sharing one mastermind.

## Product Promise

- **Critical path:** 35-45 hours across twenty-one main chapters.
- **Broad playthrough:** more than 100 hours when faction, companion, marquee, local, dungeon, and job content is pursued.
- **World:** Grenzburg city and seven connected exterior subregions, all traversable as one changing region.
- **Time:** seasons advance only at major player-controlled story milestones.
- **Party:** the player plus three active companions selected from eight full companions.
- **Aftermath:** summer remains playable and reflects casualties, settlement states, faction strength, debt, companion resolutions, and political control.
- **Continuity:** the Deep Dragon is always stopped in a completed campaign. No political ending is yet the canonical future of the setting.

## Audience and Tone

The target player wants a serious fantasy world that rewards curiosity, preparation, travel, combat mastery, and attention to people. The game may be frightening, tragic, funny, intimate, or spectacular, but it should not become cynical sludge. Good and evil are real. Institutions contain sincere servants, cowards, hypocrites, reformers, and predators without becoming morally interchangeable.

Grenzburg should feel materially believable before it feels cosmic. Bread, firewood, roads, walls, debts, wounded soldiers, refugees, bad harvests, and personal loyalties make the crisis legible. Supernatural scale enters by degrees: dangerous fauna and old ruins first, the Emerald Drake in winter, residue armies in spring, and the true Deep Dragon only after the player understands what ordinary life stands to lose.

## Reference-Game Lessons

These are craft lessons, not sources of names, plots, factions, or lore.

- **Skyrim:** readable exploration, strong place identity, faction lines that can be pursued beside the main story, and a world that invites detours.
- **The Witcher 3:** authored quests built around concrete people, legible motives, investigation, consequence, and monsters grounded in local circumstances.
- **Dragon's Dogma:** physical monster encounters, strong vocation identity, party composition, travel danger, and combat discoveries made through play.
- **Grand Theft Auto's mission rhythm:** a clear main skeleton opens new people, districts, systems, and side opportunities; time advances at major missions while the player owns the space between them.

## Design Pillars

### 1. A Frontier Worth Saving

Grenzburg is not merely a crisis delivery system. Settlements, roads, workshops, shrines, farms, inns, families, and local feuds must exist in sufficient detail that survival has an object.

### 2. Grounded Before Strange

Bandits, deserters, predators, Grobi raids, hunger, bad authority, and ordinary dungeons introduce the setting. New terminology is taught through action and repetition. No opening hour asks the player to memorize a cosmology lecture.

### 3. Independent Causes, Converging Pressure

The Grobi siege, Emerald Drake, urban faction struggle, and Deep Muster have separate causes. Characters may exploit another crisis, but no hidden hand explains everything. Coincidence must arise from geography, season, and prior history rather than authorial convenience.

### 4. The Player Is a Specialist

The protagonist rescues, scouts, negotiates, hunts, delves, sabotages, and breaks decisive positions. Generals decide campaigns and institutions make policy. The player changes those decisions through proven action, not because every adult waits for an unknown debtor to govern them.

### 5. Consequence Lives in the World

Quest outcomes alter patrols, prices, refugees, services, settlement survival, faction presence, companion scenes, enemy routes, and summer conditions. Dialogue acknowledgements alone are not sufficient consequence.

### 6. Failure Changes the Problem

A failed objective should produce casualties, occupation, pursuit, scarcity, a harder route, a changed ally, or a later reckoning. It should not usually erase the quest or demand a reload.

### 7. Gameplay Can Carry Simplicity

Not every quest needs a moral labyrinth. A clear hunt, rescue, ruin-clearing, escort, duel, or recovery is valuable when its route, enemies, space, reward, and local context are strong.

### 8. Major Quests Need Human Stakes

Complex quests begin with a person or community in a specific difficulty. Lore supports the conflict; it does not replace it. Every major supernatural problem must still answer who suffers, who benefits, and why the player acts now.

### 9. Companions Are People Before Positions

Companions may disagree sharply, but they remain personable and capable of warmth, humor, competence, embarrassment, loyalty, and change. Constant snark, gratuitous hostility, and interchangeable trauma are prohibited shortcuts.

### 10. Escalation Must Be Earned

The final Deep Dragon should feel impossible in Act I because the player has not yet been taught the history, systems, places, allies, or powers required to face it. Every act must prepare part of that understanding without announcing the final answer too early.

## Worldspace Contract

Wave 2 will lock borders, routes, hubs, and names. Wave 1 fixes the playable functions of seven exterior subregions:

1. the Northern Road and autumn Funnel;
2. the western lake country and Chapel-Folk roads;
3. Wolfsmere and its forest kindreds;
4. the Southwood and old barrow country;
5. the Highlake uplands and Tuskway approaches;
6. Timberfalls and the upper River Grenz;
7. the Fenn Road and deep southern march.

Each subregion requires a settlement or service hub, a strong visual identity, a principal traversal problem, at least one major dungeon, seasonal changes, faction presence, and reasons to return after its first main-story use.

## Content Budget

| Content family | Target | Role |
|---|---:|---|
| Critical-path chapters | 21 | Advance seasons, crises, systems, and campaign payoffs. |
| Summer aftermath quests | 2 | Resolve world state and reopen the region after the finale. |
| Deep faction lines | 5 lines of 8-10 quests | Long institutional stories parallel to the main path. |
| Companion personal quests | 32 | Four quests for each of eight companions, plus reactive scenes. |
| Marquee authored quests | 24 | Large local stories with investigation, character, and consequence. |
| Smaller local quests | 36 | Focused authored stories, discoveries, and practical problems. |
| Job templates | 12 | Gameplay-forward hunts, escorts, bounties, patrols, and recoveries. |

Counts are scope controls rather than an instruction to pad the game. Content earns its place by introducing, testing, complicating, or paying off a location, system, character, faction, or world state.

## Retired Main-Campaign Assumptions

The following material is not part of the active main campaign:

- Heart-Stone custody and theft as the three-act spine;
- a Pale-Skin or Hollow-King conspiracy behind Grenzburg's crises;
- a confidential Bank rider attached to every main mission;
- Leviticus as the mandatory winter betrayer;
- the Drake's heart-gem as a required final key;
- a predetermined Duke's Peace or any other fixed political ending.

The Heart-Stone and its barrow remain available for a later optional high-level side adventure. Pale-Skins remain part of the wider setting but do not cause or direct Grenzburg's campaign.

## Change Rule

A later wave may tune numbers and add detail. It may not quietly contradict a fixed rule here. Any change to the protagonist premise, main crisis, season model, party size, relationship model, campaign completion condition, or content budget requires an explicit constitution revision and a dated archive snapshot.
