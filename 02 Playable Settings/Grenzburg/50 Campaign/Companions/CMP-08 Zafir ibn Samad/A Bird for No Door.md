# A Bird for No Door

## Premise

Zafir has built a reed-and-wire bird that can lift a latch. There is no door it needs to open.

He invites the player to help construct an obstacle course of entirely unnecessary latches across Longlake Ward and the Yard.

No villain, theft, emergency, confession, or moral ambush interrupts the afternoon.

## The Course

The pair gather:

- a reed light enough for the wings;
- a discarded spring from Hammer Ward;
- waxed cord;
- three latches that no longer belong to doors;
- one ridiculous prize chosen by the player.

They build five stations: lift, hook, counterweight, false latch, and final bell.

## Play

The player may:

- follow Zafir's plan;
- alter the bird;
- build a deliberately unfair station;
- wager on failure;
- let a nearby child name the bird;
- refuse every technical explanation and still participate.

The bird must fail at least twice. Those failures are authored comic beats rather than skill-check punishment.

Zafir becomes competitive with his own previous prediction. If the player solves a station by lifting the latch by hand, he protests, considers the rule, and admits he never forbade it.

## Conversation

The private exchange concerns being wanted beyond usefulness.

Zafir says:

> “I thought an object without use would be wasteful. It appears I object only when I am not enjoying the waste.”

The player may respond through humor, rivalry, affection, quiet attention, or a practical criticism. No line diagnoses him.

## Outcome

- The bird may complete the course, fail magnificently, or be modified into something stranger.
- The prize goes to the player, Zafir, Karel, a child judge, or the bird.
- The model appears later at camp and in the summer coda.

No outcome changes moral direction. The scene deepens friendship because nothing has to be earned from it.
