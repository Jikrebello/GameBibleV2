# The Reed-Crown Feast

The Reed-Crown Feast is Merewick's annual Reaping Week celebration, held about three weeks before Souls-month. Most of its customs are ordinary harvest recreation, public gratitude, courtship, household memory, and preparation for winter. The First Bowl Keepers hide a sacrificial procession behind its final night.

## Public Day

- **Raised-Way Race:** paired runners carry a basket across wet planks.
- **Eel-Pot Draw:** villagers judge full traps by movement and weight.
- **Reed-Torch Leap:** adults promise to carry stored food through winter.
- **Seed-Tale Contest:** three young people recount household histories and planting knowledge.
- **Common Table:** smokehouse food, rye, beans, mushrooms, roots, eel, and ale.
- **Reed Crown:** one Feast Daughter is chosen to carry first seed to the chapel at dawn.

These customs can remain after the cult is destroyed. A community does not have to surrender music, games, hospitality, or harvest memory because murderers used them as cover.

## Concealed Night

After the public table, ordinary households shutter lamps during the **longest night**, supposedly to protect children's dreams. The First Bowl Keepers use the quiet to move the selected victim, [[The First Bowl]], and procession crews to [[Hollow Acre]].

The Feast Daughter is inducted, disappeared, or sacrificed depending upon whether an outsider or dissenter has been secured. Families are told that asking which fate occurred endangers the next harvest.

## Adventure Clues

- old seed tales omit every vanished Feast Daughter;
- green-glazed serving copies leave diners hungry early;
- procession knots face Hollow Acre rather than the chapel;
- Reed Crowns remain locked in Holt House after wearers supposedly departed;
- hidden distribution marks give Keepers larger winter shares;
- the reed-torch fire later lights the sacrificial path.

## After the Cult

The feast can be abolished, purified, divided into separate church and village events, or retained without Crown and longest-night customs. No outcome is canon. Any continued public meal must include rather than exclude the households whose hunger fed the old architecture.
