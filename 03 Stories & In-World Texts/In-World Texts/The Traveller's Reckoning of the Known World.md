# The Traveller's Reckoning of the Known World

*Scholar's apparatus: printed at Sermora in 1360 AR by its author, Ugo Tessarin, chartmaker and factor, and sold over the counter of his shop at the sign of the Crooked Compass on the Pilots' Steps, beside the charts it so often recommends. The text follows a copy that crossed the Thalassian into Thalmyric hands. Its margins carry notes in a single hand which signs itself, when it signs at all, Akakios, lector of Kolonna. The notes are undated and are given here as indented passages. Sermoran counting-houses shelve the book as a reference and Thalmyric seminaries as a warning.*

---

*The Traveller's Reckoning of the Known World: being an honest account of its roads, tolls, tables, saints, swindles and weather, for the use of factors, pilgrims, captains and all other persons obliged to leave home; with notes on who owes whom. By Ugo Tessarin of Sermora, chartmaker, in the year 1360 after the Redeemer.*

## To the Reader

I have drawn charts for thirty-one years and sold them for thirty. The first year went in learning that a chart is worth exactly what the buyer thinks the coast is worth, and that he will think it worth more if you draw a sea-serpent in the margin. In those years I have been robbed on four seas, blessed in five rites, sued in three courts and married once, in Orsena, which involved all three. I have sold the same coast to two admirals at war with each other and given both of them the true soundings, because a chartmaker who lies about rocks does not stay in business, and neither do his customers.

This book is the rest of what I know. It is not about rocks but about the people who live beside them: what they eat, whom they pray to, whom they owe, and what they will do to you if you get any of the three wrong. It is for the factor sent abroad by a house that could not spare a better man, and for anyone else with a reason to go somewhere and the sense to be frightened of it. Some rules first, learned the expensive way.

**Everyone is the heir of Atheria.** The Thalmyrics hold its old capital, the Eclessians its crown, the Covenant its northern provinces and the archive of its embarrassments, and we Sestarans hold its debts, which is the only part of an inheritance that reliably pays. Agree with each heir while you stand on his ground. It costs nothing, and the alternative costs a great deal.

**Know who owes whom.** In this year of 1360 there is scarcely a court in the world that does not owe a Sestaran house for a war, for its dead or for its passage, and scarcely a Sestaran house that does not owe another one for the same three things. A traveller who knows the debts knows the politics. A traveller who does not will learn them at a customs post.

**Carry four purses.** Sermoran silver for the sea and the ports; Medori paper for the courts and anywhere a Fiorenzian factor keeps a desk; a letter from a Jirahar house of Dahava for anywhere a Jirahar quarter will honour it, which is more places than the Medori like to admit; and bread for the Folk, who value it above the other three, and are right to.

> *Margin, in a Thalmyric hand:* And a lamp for Thalmyria. He forgets the lamp. A traveller who comes into a Thalmyric house at dusk without asking to greet the lamp-niche will be fed, because we are a hospitable people, and discussed for a year, because we are a thorough one. — Akakios, lector, Kolonna

**Count the years correctly.** The churches count from the Redeemer, and this is 1360. The Covenant counts from Mujalib's revelation, which makes it the year 819 in Iqashar, and a factor who dates a Covenant contract by the wrong count may find he has agreed to deliver his cargo five centuries ago, and that a Covenant court will hold him to it. The Jirahar count from their Prophet's death and the Folk by kings and winters, and both will explain at length, so do not ask if you have a tide to catch.

**On wonders.** I believe in the Lord, the Redeemer and the Fire, and in the Admiralty's tide-tables, in that order of confidence on dry land and the reverse at sea. What I do not believe in is every weeping statue, every talking reliquary and every lamp that a man with a collection box assures me has just kindled. The world is full of real wonders and fuller of people selling them. When I tell you in this book that I saw a thing, I saw it. When I tell you I was told, weigh the teller as I did. When I tell you what I think the explanation is, you may laugh. My readers in Thalmyria will.

> *Margin:* We do.

**On held proofs.** You will hear the phrase in every capital, usually lowered. A held proof is the thing a ruler or a church keeps in a vault and will not test, because a test might win and might lose and nobody can say which beforehand. Like a good bond, it is worth more shut than open. Every power in the world keeps at least one. I have noted the famous ones where they occur, so that you will know which subjects to avoid at table.

## Thalmyria, Which Was Here First

Thalmyria will tell you it was here first, and in fairness it was, if by *here* you mean the mountains and old cities of the Atherian heartland and by *first* you mean before its neighbours began keeping their own accounts. It lies west of Eclessia over a mountain watershed, south of the Covenant over a desert edge, and across the Thalassian from my own city, which is the direction most of its money comes from.

**The land.** Volcanic highland falls to warm lowland. The Aurin runs down to the northwestern coast at Caleran past olive groves, hard wheat and vineyards planted in black ash, which give the black wine drunk wherever the churches argue, which is to say everywhere. Above the trees are sheep, barley, and honey from meadows in old craters. Between the cities lie terraces nobody has worked since the Fall and occupation ghost towns built by the Covenant for people who are no longer there. The Thalmyrics leave this empty interior off their maps, so I have put it on mine. The chancellery at Veridun bought three copies of my sixth sheet and denied any need for it in the same letter.

**The history as I understand it.** The Atherian Empire did not fall in a day. It came apart over five centuries while the palace performed its rites and wrote in the calendar that the ones which failed had been postponed. A Thalmyric refuge chronicler named this the Guttering: *in the palace the lamps guttered, and in the passes they burned.* An Eclessian will call the same centuries the Unexamined Centuries and tell you that nobody was checking. I will tell you about the Copper Lamp. In 681 the accession lamp of Tavoran II flared white in his cathedral because a Sestaran warlock was crouched in the gallery with a brass fire-cup. Unpaid after eighteen months, he sold the cup and his account of the night to a Covenant factor at Sermora. I tell this with a patriot's pride. It is the only story of the fall of Atheria in which somebody kept proper accounts.

> *Margin:* He kept the accounts. The Covenant kept the cup. The court kept the lie. Two centuries later Covenant jurists stood outside Caleran reading the city its own charter, district by district, and the city fell. Be proud of that, Sestaran.

Caleran fell in 905. The refugees went south into the volcanic mountains, and over Veridun, they say, the smoke of the city's ovens lay for five days, as long as the bread lasted, while the besiegers dreamed of home and went there, and the Thalmyrics keep the Feast of the Veil under arms with their oven doors open. Caedros the Unbowed was crowned at Virelos in 922, took Caleran back in 957 and died at the Pillar of Concordance in the hour of victory, and the soldiers who were there swear that the Fire took his body. The rest of the heartland took until 1052, and a good deal of Sermoran shipping.

In 1227 the Sanctum offered the emperor Nikandros the Crown of Embers itself, with lances thrown in, if the Great Lamp of Veridun might be tried by its relic court. *A lamp that is tried is a lamp that is owned,* said Nikandros the Restorer, and sent the crown's envoys home with the cord they had brought to measure his head. I note only that Veridun will not let the Sanctum test its lamp, Aurelium will not let anyone see the psalter it says it does not have, and both call this fidelity.

> *Margin:* There is a difference between a lamp and a book that would not burn. Ours was never in question. — A.

**Caleran.** The old capital stands at the Aurin's mouth: three hundred and fifty thousand souls in an ordinary year and nearer half a million in a pilgrim season, when the inns charge by the hour and the pickpockets by the district. Patriarch Sophron the Unsleeping sits here, though his title is still Patriarch of Veridun. The Eternal Ember burns in the precinct of the martyrs. Under the Covenant it burned for fifty-two years as a deposit, by a ruling Idris sent by courier from Iqashar, for he never saw the city taken in his name. Its Covenant keepers pledged their own names for it and kept a ledger of its oil and their watches to the day. In the last spring of the occupation their own amir sent his guard to carry the lamp down to his ships. The keepers barred the basilica against him and eleven of them died at the lamp, and the ledger records that the oil was filled that night as usual. When the Thalmyrics came back in 957 they found the Ember alight and an invoice beside it. They paid it in full. I have tried for thirty years to collect debts with half that clarity.

> *Margin:* We paid it so that no one could ever say the Ember had been theirs.

In old Caleran, in the Painted Reign, a portrait of the emperor answered petitioners in the emperor's own voice, and a magistrate ruled through it until a soldier threw his cloak over its face. Some old Thalmyric households will still not speak a dead ruler's name in front of his picture. I have examined three speaking images in my life, in three cities, and all three had a pipe in the back.

> *Margin:* Our Church teaches that something wore that face. Keep your pipe.

**Veridun.** The refuge in the mountains is the capital now. Its coronation cathedral holds the Great Lamp and the Virelian Lamp, which Veridun offers to return to Virelia at every royal election and which Virelia never quite manages to accept. The Emperor is Caedros IV Aetherion, called Ash-Blood, past sixty and a widower: the commander who held Chapel-Fort Orinel, took Zehrim, and was adopted by the old emperor Stavren after a summer flux killed Stavren's sons. Since the Empress Vasilia died in 1357 he has kept silences of a day and more before the Great Lamp, and courtiers say the flame stands still while he kneels. His elder son, Prince Nikandros, died in a skirmish in the Velian marches in 1358, and the court is divided between Princess Alexane, Prince Sophios, and those who would have the Emperor adopt a proven commander as Stavren adopted him. Asked whether the silences are grief, illness or policy, I answer that a ruler who has discovered he need not answer questions has made the best discovery available to his trade.

> *Margin:* Do not write of the Emperor's silences as a stratagem. You were not in the cathedral.
>
> *Margin, lower, same hand:* Nor was I.

**Orinel.** Every Thalmyric will tell you about the Night of Seven Flames. In 1318 the Covenant's Iron-mounted night riders came against Chapel-Fort Orinel, the fort's seven signal braziers went out one by one, and when the last was dark Caedros led the counterattack at dawn. The songs say the bound riders smothered the fires and a great bird of fire rose out of the smoke. A Saronikan quartermaster's ledger, found a generation later, records that the fort's oil had been bought from a contractor who cut it with water. I find the ledger the more persuasive document. It has columns.

> *Margin:* The oil was watered and the bird came anyway. Your ledgers cannot hold both. Ours can.

**Who owes whom.** The Crimson Pilgrimage and the siege of Zehrim were paid for with Sermoran and Medori loans against the customs of Saronika, and Veridun has been paying for thirty-five years. At Zehrim Caedros burned the garrison's oath-ledgers on the altar; the Covenant's Court of Breaches ruled that the people they bound are still bound in the registers at Iqashar; Thalmyria says they are free; and the people themselves farm and marry and wait to see who comes to collect them. Since the well at Qarath named its own price in 1214, a well for every cup, and the emperor Markellos's chancellor tried to leave the debt on a sick man in a monastery only to hear from Iqashar that the Throne had drunk and the Throne owed, no Thalmyric emperor will put his name to a Covenant instrument or accept a cup of water from a Covenant host, and the Covenant makes no peace without one. So the frontier has had only an armed pause since 1325, and a Sermoran notary in Qabir earns more than a bishop.

**Velos.** At the Stone Bridge of Velos in the eastern lowlands the emperor Cassian saw the White Flame in 292 and won his war, and the Aurin at the bridge has not frozen since. Cassian was baptised there, dying, in 307, which the Thalmyrics call the Cassian Wager and I call excellent timing. I have put my hand into that reach in a hard winter. It is as cold as any river and it does not freeze, and I am satisfied that a warm spring rises under the bridge, as warm springs rise under half the mountains of Thalmyria.

> *Margin:* Then tell me why your warm spring waited for the year Cassian saw the Fire.

**The provinces.** **Saronika**, under Queen Thaleia IV, is shipping, fishing and naval stores, and its customs are pledged to Sermoran lenders, so I recommend it warmly: it is in a sense partly ours. **Vlastrava**, under King Dragovan II, is fortresses, horses and veterans, and ever since a Vlastravan king refused, in the wars after Qarath, to sell a peace for an old vengeance, a Vlastravan's word has been the one contract on the frontier that needs no notary. **Elenara**, under Duchess Mirena Ielen, is wine, medicine and manuscripts, and the reason every census in the successor world must state its purpose: an Elenaran prince's census was once followed down its roads by a plague that stopped only at a threshing floor at Kremna. **Arkena**, under Governor-General Kyrion Arkenos, is navigators, bronze-founders and antiquities dealers. **Kedrava**, under Prince Radovan Kedrin, is cold mountains and monasteries, and its outer coast belongs to the Saltborn.

> *Margin:* True of the Vlastravan word. I have seen a Vlastravan horse-dealer refund money to a man who had cheated him.

**Eating and living.** The Thalmyrics believe they invented law, letters, theology, proper warfare and good cooking. They are right about one of these. Fast-day food is beans, greens, lentils, olives and bread, and there are a great many fast days. Every house keeps a lamp-niche with an icon and a wick that is never allowed to die; oaths are sworn on it and the dying are turned to face it. At the Feast of the Rekindling every lamp in the empire is put out at dusk and relit from one flame carried on foot from Caleran, hand to hand, and a household whose lamp fails that night does not speak of it. Buy antiquities only before a witness: a palace lamp that lights itself is Shell-fire, and Shell-fire, as an old warlock remarked at an emperor's funeral, smells of hot coin.

> *Margin:* It was cooking.

**Saints and wonders.** During the Sweat, wherever the priests died, Thalmyric villagers sang their own dead out, and they came through the plague convinced that the Fire answers the singer and not the office. In my thirtieth year I attended a funeral in Saronika where no priest could be had. The dead man's sister sang, the neighbours sang, and the lamp at the head of the bier, which no one had lit, was lit. I report it. I do not explain it. I have tried. The Thalmyrics venerate Caedros the Unbowed; Despina of Kolonna, the soldier's widow whose lamp took the Flame at Caedros III's funeral in 1159 while the lamps of seven claimants stayed dark and the old emperor's son was acclaimed on one that burned false; Saint Naris of the drowned; and Saint Gavrin of the Halter, of whom more among the Tarkhan.

> *Margin:* I was baptised in sight of the Widow's Lamp. Every year on the anniversary the district families carry their door-lamps to it, and nobody has to explain anything.

**Rumours.** That the Patriarch refuses to authenticate a vision of a lamp carried out of a burning archive, which every faction at court claims as its own. That the Emperor will adopt again. That he will not. That the Velian marches will be the next war, because they always are.

## Eclessia, Which Is an Argument with Borders

Eclessia lies east of Thalmyria beyond the watershed, a purple stain on every map, mine included, holding one border and a dozen quarrels, each party to which believes it is the true public face of the Redeemer's Church, except those that think the Church should have no public face at all. Eclessians share a calendar, a great deal of rye bread and a conviction that everything was better before the Sweat.

**How it came to be.** In 960 a march banner at the ford of Kaltfurt took the Flame in the hands of a miller's widow, Hedwen, as she stood in the water between Tarkhan riders and a column of refugee children. The commander watching from the bank was Adalmar. The Sanctum's relic court credited the sign to him, and when she was safely dead struck her testimony through. In 1015 Aurelium asked Veridun to lift the bronze hood off its Great Lamp and let the lamp be examined; the Patriarch nailed the hood down instead, with the legate listening on the steps, and the churches split. Two years later the Primarch crowned Adalmar in the ford itself, and the millers shut their sluices and left him standing in mud. No heir of his ever passed the examination. His grandsons drew lots at Drossfeld in 1027 to choose which of them should try, and drew a blank, and in 1104 the Crown of Embers went into the Sanctum's vault *until the realm is worthy of one head*. I have used the same phrase of my nephews, with less success.

**The Bells of the Split.** At the hour of the anathemas, according to letters from eleven towns along the watershed border, all written before the news could have reached them, church bells rang with no ringers. Each church records them as the other's bells, grieving, and each holds that the first to lift its anathema confesses the bells were its own. Neither has. A Sestaran acoustician has shown, to my satisfaction, that an earthquake running along the watershed would ring every bell in its path. No bell rang in Sestara, which proves either that the earthquake stopped politely at our border or that our bells had nothing to grieve about.

> *Margin:* I have read the letter of the Brauvalis prior. He says the bells were struck *from the inside*. Your acoustician was not there. They were Eclessian bells, and they were grieving for what Aurelium had done.

**The Sanctum and Aurelia.** Aurelium, in the deep south, is the holy city of the Kingdom of Aurelia, which the Lord Primarch rules directly as a king, and the seat of the Sanctum Divinitatis, where a Primarch's election was once bought on credit and repaid in letters for the dead that nobody sang. The Primarch is Alistair III, elected in 1354: a reformer who enforces the ban on routing assurances against a thriving grey market and is said to read Freeflame pamphlets in order to understand them rather than burn them, which in Aurelium counts as radicalism. Cardinal Marcellus Vrenne and the Order of the Phoenix oppose him. The Inquisition of Saint Malveris is on trial over the Closed House, one of its prisons, where witnesses say Saint Malveris himself, or something using his name, appeared, named the victims and refused his own order a sign.

Under Alistair's feet, in one set of vaults, lie the Crown of Embers; the Unburnt Psalter of Ansgar of Brenholt, which the Sanctum has denied possessing for sixty-one years; and the file on Vessing, on which it has declined to rule for eight. A Sermoran underwriter once offered me odds on which of the three would be opened first. I declined. You do not bet on a door when you do not know which side of it you are standing.

The relic courts are the Sanctum's pride. Since the weeping Lady of Bruckmoor was proved in 1243 to be an Idol's feeding-trough dressed as a saint, every Eclessian shrine has a grille between pilgrim and relic, a separate offering chest and an inspection door under court seal, so that no apparition and no custodian holds both the evidence and the income. It is the finest accounting in Eclessian religion and I commend it to every bank. The spring at Sarnwell, which rose in 1311 from a well that eleven image-breaking Ashenmen had fouled, and to which those eleven swore on their own river, has passed every one of the five tests of sainthood the Council of Veridance wrote down. A flask of its water costs more than a flask of Thalmyric black wine. I bought one for a carter of mine whose foot had been crushed under his own wheel. He walks. I have the receipt.

> *Margin:* None of your five tests can be applied to a saint who left no body, which is to say to Caedros the Unbowed. Your Council knew it when it wrote them. That is what you call accounting.

The Ossuary Crown, bone and wire and white orchard wood, which spoke with the voices of the plague dead and testified in their title suits, sits in a Sanctum reliquary under three claims, one of them its own. In 1263 the relic court ruled that *the dead do not testify through things*. I think it was a Vortharan speaking-engine of very high quality.

> *Margin:* A Vortharan engine that knew a dead abbot had promised a mill to a village? Then hire it to do your audits.

**The Sweat and the unsung dead.** Eclessians date everything from before or after the Grey Sweat of 1232 to 1247, which killed a fifth of the east and an eighth of the west, and priests faster than anyone and which they remember as *the years the ferry queued*. Priests died faster than they could bury, and in parish after parish the unblessed dead walked. Then, in one week of 1247, the Sweat stopped everywhere at once, and the penitents who had walked the roads carrying the ash of the unblessed and speaking their names broke off their processions and went home, saying it was finished being needed. Every power in the world would like the credit for that week. None has dared to send the bill.

Afterwards the Sanctum sold sealed letters promising that a Sending House would sing named rites for a named soul, and where the rites were sung the dead stopped walking. The difficulty was arithmetic. Under Alessior II, a Sestaran of House Malvora elected on Medori credit, I am sorry to say, a holy man who believed the seal itself did the sending and gave letters away to the poor by the cartload, and his Cardinal-Treasurer Silvio Quarne, the letters were printed, priced by age with a cheaper rate for a child, sold blank, and sold at perhaps ten for every rite sung, and from about 1288 the unsung ones went in bundles to brokers. That is dead paper. It still circulates, brokers still press claims on the named dead with it, and Quarne's own countersign fetches the best price. I own a bundle, bought in Vorthara as a curiosity and kept in a drawer with my bad debts. I have slept perfectly well since, apart from one night when the lamp went out with the shutters closed and the room smelled of wet stone, which I attribute to the canal.

> *Margin:* Burn it. Better: take the names to a Lantern brother and let him sing them. You are keeping people in a drawer.

The old Church answered sooner than my trade expected and paid for it. The Council of Veridance (1319–1324) abolished the sale of assurances, ordered every letter ever sealed to be sung, the Great Arrears, and chartered the Order of the Lantern from the night-singers of Aurelium's burned Saint-Leon quarter, whom the Sanctum had spent forty years trying to suppress and whose priest had died in an Inquisitors' cell, to walk the haunted parishes by night singing names out of the old registers. Those registers had been in the Medori vault since 1289, and the Council had to buy copies sheet by sheet. It then sent the bill to the dioceses, and the bill cost it Ironvale. The Lantern is the only order of the Church working off a debt, and it pays in hymns at Fiorenzian prices. If you meet a Lantern brother on the road at night, give him the road. He is working.

The plague's saint is Gerlind of Lindenfurt, a miller's daughter who at eight years old made her neighbours say aloud the names of their dead and sang the one funeral hymn she knew, and who went on doing it for thirty years for bread and never for coin: *the dead are named, not bought.* Alessior canonised her and printed her face on the assurance letters, which then outsold all others. The Freeflame quote her against every Sending ever sold, and Lindenfurt people still go down to the mill-race to say names.

**The reformed duchies.** A friar named Ansgar, chaplain of the Sending House at Brenholt, set the letters his House sold against the rites he was given time to sing, found more than ten for one, and wrote the count in the margins of his psalter with a good deal else. In 1290 he drowned with forty-one miners in the Blackwater pit, and at the funeral the pithead tally-keeper, a woman called Irmel, read his margins to the widows in place of the rite they were queuing to buy. Those are the *Brenholt Margins*. They read like an audit, which is why my trade admires them: *prove me wrong with Fire, fathers, and not with fees.* Nine years later the Synod of Aldersbruck put the dead man's psalter to the fire and it would not burn, so they called the test void and charged the whole country a second fee for its funerals. When the fees were sold to brokers, the miners and farmers of the upper Weiss waded into the river, smeared their faces with its grey silt, swore to fight until the last false seal was burned and went down their own pits, and for twenty-three years the Ashenmen never once broke their word. In 1329 Duke Rainald of Ironvale, who had fought them all his life, was handed a levy for the Church's debts and a roll that showed his own father unsung. He burned his duchy's Sending letters in Brenholt market, and the Ashenmen held their oath fulfilled and came up to serve him.

That reach of the Weiss is the Preacher's Water, and oaths sworn in it hold. The Sanctum tested the water in 1342, declared the result inconclusive and sealed the file, which tells you all you need to know about the result. My own view is that oaths hold there because the Grand Ministry of Ironvale hangs the men who break them, and that no instrument in the world binds like a rope.

> *Margin:* The Ashenmen kept their oath for twenty-three years before Ironvale had a Grand Ministry to hang anybody. Find another rope.

**Ironvale**, governed since Rainald's house died out by a Grand Ministry of the guilds under Aldric Weyn, is foundries, black powder and congregations that cast their hymns together under tested elders, after Doctor Lucian Ferro, the notary at the trial of Ansgar's book, who wrote their order for the Sanctum as the first half of a refutation and never wrote the second: *the Fire keeps no landlord.* Its towns are orderly, literate and merciless. **Korrheim** is cantons, engineers and hired companies, governed from Heldenheim. **Caldria**, under Grand Duke Alric IV, is the old Church's sword; it held Crown Gate Pass with Vortharan help in 907, the only time Caldria and Vorthara have agreed about anything. **Veridancia**, under Duke Adrien III, is vineyards, seminaries and printing presses, and trains the old Church's best reformers and their ablest opponents. **Brauvalis**, under Duke Reinhardt, holds the middle Weiss with breweries and bell-foundries and is one succession away from changing its rite.

What holds them is the **Korrheim Accord** of 1343, drafted at Heldenheim and sworn with every signatory's hands in the Preacher's Water: *every roof under its lord's lamp, and every lamp where its dead lie.* Lawyers quote the first half in Latinate. The second half is the one that matters, because no prince chose: commissioners sat up at night in every disputed churchyard, and a yard that lay quiet went to whichever rite had been burying in it. A yard that goes a year and a day without a funeral sung is forfeit, so both churches now run to every deathbed on the border, and I have been overtaken on the road by two priests racing for one. The Accord says nothing about conscience, mixed marriages or the reunion of the churches, which the Sanctum's legates would not put into an oath sworn on water that holds oaths. Every few years someone proposes to amend it, and nobody will go back into the river to do it. The river people who bury in that water and keep no yard, the Unsworn, were counted by nobody, are protected by nobody and are hunted by everybody.

**Virelia.** East on the plains of the Orzva, King Gregor IV Radomir rules an elective kingdom whose sabre-right electors are perhaps a third reformed. An elected king lies among his own family when he dies, so the crown lands have no royal graves, and at every election the lawyers argue over whose lamp they stand under. Virelia is grain, grown by bound villages. During the Sweat the magnates declared every village whose priest had died unclean, sealed it, and moved its survivors onto their estates under bond in exchange for a priest and burial in rite. This bond of the clean village has hardened in every generation since, and its runaways walk south to any Freeflame town where the elders will bury anyone. Virelians drink honey-spirits that ought to be sold by apothecaries, and in the Vesari villages nobody paints a face above a door lintel, or will tell you why.

In 1061 a Virelian prince whose hall the riders had burned sent his house's dead lamp to Veridun to be relit from the Ember, as a Sermoran sends a clipped coin back to the mint. The Patriarch would not light a heathen's hearth, and while they argued the cold lamp lit itself in the hands of the old woman who had carried it. *It chose my hand,* she said; *I carried it.* The Patriarch kept it, and on the plains whoever keeps your fire keeps you, so the prince took baptism, Eclessian swords and a new fire from the Sanctum's own altar, carried east in an iron pot, reckoning that a landlord two months' ride away is the cheapest kind. Three years later the kingless lords of the eastern march made him their king at a moot of sabres. He is the only prince in history to be offered a miracle and take the better lease. So the Kindling Question became public, and you will be asked it at every second table in Eclessia and every first table in Thalmyria. Does the Fire dwell where it is welcomed, as Veridun says? Is it carried by the hands of the Church, as Aurelium says? Or does it keep no landlord, as Ironvale says? I answer that I am a Sermoran and the Fire has never asked my opinion. It satisfies nobody, but it has never got me stabbed.

> *Margin:* It lit in our cathedral and in no other. Write that down, Sestaran, and then stop writing.

**The marches.** South and east, the Sylvan Marches under High Warden Elias Dreymann are nominally old Church, heavily Folk, cold and hard to govern, and their parishes thin into clan-law the farther south you ride. At Vessing, a hill parish of the marches, in 1352 a sexton, a reformed carter and a runaway Virelian woman burying her child outside the churchyard wall saw a figure of banked fire standing in the sloe-field. The sexton reports it giving Veridun's answer and the carter Aurelium's, each against his own church. The mother says it said nothing, but wept and pointed over the wall at the churchyard, which reformed preachers read as *sing for them*. Captured papers of the Weeping Courts claim the apparition as their own work, which may be a confession or a boast, and the Sanctum has not ruled. On the far southern frontier lies Wolfsmere, the Lake That Remembers, where four thousand oath-keepers and seers, called there under a king's safe-conduct, were drowned in 993 and where the water rose on a windless night the next year and took a Markish shore-fort. No boats, no bells, no iron, by law and by sense. Just beyond it is Grenzburg, where the roads stop and the debts begin.

**Eating and dangers.** Markish country eats rye bread, pork, preserved cabbage, river fish and dark beer; the Latinate south eats wheat, oil, wine and cheese. At Kindlenight every hearth is relit from the church lamp. In haunted parishes the unsung dead still walk: send for a Lantern brother, not a broker. Avoid any apparition that weeps, pays or demands. The Hollow Flame preaches that the cure for a corrupt Church is to cut oneself off from the Fire altogether; its preachers are pleasant, and the villages they leave are not. Secret magistrates still buy black tiles scratched with crimes not yet committed, although the courts of both churches forbid conviction for the future fact. Do not buy black tiles.

## The Seven Seals, Which Are Mine

I come now to my own country, which is not a country, and I shall try to be fair to it, which is more than it has ever tried to be to me.

The Sestaran Concord is seven cities that share no ruler, no law, no treasury, no church, no citizenship and no foreign policy. Five of us sealed a defensive compact in 908, when the Covenant had just taken Caleran and was looking about for its next appointment; Vorthara added its seal in 909 while refusing every clause that might let anyone govern it; and Castara became the seventh seal in 1332. The compact does one thing. When a foreign power attacks any of us, the others must muster. It does not cover private war, piracy, mercenary quarrels, colonial ventures, or anything else we do to one another, which is most of what we do. Every plan to make one of the seven into somebody's province has therefore been abandoned at the planning stage, which is the cheapest place to win a war.

> *Margin:* Note that he does not say how often the seven have plundered their neighbours under licences the muster does not cover.

**Sermora**, my city, is a canal metropolis on fortified islands in the middle of the Thalassian, which we police, tax and on occasion rob, though only those who were asking for it. Male guild masters and ship-share citizens vote; the rest of us, canal folk, sailors, servants and foreigners, who outnumber the voters many times over, do as we are told, mostly.

In 1166, when a rival's fleet with hired Ättar keels and a leased storm-engine lay against the walls, a figure in a chain-tender's leather apron was seen walking the harbour wall in the worst of the fighting, and the storm-engine broke. The official songs leave out that the same figure turned and rebuked our soldiers when they began killing prisoners afterwards. On his feast the chain is lowered for one foreign or refugee vessel before it is raised for the procession, and the Admiralty's lawyers have spent two centuries narrowing what counts. We are a city of sailors, and our saint is Naris, the drowned martyr of Halmenth, whose chapels keep the registers of ships that did not come home. We are also the city of the **Thirty Sisters**. In the refugee winter after Caleran fell, thirty ships came into Sestaran ports over eleven days, sailing in formation with their rudders lashed, their cargoes whole, their meals half-eaten on the tables and not a soul aboard. I will say what I think. A Caleran shipping house saw the end coming, sent its fleet to sea ahead of the Covenant with the crews taken off at the last port, meaning to claim for the loss, and was overtaken by the Fall before it could file.

In 1345 a Sermoran press printed extracts from the registers in the Medori vault, naming Fiorenzian dead whose paid-for Sendings had never been sung, and called that vault the Bank of the Dead. It very nearly brought the Medori down. I regard it as the finest thing ever done with a printing press, and I am not impartial: my cousin set the type. The extracts were bought from a countryman of ours in a Fiorenzian debtor's coat, who was pulled out of a dike the next spring.

**Fiorenzia** is the highland duchy of vineyards, textiles, scholarship and painting, and the home of the Medori Bank, which lends to everyone, is owed by everyone, and is the reason the word *Fiorenzian* is spoken with respect in Iqashar and with loathing in Brenholt. Duke Gaius Medori rules beside a patrician senate whose Roll of Faces the family bought its way into in 1238, when the Sweat had left nearly half its houses without a head; the older houses still call this a counterfeit genealogy. Since 1289 the Medori have held the Chancery of Sendings' registers as surety for a Primarch's debts, which is to say they hold a great part of Eclessia's dead in pawn. Fiorenzians hang a face of each of their dead on the house wall. After our pamphlet the wax ones began to talk, and the friar Frate Severin preached that they had become doors for the Hungers. At the Feast of Souls in 1346 the senate burned them in the cathedral square, the Pyre of Masks, which the friar begged them not to light. Witnesses still disagree about what they saw standing in that fire. The crowd went to the Medori palace for the names, and Lauro Medori, who was a mask-maker's boy before he married the bank, opened the vault and gave them the city's pages. Severin, who never held an office, kept the whole city singing from those pages for four years. Then the citizens voted the Medori a hereditary dukedom over the senate's head, the friar preached against it, and he was found in a cellar wearing a wax face nobody could take off him. Every painter's studio in Fiorenzia now keeps a drawer of faces it will not show. Sermoran gossip says the Medori draw their contracts in an older hand than ours and that some of their clauses do things clauses ought not to do. I have said so myself, after wine, and will deny it before any Fiorenzian notary.

**Aversia**, in the southern Iron Foothills, makes the arms, armour, tools and engineers with which the rest of the world disagrees with itself. Duke Marcus Soran's mercenary house took the principality by civil war, General Aurelia Soran commands its army, and the old Shield Houses keep the magistracies and their grievances. In the high mines, between about 1110 and 1170, the miners kept a compact with the small grey people at the edge of the lamplight, who knocked to warn of a collapse, until Shield House officials faked the warning knock to send strikers and prisoners into the deadliest workings. Aversian miners still never answer the middle of three knocks. Neither should you.

**Vorthara** occupies the Black Spire above Crown Gate Pass, at the knot of mountains where Thalmyria, Eclessia and Sestara meet, and has no government at all. Its schools, guilds, families and gangs each rule their own wards under the Spire Truce, which forbids only those quarrels that close the ascent, poison the shared water or bring a foreign army through the gates. Pass-Keeper Salvi Corren holds Vorthara's seal for the muster and has no authority whatever inside. The city lies under interdict. Its workshops sell rune-scripts, constructs, reagents and Shell-fire; its arenas test the products; its bodies go missing. Silvio Quarne died in a rented room in the Black Spire wards in 1299, and the ledgers he had carried out of Aurelium were gone when the room was opened. Vortharan tellers say they still add up at night. At certain alignments the Spire hosts a market of a kind I will not describe, at which I have never been permitted to buy and at which I suspect I have been sold.

**Orsena** is a tiny neutral port at the eastern foot of the range, where the Eclessian road comes down to the sea, governed by Harbor Rector Sabina Valeris and the Harbor Book houses. Prisoner exchanges, ransoms, quiet negotiations and cargoes too awkward for direct delivery pass through it, and Orsena does not ask why yesterday's enemies need the same warehouse today. It will forgive you anything you did at home and hang you for fouling a cistern. To hold its highest offices a family must have been registered for three generations. I married into Orsena. I have not yet qualified.

> *Margin:* True. They fined me for rinsing a shirt.

**Vardessa** is the inland fair-city of the Three Roads Basin under First Burgess Tomas Bellafor and the guild benches. Its fair peace protects declared guests, worship, property and roads for the season and not a day longer. Its guarantors register the caravans of the Castaran Long Road and witness the Ash word-prices, and Vortharan contraband enters its gates as *demonstration equipment*. The neutral muster hall stands there, and every member of the Concord wants it neutral in its own favour.

**Castara**, the seventh seal and the newest, has a chapter to itself at the end of this book, as it has a world to itself at the end of its sea.

**Who owes whom.** Everyone owes us, and we owe each other. Fiorenzia owes Sermora a pamphlet's worth of grudges, Aversia owes Fiorenzia its debts, Vardessa owes nobody and charges everyone, Vorthara owes the world an explanation, and Orsena keeps the receipts.

## The Covenant, Where Everything Is Written Down

North and northwest of Thalmyria lies the yellow crescent of the Covenant, the Ahl-e-Ahd: not one people but an empire and a confession shared by Ahdari caravan clans, Iqashari administrators, Qahuri farmers, Qazmari sailors and Zanthiri, who are all of these at once, with Velians, Jirahar, Tarkhan and foreigners keeping their own houses beneath them. Their faith, Al-Ahdiya, teaches that creation rests on the Great Covenant revealed to Mujalib in 541 by the angel they call Malak al-Ahd, and their law teaches that what was not written down and witnessed did not happen. Their children are taught to understand a document before they sign it. I wish ours were.

A Covenant contract, as our countryman Vetti wrote in his *Of Princes as Borrowers*, in a Fiorenzian debtor's coat, is honest the way a well is deep. It will do exactly what it says, for ever, and if that is not what you meant, the Covenant regards the difference as your education. Their law can protect an orphan's property against a governor, and it has made slavery and the custody of names more durable than any sword could. Read every clause, then have a Velian translator read it to you again. The Redeemer's faithful are the People of the Lamp in Covenant law, and their worship is protected, taxed and restricted, all three in writing.

**The history as I understand it.** The Covenant was founded on bread. In the famine of the 540s, when the Basin's flood failed five summers in seven and a murrain killed the oxen of the settled world, the four thousand four hundred and forty-four bound devils of the Covenant dug the oasis and channels at Al-Qahur while children starved in the canal towns below, and the grain went to those who sealed their names into the Covenant. It is the finest piece of salesmanship in history and I say so with professional awe. From 574 their armies took the Atherian north, not by storming it but by sending breach-courts ahead to ask each town to produce the witness to its charter, which had been sworn before augur colleges the empire itself had dissolved; half the north was served, not fought. The advance stopped where hermit-bishops had re-sealed the charters in the Fire.

The devils, by the way, they breed. I have seen the pens from the road above Al-Qahur, or seen walls that a Qahuri guide told me were pens, and was charged extra for it.

> *Margin:* They do not breed them. They bind them. A Covenant devil is a power that has been given a name and a task it cannot refuse, and wears the task until it is nothing else. Which is worse, and which you would know if you read anything but invoices.

In the autumn of 931 Idris died, whom the Thalmyrics call the Letter-Caliph because he never set eyes on Caleran, two years after his heir had refused at the foundry to take up his oaths, and every oath sworn to the pair of them by name lapsed in the same hour. The silt hounds that guarded the northern wells slipped their feeding names, and the frontier still calls that season *the winter the wells barked*. In Iqashar a great engine of the Chain Furnace walked downhill and killed nineteen people in the dyers' yards, until an engine-feeder named Gulnar stood on the threshold stone of the Dyers' Hall and gave it its feeding name again, to no man but *to the threshold of the house of Qahir, whoever stands in it*. It lay down in a vat of indigo. They call it the Elder, and the blue still shows in its seams. The throne of the Covenant has been called the Sublime Threshold ever since: an office that cannot die, invented in a hurry by a woman who fed engines for a living. Every dynasty should have such a clerk.

**Iqashar** is the imperial capital on its northern escarpment, a city of registers famous for working drains and honest granaries. The Sultan-Caliph is Marzuq III, clever and idle, who came to the throne in 1349 while his brothers were killed, blinded or lodged in the Cull of Brothers. His mother, the Sayyida Nurhan, called the Veiled Ledger, holds the household treasury and the marriage market and through them half the court; I consider her the best accountant alive. Princes Kavus and Ardavan keep rival households, and every appointment in Iqashar is a wager on which of them will live. The Custodian of the Vault of Names is called the Unnamed Custodian because, since 1011, every Custodian has sold his name at investiture, after the first of them pledged his to a lending house to ransom a son. It is the only office in the empire that cannot be blackmailed, which tells you what the others are like.

The register of the living bound has not balanced since the Sweat, which the Covenant calls the Emptying of the Benches, because the witnesses who release the names of the dead died faster than the bound. Iqashar also keeps mercy towers, great engines that proclaim terms of surrender. One was sent against the Tarkhan in the 990s and spent a season proclaiming terms to empty pasture, and the steppe still calls it the Talking Hill.

**Al-Qahur**, south of Iqashar, is the sacred basin of the 4,444, a country of canals, grain, fish, dates and lentils, and the holy city where Caliph Uwais, Custodian-of-the-Wells, guards the pilgrimage and the Deep Vault, which holds the names of the dead and the Fourth Seal itself. His acknowledgment makes a Sultan-Caliph lawful, while the Sword-Regent, Nadir ibn Haraz, governs through the military households. Those households took their estates on fixed-term leases in 938, the leases have run out, and the canal councils have pointed this out every year since. In the Basin villages the insult for a bully is *an armed tenant who forgot the lease had an end*. Some of the old tombs of the Basin answer when spoken to. Do not speak to them.

**Qazmarid** holds the western strait on its own landmass and is an independent caliphate of the Sealed Blood under the Caliph-Wasi Zahran, who claims descent from Mujalib's daughter Rumaitha and says he keeps the Counter-Instrument beneath his throne. The quarrel between Iqashar's Witnessed Seal and Qazmarid's Sealed Blood turns on which of two instruments was their Prophet's fourth and last Seal, and I am not qualified to audit it. Qazmarid lives on citrus, oil, horses, salt and the strait tolls, and its great public rite is the Reading of the Wells, at which an old safe-conduct is read clause by clause and the mourners answer each clause that the well was dry. They are remembering Bir Qassim in 603, where the Prophet's daughter and fifteen of her household died of thirst beside a station well whose bound keeper drew for their drovers and would not draw for them, and where the drovers were afterwards killed for sharing their water. Her son lived, and went veiled ever after. A Sealed-Blood village draws its water by hand to this day and lets no bound thing near the rope. In the century after Idris it tolled every Covenant convoy that used its strait, and its chroniclers boast that Qazmarid never made war on Iqashar in those years; it invoiced it. I have that sentence framed above my counter.

**Zanthir**, in the southeast between the desert and the Thalassian, is the last pearl of the Banu Mizan, ruled from Qabir by Emir Badis al-Zanthiri, who pays tribute to Iqashar in one season and to Veridun in the other under an instrument one of his predecessors signed in 1044, which is the most elegant financial arrangement I have ever seen. Qabir holds the seat of the Velian Church's Lamp-Father, Jirahar quarters, exile monasteries, contract archives and insurers, and smells of spice, ink and harbour mud. The emir protects his Velians and his Jirahar because their presence makes an invasion expensive in conscience as well as men, and every court expects Zanthir to be the next war's first battlefield. The old Gateway country around Qabir is where, nearly nineteen centuries ago, the survivors of a city that fed children to its god ate together on the landing at Sarqen, each bringing what they had, and swore the Table Oath: *no child for the table; no seat kept empty; no guest served by worth.* The kings of that coast had to eat at that table and swear it before the towns would call them kings. I am told some harbour families of the coast still lay their boards with no high end on feast days. I have eaten at such a table. I was seated beside a porter, and the porter had the better conversation.

**Mazrakan** is the chartered market at the end of Iqashar's paved road, where the caravans meet the Tarkhan approaches to the Nine Wells of Tergai. It is governed by a Council of Nine Measures under an imperial Gate-Prefect, and its charter stops feuds and warrants at the gate. It sells everything in the open, and relics, bound spirits, names and slaves in licensed enclosures, which I did not enter and advise you not to. Iron Horde companies sell escort service in one street and their enemies sell stolen remounts under altered brands two streets away. At the spring horse fair the place where a stand collapsed in 1189 and killed a Qahirid heir is still left empty. The Market Watch can wake golems that sleep beneath the great squares, and one of them, I am told, has lately begun enforcing a clause that appears in no public copy of the charter. Nobody has dared ask it to produce its copy.

**The Velian States** lie between Zanthir and Thalmyria: a belt of cities, estates, churches and forts that has belonged to the Banu Mizan, to the Qahirids and to nobody in particular since the seventh century. Some towns pay two masters; others make themselves too useful to destroy. The Velian Church was condemned at Halmenth in 411 over the manner in which the Fire came upon the Redeemer, which I am also not qualified to audit. Its churches are whitewashed, bare of images and lit by a single lamp, its priests marry, and its liturgy is in the dialect the Redeemer spoke. Velian physicians, translators and copyists are the best in the world. The charters the Velian cities held before the conquest have sat in fireproof chests since about 610, and the chests are still shut. At Oronda in 876 an Atherian crusade raised its holy standard before the walls and nothing came; on the walls the Velian clergy lit one lamp, as in their churches, and whether it kindled depends on whom you ask. Iron-mounted raiders still cross these marches at night, and it was here that Prince Nikandros died. The Zehrim folk farm and wait.

## The Four Hordes and Their Horses

North of everything, between the Covenant, the Eclessian frontier and the road to Castara, lies the dry belt of the Tarkhan: stony well-country in the west, where they ride camels and reckon journeys in water days, and grass in the northeast, where they ride the best horses in the world and do not reckon journeys at all, being already at home. Their high heaven is Kokh, the Blue Eternal; their wind-speakers deal with sun, moon, wind and storm; and a few of them can share a hunting falcon's sight while their own bodies lie helpless in the tent, which is why a Tarkhan camp always leaves somebody at home.

**Law and manners.** A Tarkhan oath states its word-price, the compensation due if it is broken, and they judge a contract by its hospitality and its stated price rather than by the elegance of the notary. Three days of honest guest-right bind host and guest, and betrayal at a household fire is pursued for life. Women hold the tents, the stores, the milk and a great share of the herds; men hold the formal command of raids and war; a factor who bargains only with the men will learn who owns the horses. They live on milk, curds, hard cheese, preserved meat and grain, drink fermented milk at every meal, and on hard roads take blood from a living animal. Accept everything you are offered. You may refuse the blood once.

**The horses, on which everything depends.** When Atheria fell, its frontier studs, whose remount lines had been blessed with hymns by the lay brothers who kept them, scattered into the northeastern grass, and the Grass-Tarkhan caught them. Those horses did not shy from bound things. In 951 riders on them broke the hound-guarded wells and watered at Tergai, at the Nine Wells the Covenant had seized in the drought of 558 and which the Tarkhan have never stopped calling theirs. The blessing thinned with every generation, as such things do. Then, in 1249, a captive Velian farrier and deacon named Gavrin, carried north to shoe horses at a frontier fort and taken by raiders when its garrison went home in the Sweat, knelt in the snow over a dying black colt of the sacred Grey Sister's line and sang the remount hymn his village kept for sick animals. The colt stood, and its get would walk up to a bound warden as though it were a fencepost. The herd-master who owned it took the colt's name, Qarayel, united the Tarkhan in 1261 under a standard braided from the manes of its nine proven sons, and became the Black Wind. From 1265 to 1300 Iqashar paid him escort fees and every Sestaran caravan paid his grass-toll, which is why our trade went to sea and why Castara is rich. Gavrin blessed sick foals and sick children, refused to bless anything for war, and would not take a place in the clan while its other slaves stayed slaves. In 1281 he was found in the horse lines with his throat cut. Qarayel sent the heads of a Thalmyric embassy that had come to buy him to Veridun in a feed-bag, though some steppe tellers say Qarayel cut the throat himself, to keep the grace from being sold. Thalmyria canonised Gavrin. The Velians, whose great fair his horses burned in 1265, call him the Wind-Saddler and keep no feast. The Moon Horde keeps his cairn and will not let it be opened.

> *Margin:* Saint Gavrin of the Halter was murdered by the Covenant's name-hunters, as the Synod has found. Do not print steppe slanders about our envoys.

Qarayel died in 1303, undefeated, and on the night he died, they say, his old stallion carried him out of the camp with nobody at its head, every sacred mare in the camp went after it, and the herds came home eleven days later without him, in four bands to four pastures a week's ride apart. I will wager that the Moon Horde knows exactly where he lies and rides round the place every year. In 1304 his descendants met to divide the herds, found that the horses had done it for them, and after a summer's argument put a name to each of the four bands, and each Horde became what its horses made it.

> *Margin:* Nobody knows where he lies. That is the whole point of him.

**The White Horde**, under Khan Batuqai, took the numerous herds of the northwest and is Iqashar's nearest terror, raiding and slaving round the Nine Wells. **The Iron Horde**, under Khan Tegrul, took the carefully bred lines, the only horses on the frontier that will stand beside the Covenant's own bound things, and Iqashar has hired it ever since. Its companies now hold frontier offices, and it is a fair question whether Iron serves the Covenant or is quietly becoming it. **The Moon Horde**, under the Khatun-regent Sorqai, keeps the Nine-Mane Standard, without which no full kurultai can be called, together with the strictest sky-observance and the prohibition on searching for Qarayel's grave. Sorqai is old, and the steppe is waiting. **The Ash Horde**, under Khan Chilgir, took the poorest lines and the eastern road country, sells Castara the Ash Road Peace every year, and is in debt to foreign houses, which I could name, but I would lose a client.

The grace is thinning again in every Horde's foals. A proven Iron foal costs more at Mazrakan than a house in Orsena, and the price is rising, which tells you all you need to know about the supply.

## The Folk, Who Will Feed You If You Tell Them Your Name

South of Eclessia the world turns grey and green and cold, the parishes thin out into forest, and the forest belongs to the Folk. They are not a kingdom. They are a great many clans, longhouses, hill-forts, river leagues and coastal fleets who share a hearth-law, a speech that changes every valley, and the obligations of winter shelter, which bind them when nothing else does. Ask a Folk man who he is and he will give you his kindred before his name: Moss-Crown, Grey-Withy, Bent Oak, Tidewood, Gale-Crown.

**Where.** The **Green March** kindreds meet Eclessia and are the ones you will meet first: fortified villages, good iron, Eclessian ploughs and sawmills, and many Chapel-Folk, who worship the Lord through the Redeemer and keep their own language, songs and hearth-law. The **Rootbound Heartwood** in the deep south is old-rite country, the Enaidfae confederacies, groves and seers, where every path belongs to somebody and some of the somebodies are not people. The **Gale-Crown Ranges** in the eastern mountains are shepherds, mines, hill-forts and tolls; whoever holds a pass holds the trade, and knows it. The **Tidewood Coast** is fishing, salt, timber and cold-water sailing, with Ättar and Sestaran keels in every anchorage.

**Hearth-law.** A guest gives his true name and receives food and sanctuary, and a host who betrays an accepted guest, or a guest who lies about his name, has done something the Folk count worse than murder. Burning a refuge hall, poisoning a common well or breaking a winter truce puts a whole clan outside the feud-law. Give your true name. I once heard a Vortharan buyer give a false one in a Gale-Crown hall. I was not present for what followed, by the grace of the Lord and a fast mule.

**Trade.** The Folk sell timber, resin, honey, wax, furs, amber, medicines and preserved fish, and buy steel, grain, wine, books, glass and mill parts. Their land belongs to the remembered dead, the living kin and the powers of the place together, and no chief may sell it alone. Half the timber concessions I have seen offered in Sermora were signed by a chief selling what was not his to a factor who did not ask. I lost money on two of them and deserved to.

**Faith, saints and dangers.** The old-rite clans keep bargains with the powers of forest, water, storm and road, which they gather into courts, and their seers make offerings and keep taboos to avoid attention nobody wants. A power that keeps one bargain may still be dangerous to the household that made it. Take nothing from a grove. Eat nothing offered by anyone you meet on a road that was not there yesterday. The war-leader Weylan Oath-Bent, who was baptised before the drowning, carried the king's safe-conduct to the lake, and afterwards sued that king in his own bishops' court and won the peace that wrote the lake-law of Wolfsmere into the march charters, is a trophy convert to the Eclessians and *the Bent Oak* to the Folk, said with pity or contempt according to the valley. The Folk also say that nine of those drowned in that lake did not die, and keep it still. Eclessian lords whose estates the Sweat had emptied marched into the cold forest in 1258 to take captives to work them, and winter and the Grobi broke them in three years. They called it the March of the Redeemer's Sword. The Folk do not use that name kindly.

## The Ättar, Who Are Folk with Ships

They live in great halls that are granary, feast-house, court, barracks and sanctuary at once, gathered into four great kindreds and ruled by no king. The **Lion-Mane Coast** jarls want profitable sea service and a foothold in Kedrava. The **Hornwall Valleys** hold the food and the drove roads. The **Bearshield Holds** hold the iron and the Paint-Keepers. The **Grey-Wolf Range** faces the Grobi and wants everyone else to send more men.

Every old-rite chief is a skinwalker who can take the beast-shape of his kindred, by a bargain made with Mother Moon in the age of the Empty Halls. The price is one firstborn child in every generation of the ruling line. A changeling takes the child's place and leaves at adolescence, and the child comes home a Moon-Seer. I have met a Moon-Seer. She was polite, she knew my mother's name, and I have not been back. Berserkers paint themselves with an iron-skin paint that kills them slowly and lets them face things that would kill them quickly, including, if it comes to it, their own transformed chief. Halls baptised in the Redeemer's faith renounce the bargain, lose the shape for their heirs, and keep the moon-scars and something of the rage.

To *take the keel* is to go on a summer voyage, which may mean trading, raiding, escorting, whaling or hiring out, and often all of them on the same voyage. A Sermoran letter can turn an Ättar captain from pirate to privateer without changing anything about his last voyage but the paperwork, and Ättar crews are ransomed through Orsena every season. Skalds remember every insult. A torc is a gift and a loyalty, and to cut a man's oath-torc is a humiliation that will be paid for. The food is cold-country grain, fish, whale, mutton and mead, and the hospitality is enormous and dangerous.

The Saltborn of the Kedravan coast are what comes of Ättar raiders who settle, marry, are baptised and join the Thalmyric navy: saints' names beside totem bynames, mead served outside the church door, and the Emperor's Ash Guard, sworn to his person.

## The Jirahar, Who Were Everywhere Before Us

The Jirahar have no country and are in all of them. They trace their law to the Great Prophet and the refuge in the Glass, count descent through their mothers, admit converts after a long and demanding instruction, and await the Mesakha. They live in tower houses, in road companies with wagons, classrooms and forges, in sea fleets of family ships, and in turning companies whose wagons roll onto barges wherever road becomes river. You will find them in every port I have named, keeping their own law, schools, food and burial, and usually doing the translating for everyone else.

**The towers.** Five live. Migdal Qesheth stands on Zanthir's dry margin, where High Keeper Zerahiah sits, first in dignity and king of nothing. Migdal Dahava in the Velian belt keeps the clearing-houses that settle what the Medori dare not hold, and its letters are honoured in places Medori paper is not. Migdal Yamma keeps the western sea-road, Migdal Melach the road between Iqashar and the Tarkhan, and Migdal Talga the cold Eclessian marches. The spires you see are the least of them; the halls go down. The sixth is Qiryath Reqa, the Empty City, in the eastern Eclessian riverlands, where nobody lives.

**The Withholding and the Silence.** The Jirahar keep an old sign: an offering that burns without smoke and leaves the stones cool. In the first winter of the siege of the first Qiryath, more than seven centuries before the Redeemer, the city's council declared its foreign residents unhoused, so that no household within the walls might feed or shelter them, and let them starve in the caravan yard in sight of the sanctuary stair, and at the next offering the smoke rose and the stones were hot. They call the years that followed the Withholding. It lasted some ninety years, through the city's fall and exile, until a widow who kept the last cistern of the rebuilt city refused to expel the settler families who worked her water, and the offering carried by her mixed households left the stones cool again. After the Redeemer died the sign stopped once more and has not returned. The Jirahar call that the Silence, and they do not agree with the churches about why; I advise you not to raise it at dinner. Those who wait for it to end cite the widow's cool stones as proof that a withholding can.

**Qiryath and the Mesakha.** Atheria destroyed the second city in 72 after a revolt, and the emperor Vestian had it cleared about 396 under a charter forbidding anyone to rebuild it *before the Mesakha*, words he wrote in his own hand. The churches read them as *never*; the Jirahar read them as a promise. Under Zerahiah the Keepers presume every claimant to be the Mesakha false until they rule otherwise, and they have never ruled otherwise. In 1288 a man named Meshalem proclaimed himself the Mesakha and led a return movement whose families sold half the goods of the tower towns to make the journey; in 1291, before the whole camp at the Empty City, he twice went down the stair to the dead gate and twice came back up without touching it, and the movement collapsed. The Covenant had already voided the charters of every family that left. Sestaran creditors bought tower property cheap. It was a fair market in a falling price.

> *Margin:* A fair market in other people's ruin. The Jirahar remember which cities bought. Remember it yourself the next time you need a Dahava letter honoured.

**The gates.** The Jirahar keep Way-Gates by which a whole caravan can step from one place to another in a breath, and a turning-company carter has assured me that a Keeper could walk a wagon from any quay in the world to any market he pleased between breakfast and supper. It is a wonder they bother with roads.

> *Margin:* Only between their own fixed anchors at the towers, only with the Keepers' leave and the receiving tower's consent, and never for a host ruler who wants to buy passage. Your carter was selling you a story. They use roads because the world has a great many more places than it has towers.

**Faith and neighbours.** The Jirahar reject the Redeemer's claim while remembering him as a son of their people, and Thalmyria venerates a Jirahar Redeemer; between the two facts, conversation in Caleran requires care. Their road memorials near the Glass tell of the City After the Last Name, where a traveller's record arrives ahead of him and comes back alone. The Keepers teach it as a warning about burial and say no living road leads there. I have not looked for one.

## The Cold South, Where the Map Turns Honest

Every chartmaker knows that the world grows colder as you go south, and every traveller learns it through his boots. South of the Thalassian, winter halves every day's march and closes the high passes outright. Below the Folk forests and the Ättar ranges lies country where the summer is short, the ground stays frozen a hand's depth down, and the ruined roads of the Giant empires run away into the taiga where nobody follows them.

Out of the far southeast, from what our maps call the Grobi Wastes, come the Grobi. Every winter, when food fails in the deep south, their warbands come north along old corridors that point, as nearly as I can draw them, at Grenzburg's latitude. They begin as goblins the size of a ten-year-old child and grow with eating: into hobgoblins, into orcs taller than a doorframe with the strength of five men, into ogres that break gates, and into trolls that grow back whatever you cut off. They speak, plan, make crude iron and raise their young, and to them a raid is a meal; captives are carried as provisions for the march. The Folk say that a man lost long enough in the winter forest, starving and alone, becomes one of them in the end, and I see no reason to doubt it. I have seen what hunger does to men in an ordinary siege.

> *Margin:* No man becomes a Grobi. They are born Grobi, of Grobi. Ask any Frost-Road veteran in Kedrava. Your Folk informant was frightening you, and a man frightened by a story buys a better guide.

The Folk and the Ättar keep refuge halls, beacons, winter stores and watches against them, and young warriors from safer country take the Frost-Road south to serve the winter watch. That is where comfortable valleys send their sons to earn names, and where many of them leave them.

Farther still, a hard month's sail from the Ättar coasts or from Qazmarid across the far southwestern ocean, lies the separate continent of the Nsizwa. I have never been. Every Nsizwa I have met has assured me that the crossing is hard, and that they prefer it so.

## Castara and the Boiling Sea

In the far northeast, along the equatorial ocean we call the Boiling Sea, the hurricane belt stands for most of the year like a wall, and on its far side lies the northern half of the world, where the Giant empires died. Until my own lifetime only fools sailed into that wall and only liars came back. Then, in 1328, the Castaran salt-hauler *Naris's Hand*, under a pilot named Ottavia Sele, was caught at sea on one of the days when the whole hurricane belt falls flat calm, and she crossed. She reached the Shattered Isles and came home with green crystal, Expanse hardwood and a chart-case of old Viscaran surveys taken from a wreck, whoever the Viscarans were; the University has six theories and a seventh for subscribers. Castara's new University rebuilt the Viscaran storm-keel from the wreck's lines, and by about 1331 Castaran ships were making crossings of six to ten weeks in the windows of the hurricane season. Castara, a salt-and-turtle port the Black Wind's tolls had fattened, became the seventh seal in 1332.

**The Held Breath.** Pilots call such a day a Breath day. It comes about once in a generation, across the whole hemisphere at once: glassy, windless, impossible. Castaran pilots keep a standing kit packed for one and will not discuss with passengers what happens on the far side. A handful of ships across the centuries have come back swearing that they sailed for a week on a sea that was calm for one day, and their logs agree with them and with nothing else. The *Naris's Hand*'s log says a week. I have examined that log, and I put it down to a navigator who reckoned his days by his bottles.

> *Margin:* Our Saltborn say the sea holds its tongue for a funeral. I do not know whose. Neither do you.

**The city.** Castara is crowded, new, loud and rich, governed by its Merchant Princes under First Speaker Eliana Corvesi. Anyone may enter the citizen roll after seven years' residence with civic service or a qualifying stake in an expedition, but only the Founders' Roll may supply a First Speaker, and so the city does a brisk trade in founding ancestors; I have been offered three. Old-Church, Freeflame and expedition chapels stand side by side; in the yards a master shipwright outranks a lord; and a great many labourers qualify for nothing at all.

**The Long Road.** Castara's land link runs west across Ash Horde grass, north of Eclessia, toward Vardessa and Aversia. In 1333 Castaran surveyors staked wells on Ash pasture, every Tarkhan who heard of it thought of the Nine Wells, and the Ash banners burned the survey camps. Since 1334 Castara has paid the Ash Road Peace every year, in silver, grain, tools and privileges, for declared escorts and passage. Castara calls it an escort compact. Aversia calls it tribute while depending on it. A missed payment ends the peace, the road is not Castaran ground, and the muster does not cover it. Twelve road-days west of Castara lies Cavora, a road town on a holding granted by the Grey-Rein Banner, with three wells and a market. Register at Vardessa or Castara, state your word-price and do not stray from the declared road.

**The new lands.** The Emerald Expanse is the northern half of the world, and every expedition there digs into the ruins of the Age of Chains whether it means to or not. Castaran taverns are full of men who have seen things in the Expanse; I have bought most of them a drink and believe one. Castaran prospectuses describe its land as empty. Distrust any prospectus that calls a land empty: it means the man who wrote it did not ask. The old Viscaran charts mark certain sites with a little bird, and nobody knows what the bird meant. Every bird-marked site anyone has reached has been empty, and not abandoned-empty but swept, as though tidied for an inspection, and the University has quietly stopped assigning bird-marked digs, for reasons of budget. My own view is that the Viscaran surveyors marked the sites they had already looted, and tidied up behind themselves out of professional pride, as a good surveyor should.

> *Margin:* He sells charts of the Expanse. Ask him whether they have birds on them.

## A Last Word on Who Owes Whom

I promised to tell you who owes whom, and I have. Thalmyria owes Sermora and the Medori for Zehrim. Eclessia owes the Medori for its dead and pays in hymns. The Covenant owes its witnesses, who are dead, and has not balanced its books since. Castara owes the Ash Horde, which owes everyone. Iqashar depends on the Iron Horde, and the Iron Horde has begun to collect in offices. Every capital owes something to a box it will not open.

I have one account of my own to close. In the year Alessior died my grandmother bought a Sending letter for her father, under the Gerlind seal, and it was never sung. It was in the Medori registers, and I have a copy of the page, at a Fiorenzian price. Two winters ago I took it to a Lantern brother who was walking the Sermoran parishes, a young man with a bad cough, and offered him the fee. He would not take it. He asked me to say my great-grandfather's name aloud, and I did, on the quay, in the rain, feeling a fool, and he sang. I am a man of figures, and I have no figure for what happened on that quay, and I have not tried to make one.

That is the end of my book. My charts are for sale at the sign of the Crooked Compass on the Pilots' Steps, at fair prices, with true soundings.

> *Margin:* This is the only page I have not corrected. — Akakios
