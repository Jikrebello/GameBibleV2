# The Bir Qassim Safe-Conduct - Annotated

*The Witnessed-Seal recension of the instrument under which Hilal ibn Raiq and fifty-seven members of his household travelled to arbitration in 603 AR. The Sealed-Blood recension differs in no clause and in every annotation. At the Reading of the Wells, each clause receives the answer: “And yet the well was dry.”*

## The Instrument

**In the name of the One Who Witnesses All Seals**, this instrument of passage and protection is entered between the Covenant, by its Marshal of the Roads, and Hilal ibn Raiq with his household, hereafter *the Travellers*.

**Clause the First.** The Travellers shall proceed under the Covenant's protection to arbitration at Iqashar and shall suffer no harm, hindrance, insult, or delay from any sworn to the Covenant.

**Clause the Second.** Protection extends from departure until the seventh sunset after arbitration closes, including return.

**Clause the Third.** The Travellers may bear personal arms but shall appear unarmoured at arbitration.

**Clause the Fourth.** The protected passage shall follow the North Well Road by Qafra, Bir Nadim, and Bir Qassim, and no other way.

**Clause the Fifth.** Breach by either party voids this instrument entire, saving nothing.

*Witnessed beneath the Marshal's, College's, and Guard's seals. The fourth space is empty. Every recension agrees it is empty and disagrees why.*

## What Happened

Flood damage took the causeway at Bir Nadim. The escort led the Travellers six miles along a drover's track before returning to the named road above Bir Qassim. The station well's bound keeper drew for the escort and for the unnamed drovers, and would not draw for the Travellers. A travelling court at Qafra, under the jurist Jarud ibn Sulayt, ruled that departure from the specified route voided the entire protection, regardless of cause or escort responsibility.

Under ordinary law Hilal was then a rebel under arms, and water given to him was aid to a rebel. By the sixth evening twelve of the Travellers had died of thirst. That night Hilal read this instrument aloud at the well-mouth, clause by clause, and after each clause the bucket came up dry. At dawn the escort refused the order to disarm the Travellers and carried the thirty-seven who could ride west towards the strait. The Marshal who had issued the instrument followed them. Nine Travellers too weak to ride, the Prophet's daughter Rumaitha among them, stayed at the well with fifteen drovers. Four of the nine died of thirst. A column of the guard of the wells, applying the court's ruling, killed the other five and fourteen of the drovers. Its commander paid for five stones, a fact the mourning liturgy remembers with contempt rather than gratitude; the mourning houses paid for thirty more.

## The Two Annotations

Witnessed-Seal schools offer necessity of form, a mutinous escort, and tragedy without legal error. The dominant modern position, after the first book of Suhrab's *Ledger of Ledgers* entered the deaths as a debt and supplied a flood clause centuries too late, is the formula **lawful and wrong**.

The Sealed-Blood recension places one sentence beside Clause the Fourth: *The law was the knife. The flood only carried it.*

On the empty fourth space, the Witnessed-Seal annotation says that a road instrument needed the three seals of the Hand-Sealed and no other. The Sealed-Blood annotation says that the only hand entitled to fill the space was travelling under the instrument.
