# The Velescar Correspondence

Player-readable documents for [[Elara Companion Campaign]]. All four items are authenticated through [[Claims-Factor Marco Bellandi]] and quoted whole in play — never paraphrased by another character, and never summarized in dialogue before the player has read them.

- **The Restoration Offer** — read in the records room during [[The Patron's Cabinet]].
- **The Second Letter** — arrives by ordinary mail in early Act III. Output: `vardek_second_letter_read`.
- **The Household Instructions** — four excerpts from the packet, quoted during the hearing movement of [[No Lady of Velescar]].

---

## The Restoration Offer

*Ostrian laid paper, deckled, unwatermarked. Vardek's own hand throughout, including the clause.*

> My dear Elara,
>
> I have kept this paper four years for you. You told me once that Ostrian laid is the only stock that takes a fair hand without flattering it, and I have never since been able to write to anyone else on it without feeling I was spending something of yours.
>
> I will not insult you with an account of the unpleasantness. You were there; I was reading letters from men I had trusted with my name and my money, and by the time I understood what they had arranged in Virelia you had already been given to the courts by people whose wages I was paying. I have dismissed them. It is far too little and four years too late, and I have not written until I could offer something better than an apology.
>
> Here is what I can do, and I ask you to read it as a schedule rather than a kindness.
>
> The title is not extinguished. It is suspended, and suspension is a thing courts undo when a house can show it will not fail again. I can show it. I can settle the riding debt and the grain arrears entire, recover the landing from my own creditors, and place a private reserve in your hands sufficient to keep a reduced house standing without your ever again taking a commission from anyone — including me. The Medori liabilities attaching to your person I will purchase and close. You would leave the Blackjack rolls this spring.
>
> There is a public account in this, and I will not pretend otherwise. It says that a young woman inherited a ruined estate and was misled by factors who traded on my name and her need, and that she has paid for it more heavily than either of us thought a court would ask. It is not a generous account. It is merely the true one arranged in the order that serves you.
>
> I would add one instrument, and it is the part I expect you to argue with. The house cannot be restored while sixty-three households hold open claims against it, and those claims will not resolve themselves through a war and a Fiorenzian clerk's postbag. **I ask you to accept the authority to settle them — to hear, compound, and close the household claims in the name of the house, at your discretion, funded from the reserve.** No one alive knows those families as you do. No one else can do it without cheating them, and I have watched enough men in my own service to know how it is done. Do it yourself, with your own hand, and it will be done decently.
>
> You were the bravest person at my table for six years and the only one who ever argued with me about anything that mattered. I do not think that woman was invented by a title, and I do not think she should spend her thirties carrying other men's arrangements up and down a frontier for a bank.
>
> There is no term on this. Write when you can.
>
> — Marek

*Design note: the letter must be persuasive on first read; a player who skims should think it kind. The bolded clause is the whole instrument — see [[Lord Marek Vardek#Why He Pays to Restore Her]]. He never names the crime, never threatens, and never lies about a fact Elara can check.*

## The Second Letter

*Ordinary courier paper. Early Act III, through the ordinary mail, unsealed by Bellandi in the usual way.*

> Elara,
>
> A short one. I am told the winter went badly and that you went through it usefully, which I never doubted.
>
> I hear you have taken up hosting again — that there are blue cups now, and that the quartermaster permits them on the grounds that they hold soup. I hear the porter Feld was cleared and has said so publicly rather more often than a modest man would. I hear there is a board in the yard with everyone's name on it and that you have stopped putting yours at the top of it. I hear you raced a Blackjack across a bridge for a wager you lost with great style.
>
> None of this is intelligence. Half your yard writes letters and the other half talks to people who do. I mention it only so you understand that I have been paying the kind of attention I have always paid you, and that I am glad of what I hear.
>
> My earlier offer stands as written. It stands until the vault under the Quays is opened, and after that it is a matter for whoever is holding the paper.
>
> — M.

*Design note: no threat anywhere in it. The menace is entirely the demonstrated knowledge and the closing deadline. Every named detail must be something the player has actually done or seen by that point in the campaign; substitute equivalents from the actual playthrough state where available.*

## The Household Instructions

*Four of the authenticated instructions from the pre-siege packet. Estate-roll numbers as recorded; the households' own words as taken down by whichever hand each could reach.*

### Roll 14 — the Ferrant holding

> We do not want the commons and we do not want a share of the landing. We want the ten acres above the mill race that we have worked since my grandfather, in fee tail to us and to ours, and we want it written in a way that no steward can read backwards. We have had eleven years of the house's protection and it has cost us a third of everything we grew. Give us the field.

*(Fee tail is not what they want and would restrict the inheritance they are trying to secure. Nobody in the packet corrects them. Any settlement drafted from their instruction must either give them what they asked for or what they meant, and the difference is a real decision.)*

### Roll 31 — Hanne Oster, for the Oster household

> Keep her money.
>
> I am told she is paying restitution and that some of it may come to us, and I am told I should be pleased. My husband dug for her. He came home grey and he stayed grey and he is grey now, sitting across from me while I say this to a clerk who will write it prettier than I said it. She fed him well. She gave him a coat. He tells me about the coat.
>
> If the court makes her pay, let it go to Roll 14 or Roll 52 or the burial priests or the river, and if it must come here then send it as the court's money and not as hers, and do not put her name on the paper. I will not have her generous at me twice.

### Roll 52 — the Merrow household

> There were cups. Green glass, eight of them, and a jug with a chipped foot, from the lady's mother's table, and they were in the small pantry when we were moved out and they are not there now. We do not say she took them. We say they were there.
>
> We want the cups, the jug, and the two ledgers of the dairy accounts in my mother's hand, and we do not want money instead of them. We will take our chance with the field like everyone else.

*(The cups were blue and there were six. The jug is correct. Elara knows all three facts, and correcting a grieving household about the colour of its own remembered glass is the smallest possible test of what she has learned. The cups exist and can be recovered.)*

### Roll 9 — Old Piet Sarn, tenant, for himself and four others

> I want the house back.
>
> I know what she did and I am not soft in the head about it, and I know the men in the villages who say good riddance to the pair of them. Let them say it. I was born under this roof's law and I know what the law is and who to shout at when the drains fail, and I do not know any of that about a commons committee in a district I have never voted in, nor about whatever gentleman buys the landing when it is sold off in pieces.
>
> She was a fool and a bad steward and she never once let the reeve take a widow's beasts, and the man over the hill does, every winter, lawfully. Tell her the roof of the chapel is going. Tell her to come home and be sorry here where it is useful.

*(Roll 9's instruction is authentic and opposes the morally resolved outcome. Relinquishment disappoints a real person who has thought about it. No scene may explain Piet's reasoning away as false consciousness, and no delegate speaks for him.)*
