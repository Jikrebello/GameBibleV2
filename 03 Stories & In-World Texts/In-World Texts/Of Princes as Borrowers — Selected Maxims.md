# Of Princes as Borrowers — Selected Maxims

*Scholar's apparatus: the desk-book of Taddeo Vetti (1289–1346 AR), a Sermoran-born factor of the Medori who kept the Bank's foreign desks at Aurelium, Saronika and Qabir for nearly thirty years. He wrote it for the counting-floor, a few lines at a time from about 1318, as a factor's guide to reading a sovereign before lending to one, and his own heading for it was "Of Princes as Borrowers". His last desk failed in the defaults of the Reform Wars, and in 1336 the Bank sold him into Fiorenzia's Debtors' Corps, where he went on adding to the book in the grey coat of a walking ledger. In 1345 he sold extracts from the Chancery registers to a Sermoran printer. In the spring of 1346 he was found drowned in the river dike he had been set to mend. The printer published the desk-book the next year under the title it has kept, with a dedication to Lauro Medori that Vetti did not write. Banned by the Sanctum's Index and Veridun's Codex alike; condemned by Covenant jurists (whose marginalia, where recovered, are admiring); found in the private shelves of every ruler on the board, each of whom wishes to know how the Bank reads him. The original is said to be in the Medori vault, filed with Vetti's debt-paper. Of course it is.*

---

> **On the two churches.** A prince who quarrels with one church will have his banners blessed by the other for nothing, and is a fair risk. A prince who quarrels with both has discovered how much they agree. Call the loan.

> **On relics.** Count a relic in the treasury as collateral and a saint in heaven as goodwill. Ask how often the relic is shown: a wonder shown daily becomes furniture, and a people who are done wondering begin counting.

> **On mercenaries.** The prince who holds his state by hired swords holds a lease, and has not read it. The company that saves him in autumn prices him in spring. Lend to the company.

> **On debt.** The Bank forgives no sins; it collects them. A prince may safely owe us any sum that would embarrass us to lose. Let him owe less and he is a client. Let him owe vastly more and he is a partner, and partners attend the meetings.

> **On fear and love.** Princes ask whether it is better to be feared than loved. Ask instead whether he is solvent. Fear rusts, love strays; a paid garrison in a full granary loves and fears its prince precisely enough, every day, at a known price, and we know the price.

> **On the grey coat.** A man who has been sold learns exactly what his master's ledger is worth. The master never learns what the man's is. Lend as if every clerk had read your books, because the ones who carry them have.

> **On contracts.** Sign nothing an Ahl-e-Ahd witness smiled at. Their law is honest the way a well is deep.

> **On heresy.** Lend nothing for the persecution of a heresy that empties the borrower's rival's collection plate; he will thank you for the refusal within the year. Doctrine is the one commodity that grows scarcer when hoarded and dearer when burned.

> **On the appearance of piety.** Men judge by the eye, and few stand close to the altar. A prince need not kindle. He need only glow. Do not mistake the glow for security, and do not tell him you can see the difference.

> **On fortune.** Fortune is a river in spring flood: divinity when she drowns the debtor's enemy, engineering the rest of the year. Ask to see the dikes in summer and let the priests explain the flood.

> **On the dead.** Take nothing in pledge that can be neither sold nor handed back. The dead are such a pledge, and one day a city will come for them with the living behind it. *(In a different ink, and later than the rest.)*

> **On the last account.** All princes owe one fare that cannot be refinanced, delayed by counsel, or paid by an heir. The Boatman audits everyone. Price every sovereign as though that ledger were already open — not for virtue's sake, but because it is.

*(Sanctum censor's note in the Index entry, hand unknown: "The wickedness of this book is that it is never wrong about anything small.")*
